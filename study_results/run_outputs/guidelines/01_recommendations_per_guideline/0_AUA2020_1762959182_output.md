Based on the AUA Update Series guideline, the following table outlines the explicit treatment recommendations for early-stage squamous cell carcinoma of the penis.

| Aspect of Care | Recommendation |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Diagnosis and Staging** | **Definitive diagnosis must be achieved through tissue sampling/excision.** A thorough physical examination is required to assess lesion size, location, and involvement of the corporal body or urethra. |
| **Diagnosis and Staging** | **Inguinal lymph nodes must be palpated for lymphadenopathy.** Imaging (e.g., CT, MRI, Doppler US) is often required to determine the extent of tumor invasion and assess lymph nodes, as physical examination alone can result in false-negative findings. |
| **Diagnosis and Staging** | **Computerized tomography (CT) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis is typically performed to evaluate for metastatic spread.** The scan should extend through the mid-thigh to ensure the groin is included in the interpretation. |
| **Primary Treatment (General Principles)** | For **early stage (Tis, Ta, T1), low-grade (G1, G2), low volume disease with favorable histopathology**, organ-preserving techniques should be considered. |
| **Primary Treatment (General Principles)** | The goal of surgical treatment should be **R0 surgical excision (negative margins).** A 2 cm surgical margin is no longer considered applicable; a 5 mm margin for G1/G2 tumors and a 10 mm margin for G3 tumors is suggested based on histological spread. |
| **Primary Treatment (Topical Therapy)** | Topical treatments (e.g., **5-fluorouracil, imiquimod**), cryotherapy, and photodynamic therapy can be considered as **first-line options for non-invasive and early-stage penile malignancies (carcinoma in situ, Ta, T1).** |
| **Primary Treatment (Topical Therapy)** | **5-fluorouracil (5-FU)** is widely accepted for **early-stage penile condylomas and carcinoma in situ (Tis).** |
| **Primary Treatment (Topical Therapy)** | Topical therapy is **ineffective for more advanced stages** of penile cancer and should only be prescribed after an early and accurate diagnosis is established. |
| **Primary Treatment (Laser Therapy)** | Laser therapy (e.g., **CO2, YAG**) can be used as monotherapy or with local excision for **early-stage (Tis, Ta, T1) non-bulky tumors.** |
| **Primary Treatment (Laser Therapy)** | A surgical margin of **5 mm is considered oncologically safe** for laser therapy. |
| **Primary Treatment (Surgical - Organ Preserving)** | **Circumcision** is indicated and can be curative for **low-grade, low-stage (Tis, Ta) preputial disease** in uncircumcised men. For tumors near the coronal sulcus, the margin must be extended. |
| **Primary Treatment (Surgical - Organ Preserving)** | **Mohs micrographic surgery** can be considered for disease with **favorable histopathology** when the lesion is located on the **distal penis or glans.** |
| **Primary Treatment (Surgical - Organ Preserving)** | **Glansectomy (partial or total) and glans resurfacing** are options for tumors located on the **glans penis.** |
| **Primary Treatment (Surgical - Aggressive)** | **Partial or total penectomy** are acceptable options for patients with **higher stage tumors, unfavorable histopathology, or recurrent disease,** or for patients who would not be suitable surgical candidates in the event of recurrence. |
| **Treatment of Recurrent Disease** | For a **recurrent lesion at a new site**, treatment options include topical therapy, laser therapy, surgical excision, Mohs surgery, and glans-specific procedures (glans resurfacing, glansectomy) if applicable. |
| **Treatment of Recurrent Disease** | For a **recurrent lesion at the same site**, options include laser therapy and surgical excision. For high-risk recurrent lesions or if follow-up is unreliable, **partial or radical penectomy** is reserved. |
| **Radiation Therapy** | Radiation therapy may be considered for patients with **T1-T2 disease who are unfit for surgery** and are willing to accept the associated late-onset side effects. Surgery remains the preferred approach due to higher success rates. |
| **Lymph Node Management** | Following circumcision, patients with **moderately or poorly differentiated tumors (grades 2 and 3) will require inguinal node dissection.** |
| **Follow-up Care** | **Close postoperative follow-up is crucial** after all organ-sparing treatments, particularly circumcision. |
| **Follow-up Care** | After **laser therapy**, close follow-up is recommended, especially during the **first 6 months** when the majority of local recurrences develop. |
| **Follow-up Care** | Following any penile-preserving technique, **close follow-up for at least 5 years is required** to detect and re-treat potential recurrences. |
| **Follow-up Care** | **Post-laser therapy follow-up schedules** vary by laser type: <br> • **CO2 Laser:** After 2 months, then every 3 months for 3 years, then biannually. <br> • **Nd:YAG Laser:** Every 3 months for 2 years, then biannually. |
