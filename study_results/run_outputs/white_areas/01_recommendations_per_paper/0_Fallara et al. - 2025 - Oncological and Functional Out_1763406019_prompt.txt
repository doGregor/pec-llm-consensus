Abstract

Context: Penile shaft sparing (PSS) surgery for localised penile cancer (PeCa) aims to balance oncological and functional outcomes.
Objective: To summarise the published evidence on different PSS approaches.
Evidence acquisition: We performed a systematic review adhering to Preferred Reporting
Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses guidelines. The systematic search was
performed on PubMed, EMBASE, and Scopus databases up to February 14, 2023. The
inclusion criteria encompassed retrospective or prospective studies including patients
�18 yr of age with localised PeCa treated with different PSS approaches, classified as
laser ablation, circumcision, wide local excision, glansectomy with or without split skin
graft, glans resurfacing, and mixed technique excision. The risk of bias was assessed
using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale. A quantitative synthesis was not performed due to
anticipated data heterogeneity and a lack of comparative studies.
Evidence synthesis: Out of 4343 articles identified, 47 met our inclusion criteria, including 10 847 patients. The year of publication ranged between 1983 and 2021. Nine studies
were prospective case series, while the remaining studies were retrospective. No comparative studies were identified. Most of the cases included in these studies were Ta
and T1. The oncological outcomes were good for all the approaches, with cancerspecific mortality ranging between 0% and 18%. Sexual and cosmetic outcomes, despite
being under-reported, were good for all the approaches, with almost all patients being
satisfied with their quality of life after surgery. The Newcastle and Ottawa scale revealed
a high or severely high risk of bias in all the included studies.
Conclusions: PSS approaches were safe and had good functional outcomes, considering
however the overall low quality of the studies on this issue.

1. Introduction

Penile cancer (PeCa) accounts for a minority of cancer cases
in developed countries but represents a frequent cancer in
some areas such as Brazil, India, and Sub-Saharan Africa

[1]. PeCa is associated with poor oncological outcomes,
especially in the case of metastatic disease [1]. As well,
the functional and psychological outcomes after PeCa diagnosis and treatment are poor [1].
A big effort has been made over the past decades to
improve both oncological and psychofunctional outcomes
of patients with PeCa treated with surgery, especially with
the so-called penile shaft sparing (PSS), or organ-sparing,
approach [2]. This approach aims to remove the cancer
safely and preserve as much as possible the function and
cosmesis of the penis, while maintaining good oncological
outcomes and preserving quality of life [3].
The extent of the disease is a main driver of the choice of

the surgical treatment. Usually, indication for PSS is
restricted to PeCa stages pTis, pTa, and pT1, and includes
circumcision, wide local excision (WLE; ±split skin graft

[SSG]), laser ablation, glans resurfacing, and glansectomy
(±SSG) [4]. These techniques are all viable options, and there
are no evidence and no precise recommendations in international guidelines on PeCa management regarding which
one to choose in the different settings.
The aim of the current systematic review is to summarise all possible options for PSS in the case of localised
PeCa, focusing on oncological and functional outcomes.

2. Evidence acquisition

2.1. Search strategy

We conducted a systematic review of all published studies
that deal with PSS for localised PeCa. The review was conducted based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [5,6].
The study was registered in the International Prospective
Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO ID:
CRD42023388309).
The PubMed, EMBASE, and Scopus databases were
searched for studies indexed up to February 14, 2023. Combinations of the following keywords were used: penile cancer, circumcision, glansectomy, laser, organ sparing, penile
sparing, resurfacing, skin graft, and wide local excision.
The titles and abstracts of the manuscripts obtained from
the search were used to screen for initial study inclusion.
A full-text review was performed when the abstract was
not sufficient to determine study inclusion. Reference lists
of the included studies were hand searched for completeness, and if a study was suitable for inclusion, it was
included in our systematic review. Given the anticipated


absence of randomised control trials, retrospective or
prospective case series and cohort studies were included
in the current systematic review. Review articles, commentaries, editorials, and articles that did not undergo a peer
review were excluded. Two authors completed the study
selection independently. Potential disagreements were
resolved by consensus among all coauthors.

2.2. Study inclusion

All studies including males, aged �18 yr old, with localised
PeCa (ie, stages Tis, Ta, and T1), treated with PSS (ie, circumcision, WLE ± SSG, laser ablation [carbon dioxide {CO 2 }, neodymium:yttrium aluminium garnet {Nd:YAG}, or others],
glans resurfacing, and glansectomy [±SSG]) were included
in the current systematic review. Studies with incomplete
baseline data regarding the stage and grading of PeCa were
excluded. We anticipated that some of the included studies
may present aggregated data on surgical techniques and
baseline characteristics (ie, regarding staging) but were
included in the systematic review for completeness. Studies
on brachytherapy, radiotherapy, or non-PSS techniques
were excluded.

2.3. Data extraction

Data were collected according to a proforma that included
the year of publication, authors’ names, study design, number of included individuals, baseline age and comorbidities/
performance status, type of PSS, clinical stage and grade of
PeCa, histological features, rates and type of complications,
overall survival, cancer-specific survival, local recurrencefree survival, distant progression-free survival, sexual satisfaction and function (if possible, according to validated
questionnaires or not), urinary satisfaction (if possible,
according to validated questionnaires or not), and overall
quality of life. All time-dependent outcomes were collected
at the last follow-up available.

2.4. Data analysis

The risk of bias (RoB) was determined using the Newcastle
and Ottawa scale for nonrandomised studies independently
by three authors [7,8]. Only a qualitative synthesis of the
included studies was possible given the a priori expected
high inconsistency of data and the lack of comparative studies that hindered any kind of quantitative meta-analysis.

our inclusion criteria, involving 10 847 patients (Fig. 1,

In total, 14 studies were conducted on the treatment of
localised PeCa involving glansectomy with or without SSG

[18,20,24,33–43]. Of these, two studies were prospective,
while the remaining ones were retrospective [18,35]. The
overall population consisted of 1382 patients. The details
are shown in Table 4.
The mean age at treatment ranged between 60 [34] and
71 [38] yr. Most of the cases were T1 (ranging from 19% to
60%) [20,36] or T2 (ranging from 4% to 61%) [20,43]. In one
study, all included patients treated with glansectomy had
Tis disease [24]. One study reported intraoperative complications in a single case, but it did not specify the type of
complication that occurred [43]. Complications after surgery were documented in up to 29% of cases [43]. Among
the complications reported, partial graft loss, 12% [35],
and graft failure, 3%, were most frequent [40]. Five studies
lacked information on complications [18,20,24,39,41].
Positive margin rates ranged between 0% [35] and 17%

[42], while the rate of local recurrence ranged between 0%

[35,38] and 18% [43], despite variations in the follow-up
duration among the included studies, ranging from 10

[38] to 43 [20] mo. Additionally, the disease progression
rate ranged from 0% [24,33,37,38] to 11% [40], with three
studies not reporting information on it [20,41,43]. Overall
mortality rates ranged between 0% [24,33,36–38] and 21%

[41], and cancer-specific mortality ranged between 0%

[24,33,36–38] and 18% [44]. Two studies provided no information about mortality [20,42].
Finally, functional outcomes were under-reported, with
only seven studies detailing urinary and sexual functions
after treatment [33–38,42]. Only two studies used validated
questionnaires [35,42]. Most of the studies reported a complete or almost complete recovery and preservation of sexual function.

3.6. Mixed

Eight studies [49–56] involving 7423 patients reported data
on the use of mixed techniques for the treatment of localised PeCa. The most frequent techniques used were laser
surgery, WLE with or without circumcision, and glansectomy with or without glans resurfacing, as reported in
Table 6. However, detailed data on patients’ characteristics
and follow-up were not provided separately for each surgical approach.
The mean age at treatment ranged between 61 [51,54]
and 63 [55] yr. Most of the cases were Tis (range 14–50%)

[50,52,56] or T1 (range 30–90%) [53,56]. One case series
reported PSS use in T3 cases (12%) [52].
Only two out of eight studies reported data on surgical
outcomes [16,54]. The study by Philippou et al [51] reported
that only 2% of the cases experienced postoperative complications, while no intraoperative complications were
reported.
Positive surgical margin rates ranged between 6% [54]
and 21% [50], while local recurrence rates ranged between
6% [54] and 46% [52], although data were not provided separately according to different techniques. Only four studies
reported data on disease progression, which ranged
between 0% [54] and 9% [55]. Moreover, three out of eight
studies reported data on overall mortality and cancerspecific mortality, ranging between 0% [54,55] and 6% [51]
and between 0% [54,55] and 4% [51], respectively.
Finally, only the study of Feldman and McDougal [50]
provided data on postoperative functional outcomes reporting that 100% of the patients maintained satisfactory sexual
function after WLE with or without circumcision (n = 36) or
partial glansectomy (n = 12).

3.7. Discussion

In this systematic review, we summarised the current published evidence on PSS for localised PeCa. We identified 47

60 E U R O P E A N U R O L O G Y F O C U S 1 1 ( 2 0 2 5 ) 4 6 – 6 1


studies, enrolling >10 000 patients. There were very few
prospective studies, and most of the studies were retrospective single or multicentre case series. We categorised PSS
into five main approaches, that is, WLE, circumcision, laser
ablation, glansectomy ± SSG, and glans resurfacing. For each
of these approaches, we summarised the baseline patient
characteristics and the perioperative, functional, and oncological outcomes.
The assessment of the quality of the included study
revealed that the RoB was high, so definitive recommendations cannot be drawn from our systematic review and all
the reported data should be read considering this scientific
weakness. A previous review on the topic drew similar conclusions on the low quality of evidence on PSS, and unfortunately there is no sign of improvement for this issue over
time [2,3,57].
Mostly, PSS approaches were used in the case of Tis or T1
disease. Interestingly, laser ablation was also used in two
cases of recurrence of a previous T3 disease [23]; circumcision and WLE were used in some T2 cases [20,21,32]. Unfortunately, the oncological outcomes of these cases were not
presented separately from those of the overall cohort, so
we were not able to comment on the use of PSS in locally
advanced disease.
Regarding perioperative outcomes, most of the studies
lack information on intraoperative outcomes, while those
reporting such information showed a very low rate of intraoperative complications. WLE, laser ablation, and circumcision showed a very low rate of postoperative complications;
glans resurfacing and glansectomy ± SSG had a higher rate
of complications, even up to 25% of cases in some case series

[38]. These results should be read in light of the retrospective nature of most of the included studies and the difficulty
in retrieving data on complications [58]. In addition, a standardised classification of postoperative complications, such
as Dindo et al’s [59] classification, was not adopted
uniformly.
Oncological outcomes showed that PSS approaches are
reasonably safe procedures, given the very low rate of local
recurrence, good disease progression, and low cancerspecific mortality rates. However, the lack of a standardised
follow-up protocol, the different definitions of disease progression, and the different lengths of follow-up, together
with the retrospective nature of most of the included studies, hindered final conclusions on this issue.
Unfortunately, when it comes to functional outcomes,
which is one of the main drivers in the decision-making
toward a PSS approach for the management of localised
PeCa, it is difficult to draw definitive conclusions due to
the high inconsistency regarding the use of validated questionnaires, the proportion of patients with reported functional outcomes in each study, and the retrospective
nature of the included studies.

4. Conclusions

In summary, this systematic review may serve as a valuable
complement to the existing guidelines, providing an overview of the current literature. However, due to the relatively poor quality of the available data and the lack of
comparative studies, caution should be used in drawing
conclusions regarding the safety and efficacy of PSS for loca

lised PeCa. This review underscores the need for standardised methodologies and comprehensive reporting in future
studies on PSS.
