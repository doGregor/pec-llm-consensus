**ABSTRACT**
Compared with Europe and America, adoption of human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination into national
immunization programs across the Asia-Pacific (AP) region has remained low. Moreover, HPV burden in
this region has not been reviewed comprehensively. Therefore, this systematic literature review (SLR)
aimed to summarize the clinical and economic burden of HPV and HPV-related diseases in select AP areas.
An SLR was conducted January 2000-February 2022 using MEDLINE/Embase. Observational studies
reporting incidence, prevalence, costs, or healthcare resource utilization (HCRU) of HPV and HPVrelated disease among adults (≥18 years) from select AP areas were included. A total of 254 publications
were included. Reported incidence per 100,000 person-years was 15.4–252.0 for cervical cancer ( *n* = 5
publications), 0.2–55.5 for head and neck cancer ( *n* = 7 publications), and 0.2–13.7 for anal cancer ( *n* = 4
publications). Prevalence rates were 9.1%-100% for cervical cancer ( *n* = 40 publications), 0.0%-95.6% for
head and neck cancer ( *n* = 48 publications), 0.0%-100% for anal cancer ( *n* = 4 publications), 36.0%-79.6%
for penile cancer ( *n* = 4 publications), and 44.0%-82.0% for vaginal/vulvar cancer ( *n* = 3 publications). Few
studies reported on costs or HCRU, and high data variability was observed. Despite data variability, the
high burden of HPV and HPV-related diseases makes clear the need for effective HPV screening, greater
education, and reductions in vaccine hesitancy, particularly among lower- and middle-income areas.
Improved data collection measures should be considered in data-scarce areas to better inform policy
decision-making and improve monitoring of the impact of HPV vaccination.

**Introduction**

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is among the most common
sexually transmitted infections globally. [1 ] There are more than
200 types of HPV, including low-risk types that are associated
with genital warts and recurrent respiratory papillomatosis
and high-risk oncogenic types that account for most cases of
cervical cancer, as well as head and neck, anal, penile, vaginal,
and vulvar cancers. [1 ] Although HPV genotype distribution in
cervical cancer/precancer and genital warts has been widely
studied, [2–6 ] there is a need for more literature on genotype
distribution in other HPV-related cancers.

The incidence and mortality of HPV-related diseases such
as cervical cancer are higher in transitioning countries (i.e.,
those classified as low/medium on the Human Development
Index), [7 ] primarily attributable to a relative lack of high-quality
screening and timely access to cancer surgery, radiation ther-­
apy, and other cancer management services in those
countries. [8][,][9 ] In 2020, the World Health Organization launched
a global initiative to eliminate cervical cancer, focusing on the
need for high levels of HPV vaccination, screening, and treat-­
ment, with 90-70-90 targets to be met by the year 2030: 90% of


girls fully vaccinated with the HPV vaccine by the age of 15
years, 70% of women screened by the age of 35 years and again
at 45 years, and 90% of women diagnosed with cervical disease
receiving treatment (for both precancer and invasive cancer). [9 ]

Declines in the incidence and prevalence of cervical cancer
have been observed since the introduction of HPV vaccinations
for girls in many countries, [10 ] but the burden of HPV-related
disease in males remains high. Moreover, high-risk or persistent
HPV infection is associated with recurrence of cervical intrae-­
pithelial precancerous lesions. [11–13 ] HPV vaccination could be
used for primary prevention of other HPV-related cancers that
are too rare for population-based screening, those where highrisk populations have not yet been identified, or those for which
no screening tests are available. [8][,][9]

Compared with Europe and America, adoption of HPV
vaccination into country national immunization programs
across the Asia-Pacific (AP) region has remained low, [14 ] due
to vaccine hesitancy, poor diagnosis and treatment, lack of
cancer prevention, awareness, high cost, and difficulties in
vaccine implementation. [15 ] While prior publications have
assessed HPV burden in the AP region, these publications

**Methods**

***Search strategy and data sources***

An SLR was conducted on February 25, 2022, to identify
publications regarding HPV and HPV-related diseases in
select AP areas. Journal articles and conference abstracts pub-­
lished from January 1, 2000, to February 25, 2022, in
MEDLINE and Embase were searched via ProQuest
(Supplemental Table 1).

***Study selection and data extraction***

Observational studies reporting the outcomes of interest from
select areas in the South-East Asia and Western Pacific regions
were included (Table 1). Hong Kong was included, but mainland
China, India, Australia, and New Zealand were excluded. The
outcomes of interest were the incidence or prevalence of HPVrelated disease, the distribution of 9 HPV genotypes (HPV6,
HPV11, HPV16, HPV18, HPV31, HPV33, HPV45, HPV52,
HPV58) in the population, and costs and healthcare resource
utilization (HCRU) associated with HPV-related disease.

Two reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of identified

citations and selected full-text articles for potential inclusion.

Reviewers extracted relevant data from each included publica-­
tion; data were reconciled to ensure accuracy. Data on study
characteristics and methodology, patient characteristics, the
incidence and/or prevalence of HPV-related diseases, HPV
genotype distribution (i.e., number of patients within the
study sample with specific HPV genotypes), costs, and
HCRU were extracted into a database. The numerator and
denominator (sample size) were extracted for binary outcome

and change-from-baseline values, as well as mean differences
(with their respective standard error, standard deviation, or
confidence interval) between cohorts, if reported. Figures and/
or tables were developed to synthesize individual study data
where applicable.

Data from included publication were descriptively reported to
summarize results. Per the study protocol, no meta-regression or
sensitivity analyses were performed for this literature review. This

reporting. [16]

**Discussion**

This systematic review identified literature on the clinical and
economic burden of HPV-related disease across the SouthEast Asia and Western Pacific regions. Among 254 included
publications, the incidences of HPV and HPV-related diseases
were reported in 15 publications. Across cancer subtypes,
incidence rates varied by country, cohorts, and time periods,
with variable rates. The highest reported incidence rate
(252.00 per 100,000 PYs) was reported for cervical cancer
among women in Taiwan aged 30 to 65 years with any type
of HPV (i.e., carcinogenic types [HPV16, HPV18, HPV31,
HPV33, HPV35, HPV39, HPV45, HPV51, HPV52, HPV56,
HPV58, and HPV59], possible or probable carcinogenic types

[HPV26, HPV53, HPV66, HPV67, HPV68, HPV70, HPV73,
and HPV82], and other HPV types). [18 ] Variability in reported
HPV-related cancer incidence rates is attributable to the het-­
erogeneity of study cohorts and time periods. Differences in
study cohorts reflected the causal relationship between HPV
and cancer incidence. For example, a study of 10,123 women
screened for cervical cancer in Taiwan reported an overall
cervical cancer incidence rate of 46.4 per 100,000 PYs; how-­
ever, when the cohort was grouped by HPV status at study
entry, cervical cancer incidence rates were 16 times greater
among HPV-positive women (252 per 100,000 PY) compared
with HPV-negative women (15.7 per 100,000 PY). [18 ] Studies
also differed in reporting HPV-related disease incidence over
a single year versus a longer time period, which may have
contributed to the wide range in reported rates. Included
publications also reflected the difference in HPV-related can-­
cer incidence among men and women. In a 2014 study of
several head and neck cancer subtypes across countries and
gender, and two time periods (1983–1987 and 1998–2002),
incidence rates for oral cavity, oropharynx, and other head
and neck cancer were consistently greater among men in
Hong Kong, Japan, the Philippines, and Thailand compared
with women, though incidence rates had dropped in later time
periods, [31 ] potentially due to improvements in practices that
are associated with head and neck cancer (e.g., reduced
tobacco use). Reduced incidence rates in some publications
could also potentially reflect increased screening for both HPV
and HPV-related diseases in more recent years, thereby high-­
lighting the importance of timely HPV detection. Recent pub-­
lications have reported the potential benefits of HPV
vaccination for patients with high-grade or precancerous cer-­
vical lesions. [12][,][49][,][50]


The majority of included publications ( *n* = 238) reported
prevalence rates or the proportion of patients with HPV
among cancer subtypes. As with incidence rates, prevalence
rates and proportions varied by country, cancer types, and
time periods. Common HPV genotypes in included study
cohorts were HPV16, HPV18, HPV45, and HPV52, which
were almost every cancer subtype, all of which are aligned
with current evidence on high-risk HPV subtypes. [51]

Despite the variability of evidence across included publica-­
tions, HPV and HPV-related incidence and prevalence rates in
the AP areas covered in this review were, in some cases, greater
than those reported in other World Health Organization
regions and in particular North America and Europe. The
US National Cancer Institute Surveillance, Epidemiology,
and End Results Program estimated the US cervical cancer
incidence at 7.6 per 100,000 PYs in 2024, [52 ] less than half of
that reported in these publications. Similarly, head and neck
cancer incidence rates were reported to be as high as 55.45 per
100,000 PY [30 ] among included publications, well above the
global estimates of ~ 1.1 to 4.0 per 100,000 PY, according to
the World Health Organization GLOBOCAN database. [53 ]

Differences in cultural and social practices, as well as lifestyle
factors such as alcohol and tobacco use, across regions could
contribute to the high incidence in lower-middle income
countries. [54]

Evidence on total, direct, and indirect costs was limited and
reported only from Japan, the Philippines, and South Korea.
Cost estimates ranged across countries, cancer subtypes, and
time periods, with higher reported costs in recent years. HCRU
data on HPV patients in select AP areas were also limited, with
only 2 publications reporting data on patients with genital
warts in the Philippines and cervical cancer in Japan. By con-­
trast, publications on the economic burden associated with
HPV-related disease are more plentiful in the US and
Europe. [55][,][56 ] While prior systematic publications have focused
on the health economic evaluation of HPV vaccines, [57][,][58 ] there
is an evidence gap in the economic impact of the disease on
patients.

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review to
summarize HPV-related disease burden specifically in the
AP region. A non-systematic review article on the burden of
HPV infections and HPV-related diseases was published in
2008, reporting incidence, prevalence, and mortality rates. [59 ]

A 2017 publication reported the global burden of cancer
attributable to HPV by site, country, and HPV type. [8 ]

However, this publication used data from GLOBOCAN
2012 and reported publications up to 2016. A more recent
publication by the same author reported the global burden
of cancer attributable to infections in 2018. [60 ] However, the
publication reported only incidence rates and did not pro-­
vide country-specific rates in some cases. Our review there-­
fore adds to the currently available literature by providing
more recent publications on the clinical and economic out-­
comes of HPV-related disease in the AP region. The benefits
of including HPV vaccination in national immunization
programs has been explored previously, with some publica-­
tions estimating that HPV immunization could control
nearly 70% of all cervical cancers [61 ] and that high HPV
vaccine coverage could reduce cervical cancer incidence

rates significantly by the end of the century. [62 ]

Understanding the HPV disease burden in the AP region
therefore provides greater context for increasing HPV vac-­
cination and coverage to help reduce the incidence and
prevalence of HPV-related diseases.

Although this review provides a comprehensive overview
of HPV-related disease burden among the select AP areas,
several limitations must be noted. Methodological differ-­
ences between studies (e.g., population studied, detection
methods, outcome definitions) may have contributed to the
high data variability observed in the SLR. Sample sizes were
not always reported; when reported, they varied widely
across studies. The results may also be biased through selec-­
tive reporting since the studies had observational cohorts.
Most of the data were from single-institution studies and
had small sample sizes, particularly the studies from
Southeast Asia. This suggests that better data systems may
be required to capture national disease incidence in these
countries. In addition, most of the data captured in this SLR
precede the HPV vaccination era. It is important for coun-­
tries to continue monitoring disease burden to understand
the impact of HPV vaccination. Finally, we focused on the
general population and did not thoroughly search the litera-­
ture related to people living with HIV. However, we know
the disease burden of HIV within the region is substantial, as
is the disease burden of HPV-related cancer and disease
among people living with HIV. The area could be explored
in future research. Nevertheless, despite the limitations
inherent to SLRs, the evidence gathered here may be an
important resource to understand the clinical outcomes of
HPV-related disease in the AP region. Moreover, this review
highlights the importance of HPV screening, not only for the
detection of HPV-related cancers but for the management of
patients, particularly those with precancerous or early-stage
lesions.

While not a specific aim of this literature review, future
research should also examine the humanistic burden of
HPV-related disease across countries in the AP region to
understand the impact of disease on patients. Moreover, the
impact of HPV on reproductive concerns for women, which
has been studied in prior systematic reviews, [63][,][64 ] as well as
male HPV infection, for which limited data has been pub-­
lished previously, [65][,][66 ] could also be explored further in the
AP region to understand differences compared with other
global regions.

**Conclusions**

This SLR describes the high burden of HPV and HPV-related
disease within the AP region. Data availability varies across the
region, with the most comprehensive data available in
Northeast Asia (Japan, South Korea, Taiwan); for Southeast
Asia, Thailand had the most reported data. Limited data were
available for HPV-related diseases other than cervical cancer;
data on costs and HCRU were also limited. Despite the varia-­
bility of data, the high burden of HPV and HPV-related dis-­
ease, particularly in comparison with the rest of the world,
makes clear the need for enhanced, timely HPV screening,
greater education on HPV transmission and HPV-related

among lower- and middle-income areas. Improved data col-­
lection measures should be considered in areas where data are
scarce to better inform policy decision-making and improve
monitoring of the impact of HPV vaccination.
