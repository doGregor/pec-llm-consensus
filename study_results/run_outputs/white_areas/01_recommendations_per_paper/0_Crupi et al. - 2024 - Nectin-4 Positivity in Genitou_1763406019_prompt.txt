Emanuele Crupi, MD [1,2] ; Tiago Costa de Padua, MD [1] ; Laura Marandino, MD [1] ; Giuseppe Fallara, MD [3] ; Filippo Pederzoli, MD [4] ;
Alessia Cimadamore, MD [5] ; Emanuele C. Goetz, MD [6] ; Antonio Cigliola, MD [1] ; Damiano A. Patan´e, MD [1] ; Chiara Mercinelli, MD [1] ;

[DOI https://doi.org/10.1200/PO-24-00470](https://doi.org/10.1200/PO-24-00470)


ABSTRACT

PURPOSE Aberrant expression of nectin-4 (N4) has been observed in several malignancies
emerging as new target for antibody-drug conjugates, especially in urothelial
carcinoma of the bladder (UBC). Limited data on N4 positivity in nonurothelial
genitourinary (GU) cancers are available. This systematic-review aimed to
investigate N4 positivity among GU malignancies.

METHODS A systematic literature review was performed on March 2023 using PubMed,
MEDLINE, and Embase databases according to Preferred Reporting Items for
Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses statement. Protocol was amended to
incorporate a new updated search on March 2024.

RESULTS Twenty-five studies evaluating N4 positivity in GU tumors were included, 14 on
UBC, three on upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC), six on histologic
subtypes (HS) and divergent histology of the bladder, one on papillary renal cell
carcinoma (pRCC), one in chromophobe RCC (chRCC), two on penile cancer, one
in prostate cancer (PCa). Among UBC, stratifying per stage N4 positivity was

–
higher in metastatic (weighted mean [WM], 90.8; range, 59.6-100) and in non
muscle-invasive (WM, 87.4; range, 86.7-88.3) than in muscle-invasive UC
(WM, 83.1; range, 68.2-100). The N4 positivity of UBC was higher than UTUC
(WM, 62.9; range, 44.4-65.7). Immunohistochemistry N4 positivity was reported to be lower in non-UC malignancies, including pRCC (WM, 44.1; range,
44.1-44.1), HS (WM, 63.5; range, 0-100), PCa (WM0; range, 0-0), chRCC (WM,
18.5; range, 18.5-18.5), and penile cancer (WM, 86.5; range, 61.4-98.3), compared with UBC overall (WM, 87.1; range, 59.6-100).

CONCLUSION Non-UC malignancies seem to have a lower N4 positivity rate than UC. N4
positivity in bladder cancer appears to vary according to stage and presence of
HS. The predictive and prognostic role of N4 must be further characterized in
larger and prospective studies.

INTRODUCTION

In past few years, targeted therapies, immunotherapy, and
antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) have revolutionized the
urothelial carcinoma (UC) treatment landscape. Despite
encouraging developments, several patients with advancedstage still experience poor responses, leading to unfavorable
outcomes. Identifying reliable biomarkers to guide patient
selection is crucial for improving clinical outcomes.

Several tissue-based biomarkers have been studied as

prognostic and predictive factors. [1][-][3] Nectin-4 (N4), a
transmembrane protein of the nectin family, plays a crucial


role in migration, cell adhesion, and proliferation. [1] Aberrant
expression of N4 has been observed in several malignancies,
including breast, ovarian, lung, and pancreatic cancers, with
prevalence ranging from 40% to 80%. [4][,][5] N4 immunohistochemistry (IHC) positivity has been reported in over 70%
of UC specimens, making it a potential target for ADCs
(Fig 1), contrary to what has been observed in normal
urothelial tissue. [5] In metastatic UC (mUC), prospective and
retrospective studies reported conflicting data regarding N4
positivity. [5][-][9]

However, a gap exists in understanding N4 expression’s
relevance in non-UC genitourinary (GU) cancers, such as

METHODS

Search Strategy

The study protocol was registered in the International
Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO;
Registration ID CRD42023403988). According to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and MetaAnalyses statement, we conducted a systematic literature
search using PubMed and MEDLINE databases on March 14,
2023, to identify studies reporting N4 positivity, histo-score
(H-score), and/or expression profiles in a specimen of patients with GU malignancies. Additional search of reference
lists and review articles was performed to ensure literature
saturation. After the initial screening, protocol was amended
to incorporate an updated search on March 1, 2024, using the
same search strategy, to identify any additional relevant
studies.

Search strategy and inclusion/exclusion criteria are detailed
in the protocol.

Study Quality Assessment

Risk of bias and quality assessment were performed using
ROBINS-I tool (Appendix Table A1) for interventional
nonrandomized trials and Joanna Briggs Institute criticalappraisal tools for retrospective case series [18][,][19] (Appendix

Data Collection, Variables, Outcome Definitions,
and Assessment

Data were collected using a proforma, including first author,
year of publication, study design, IHC assays, antibodies
used for IHC, and number of included patients. N4 positivity
and H-score were presented for each study and grouped
according to the histotype in UC, upper tract urothelial
carcinoma (UTUC), renal cell carcinoma (RCC), PCa, and
penile cancer. Regarding UC, considered settings were mUC,
non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), and muscleinvasive bladder cancer (MIBC), along with presence of
histologic subtypes (HS) and non-UC bladder cases. To
ensure transparency and reliability, authors involved in
selection and data extraction had no relevant conflicts of

interest.

H-score assigns a grade to staining intensity (0 5 absent, 1 5
weak, 2 5 moderate, 3 5 strong) and calculates the percentage of N4-positive cells (0%-100%). Scores are multiplied to yield the H-score, ranging from 0 to 300, and
categorized as negative (0-14), weak (15-99), moderate
(100-199), or strong (200-300). [5] Across studies, N4positivity assessments vary with different cutoffs, antibodies, or median values as thresholds. Depending on the
study, pathologists considered membrane/cytoplasm only or
membrane and cytoplasm stainings (Table 1).

Assessed outcomes included N4-positivity rates across
different histologies, stages, and sites, and H-scores between prospective and retrospective studies. We aimed to
identify N4-positivity patterns and clinical significance, and
address methodologic impacts. To explore N4 predictive and
prognostic role, outcomes included objective response rate
(ORR), recurrence-free survival (RFS), disease-free survival,
progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS).

For continuous variables reported as the median (range),
results were converted to mean 6 standard deviation (SD)
using a dedicated formula. [36] After obtaining the SD, 95% CIs
were computed. For continuous variables (ie, H-score), the

4 | © 2024 by American Society of Clinical Oncology


weighted mean (WM) was calculated by multiplying each
value by its weight (ie, number of patients), summing these
products, and then dividing by the sum of weights. Proportions were used for categorical variables. For categorical
variable (ie, N4-positivity rate), WM was calculated by
weighting each study’s proportion by its sample size and
then dividing the sum of weighted proportions by the total
sample size across all studies. For one-study subgroups,
study proportion was used directly as the WM. Data were
represented using the RStudio v.0.98 for R software v.3.0.2
with the Metafor package.

Given the high heterogeneity in study designs, H-score
techniques, N4-positivity rates, and reporting criteria, we
did not perform quantitative analyses. The protocol describes these aims and discontinued preplanned subgroup
analyses.

RESULTS

Literature Search and Study Selection

From the database search, 170 records were identified. After
removing duplicates (n 5 86), 25 studies were included in the
final review: one on PCa, one on papillary RCC (pRCC), one on
chromophobe RCC (chRCC), and 16 on UC, six on HS and
non-UC of the bladder (Appendix Fig A1). Study characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Among included studies, 2,930 patients with UC, 230 with
non-UC of the bladder, 85 with HS of the bladder, 297 with
pRCC, 224 with UTUC, 178 with penile cancer, 81 with PCa,
and 82 with chRCC were assessed. Most studies used IHC to

evaluate N4 positivity, using different antibodies and
quantifying results with the H-score.

Overall Results

N4-positivity rates in GU malignancies are summarized in
Figure 3. UC showed the highest positivity rate, ranging from
a mean of 83.1% (range, 68.2-100) in MIBC to 90.8% (range,
59.6-100) in mUC. Other rates were 62.9% (range, 44.465.7) for UTUC, 63.5% (range, 44.4-65.7) for HS, and 69.6%
for non-UC of the bladder (range, 35.5-85.8). N4 positivity in
non-UC tumors was 44.1% for pRCC, 18.5% for chRCC, and
86.5% for penile cancer (range, 61.4-98.3). The WM H-score
from prospective trials in UC was 281.6 (279.5-283.7), while
from retrospective studies was 109.6 (103.4-115.9; Fig 4;
Appendix Table A3).

Correlation between N4-positivity rate and treatment response or outcomes was not assessed because of high data
heterogeneity (Data Supplement, Table S1).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review evaluating the N4 positivity among different GU malignancies,
histotypes, and clinical stages.

However, several limitations related to the exploratory
nature of the review, the absence of comparative studies, the
heterogeneity in clinical characteristics of the included
patients, and the methodologies used to evaluate outcomes
need to be stated. These factors contribute to potential biases
and variability, making it challenging to compare findings
reliably. Therefore, our results highlight the need for validation through larger, prospective studies.

Despite already explored in several cancer types, N4 positivity was not universally found as predictive or prognostic in
GU malignancies.

Although it was not possible to make a direct comparison, our
exploratory findings show that UC seems to have a higher N4IHC positivity rate than non-UC GU malignancies. In bladder
cancer, the positivity rate seems to vary among different


stages and sites of disease (primary v metastasis). Conflicting
results regarding N4 positivity in the metastatic setting were
found. UTUC did not seem to have as high N4 positivity as UC
of the bladder, further suggesting potential differences in
tumor biology. Data on the UTUC subgroup derived from
EV201/301 trials are inconclusive regarding the putative
predictive role of N4 expression.

Among HS and non-UC of the bladder, SCC and nested
variants were found often N4-positive, while NE-negative.
In the UNITE study, for example, sarcomatoid and plasmacytoid demonstrated responses to EV, while NE and all
others pure HS showed no benefit. [37] Such findings require
validation but may lead to new therapeutic approaches in HS.

Among non-UC GU malignancies, PSCC appeared to present
an elevated N4 positivity, especially in HPV1 tumors, although a correlation with outcomes was generally not found.
On the contrary, Tekin et al [35] found that high expression of
N4 seems to be prognostic. In this study, the expression of
N4 was significantly elevated in high-risk HPV1 tumors
compared with negative ones. Similar findings among HPVdependent SCCs have been reported at other anatomic sites
(head and neck SCC). [38] In HPV1 tumors, E6/7-mediated
oncogenic activity or PI3K pathway alterations may upregulate N4 expression. [34] Without valid therapeutic options,
this orphan disease could be an ideal candidate to explore
anti-N4 therapies in trials.

Our review did not find a clear predictive and prognostic role
of N4 in non-UC malignancies. In UTUC, N4 positivity
correlated with shorter PFS only in high-risk disease. [27] In
retrospective mUC studies, elevated N4 positivity was suggested as potentially predictive, and low N4 positivity was
associated with shorter PFS. However, prospective trials of
EV did not specifically examine the potential of N4 positivity
as a predictive marker.

EV is approved regardless of N4 positivity. [12][,][16] Data from the
EV-101 study granted the assumption that N4 is widely
expressed in UC without performing a pretreatment assessment. In this trial, nearly all specimens were reported to
strongly express N4, with an elevated H-score. [12] The positivity rate and H-score were also elevated in subsequent
trials, including EV alone or in combination. [13][-][16] Otherwise,
several retrospective cohorts reported lower IHC positivity
rates and H-score.

In a multicenter EV-treated mUC cohort, N4 gene amplification (approximately 25% of cases) was linked to high N4expression levels, a high ORR of over 90%, and long-term
survival rates. [39] These data suggest that there could be a
linear association between N4 targetability and EV response,
which may help to optimize the inclusion of new treatments
(ie, EV/pembro) into regular care. [40]

Previous treatments could reduce N4 positivity and could be
linked to resistance to EV. [8][,][26][,][41][,][42] Miyake et al [23] reported a

Crupi et al


significant reduction in the mean H-score in postchemotherapy RC compared with baseline. By contrast, Ghali
et al [25] found that N4 positivity remained predominantly
conserved in both primary and matched metastatic samples
in the postchemotherapy and postimmunotherapy settings
for patients with conventional UC and non-NE HS. Ongoing
perioperative clinical trials, such as MK3475-B15 and
Keynote-905, offering EV 1 pembrolizumab in MIBC, represent an opportunity to compare the N4-positivity baseline
expression and changes in responders versus nonresponders, and assess correlation with outcomes.

Klumper et al [¨] [26] also showed that the N4 positivity significantly decreases during metastatic spread, with over 30% of
patient-matched distant metastases being N4-negative. In
their multicenter EV cohort, absent/weak N4 positivity
(Appendix Table A3) was associated with significantly
shortened PFS. Because of the complexity of N4 dynamics
and the intralesional and temporal heterogeneity, relying
solely on IHC from a single biopsy may not be fully representative, especially for patients undergoing multiple lines
of therapy. Several studies reported the intratumoral heterogeneity regarding N4 expression. [25][,][26] Conversely, a
bystanding effect for EV between N41 and N4- tumor cells
was proposed to explain a therapeutic effect despite the
positivity. [43][,][44]

Regarding N4 IHC, we know that beyond the antibody and
staining protocol used, the use of tissue microarray, the
interpretation (membrane only or membrane 1 cytoplasm
staining), the H-score’s cutoff chosen, preanalytical issue,
that is, sample age and fixation, and the type of tissue
(TURBT, cystectomy, and metastasis) are confounders.

Variations of N4 positivity may reflect differences in the
sensitivity and specificity of antibodies used for IHC staining.
The antibodies differed in their species origin (mouse, rabbit,
or goat) and type (monoclonal v polyclonal). Monoclonal
antibodies are produced by identical immune cells, all clones
of a unique parent cell, are more specific, and have less batchto-batch variability than polyclonal antibodies. [45]

The M22-244b3 clone was used in EV trials. However, direct
comparisons among different antibodies have yet to be
extensively investigated, potentially affecting the H-scores.
Further research and validation efforts are mandatory to
enhance reliability and reproducibility. Given the absence of
normative data on the cell membrane or cell cytoplasm
staining intensity in the literature, the assessment of N4
positivity in series and clinical trial data was arbitrary
(Appendix Table A3).

Regarding the correlation between N4 and different biomarkers, the HLAA2-restricted cytotoxic T lymphocyte
(CTL) epitope of N4 has been identified. [46] The strong expression of N4 in tumor cells might induce N4-specific CTLs,
potentially activated by immunotherapy. Ueki et al [24] investigated the association between N4 positivity and

12 | © 2024 by American Society of Clinical Oncology


response to pembrolizumab in a small, retrospective cohort
of patients with la/mUC. Although the mechanism of action
of pembrolizumab for targeting N4 protein is unclear, further studies on the correlation between N4 expression and
PD-1/PD-L1 are needed.

In a recent post hoc analysis of a cohort of patients with
MIBC, almost 50% of patients showed negative to weak
membranous N4 expression, exhibiting significant TROP2
expression. [47] Similarly, Bertucci et al [48] reported data on the
IHC expression of N4 and TROP2 in a cohort of 407 patients
with mUC. Almost 50% of the patients were N4-negative and
TROP2-positive. [48] Both authors suggested that this expression pattern likely contributed to the high ORR of 70%
reported for the combination of EV and sacituzumab govitecan in a phase I trial. [49] Further data are warranted to
understand better the heterogeneity of N4 expression and
the mutual expression with TROP2, particularly in the
context of ADC combination strategies.

Emerging data for noninvasive N4 assessment holds promise
for selection and dynamic treatment response evaluation.
Uan et al [50] conducted a first-in-human study using the
radioligand 68Ga-N188 for positron emission tomographychemotherapy imaging targeting N4 in advanced UC. Their
study presented a clear correlation between SUV value and
N4 expression. [50] Future trials exploring different tracers,
imaging protocols, and patient cohorts will contribute to a
better understanding of the strengths and limitations of N4
imaging techniques.

N4 positivity in UC seems dynamic, with high levels in
NMIBC. This might lead to a targeted treatment when an
organ-sparing approach is planned, especially in BCGunresponsive NMIBC. The EV-104 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov
[identifier: NCT05014139) in high-risk BCG-unresponsive](https://www.clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT05014139)
NMIBC is highly relevant. [51]

Our review has several limitations, including its retrospective and exploratory nature, selection and confounding
biases, the heterogeneity of the studies, clinical characteristics, tumor types, methodologies, and included data sets.
Most findings came from retrospective studies, which introduced potential variability in the methods, reported results, and follow-up times, also limiting interstudy
comparability. Variations in antibody specificity, staining
protocols, and assessment criteria contribute to inconsistencies in reported N4-positivity rates. Additionally, publication bias may have influenced the results.

Given these limitations, future research should focus on
conducting comparative studies with standardized methodologies to enhance the reliability of N4-expression data.
Investigations should comprehensively address critical
questions regarding the role of N4, evaluate the impact of N4
expression on treatment response, and explore the mechanisms of resistance.

Nectin-4 Positivity in Genitourinary Malignancies


In the era of anti-N4 ADCs, our review tries to shed light on
the unmet need to assess N4 expression in non-UC GU
malignancies, including rare diseases. The N4 positivity is an
attractive biomarker for selecting patients to enroll in desirable basket trials. Because of their rarity, this approach
can offer a biomarker-selected treatment.

In conclusion, data on N4 positivity in non-UC GU malignancies are limited, whereas more robust data exist for UC.
