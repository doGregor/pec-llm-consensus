INTRODUCTION

Human papillomavirus (HPV)–associated cancers impose a substantial burden on society. HPV causes more than 30,000
oropharyngeal, cervical, and other anogenital cancers in the United States each year. [1] HPV-associated oropharyngeal
cancer (OPC) has risen rapidly in incidence and has recently overtaken cervical cancer, and its incidence is expected to
continue to increase over the next few decades. [2,3] The treatment of HPV-associated cancers is associated with substantial
morbidity, and more than 8000 patients die of the disease annually. [4] These cancers are also expensive: the cost of cancer
care totals approximately $1 billion, and population-level cancer prevention costs an estimated $8 billion annually. [5]

Ongoing primary and secondary preventive interventions targeting HPV-associated cancers effectively protect particular populations [6] but fail to address all individuals vulnerable to HPV-related cancers. For example, prophylactic HPV
vaccination is recommended only for females and males aged 9 to 26 years and is optional for adults aged 27 to 45 years.

novel secondary preventive interventions. Blood-based
biomarkers represent an ideal potential modality for the

cytokine levels, vitamins and cofactors, and various gene

MATERIALS AND METHODS

*Overview*

The study protocol did not involve identifiable human
subjects, was classified as exempt human subjects research,


and did not require oversight from the Institutional Review
Board. With search concepts including *human papilloma-*
*virus*, *cancer*, *biomarkers*, and *blood*, formal comprehensive
search strategies were developed with the assistance of medical librarians (H.G.M. and K.L.). MEDLINE, EMBASE,
Web of Science, Scopus, and Cochrane Central were
searched from their inception through October 26, 2018,
with the search terms documented in the supporting information. In all, 5687 articles were identified and exported to
EndNote 8; 2179 duplicates were removed, and 3631 candidate titles and abstracts were independently reviewed by 2
authors (S.B. and S.K.). Disputes were arbitrated by a third
author (A.T.D.). Forty-three additional articles were identified on bibliographic review. Of the 3674 articles, 702 underwent a full-text review, and 137 were included (Fig. 1).

*Inclusion Criteria*

Eligible manuscripts included cohort and case-control
studies involving at least 5 patients with text available in
the English language. Cases were defined as patients with
primary, invasive cancers involving the oropharynx, cervix, anus, vulva, vagina, or penis with blood samples collected before treatment. Controls were defined as patients
without cancer or precancer.

*Exclusion Criteria*

Studies not evaluating a blood-based biomarker or studies
without human subjects were excluded. Articles without appropriate cases were excluded; they included studies without
invasive cancer cases (ie, patients with cervical intraepithelial
neoplasia [CIN] or carcinoma in situ only), studies aggregating cancers and precancers (ie, studies not differentiating
invasive cancer cases from dysplasia, CIN, or carcinoma in
situ), and non–subsite-specific studies that did not sufficiently differentiate between HPV+ and HPV– disease (ie,
head and neck mucosal squamous cell carcinoma patients
with involvement of the oropharynx or another site [oral
cavity, larynx, or hypopharynx]). Articles without appropriate controls were excluded; they included case-case studies,
case-only studies (ie, absence of a control group), and studies
aggregating controls with dysplasia or precancer (ie, inclusion of CIN patients with controls). Genotype and human
leukocyte antigen (HLA) studies were also excluded. In addition, because of the high volume of HPV antibody studies in the cervical cancer literature, blood-based antibody
studies involving this site with fewer than 500 patients (cases
plus controls) were excluded. Conversely, all eligible bloodbased antibody studies involving other anatomic sites were
included. Studies that did not present data in a numerical

DISCUSSION

This meta-analysis demonstrates that among all identified blood-based biomarkers to date, HPV-16 E
antibodies and circulating HPV DNA exhibited the
strongest associations with HPV-associated cancers.
Blood-based HPV-16 E antibody biomarkers exhibited
robust associations with oropharyngeal and anal cancers
and weaker associations with cervical, vulvovaginal, and
penile cancers. Notably, the strength of the associations
was significant across all 3 OPC study populations: prediagnostic serum specimens from cancer epidemiology
cohorts, and p16+/HPV+ or p16/HPV-unspecified
cases from serum collected at the time of diagnosis.

Prediagnostic HPV-16 E6 antibodies showed significant associations with cancer across all anatomic sub
sites. A recent nested OPC case-control analysis of pooled

0% of individuals more than 30 years before their OPC
diagnosis. The identification of a detectable HPV-16 E
antibody immune response decades before the development of OPC indicates that a subclinical disease state ex
ists. Collectively, these findings highlight the promise of
HPV-16 E6 antibodies in screening for HPV-associated
cancers and for HPV+ OPC in particular.

according to the anatomic site. HPV-16 E antibody biomarker validity is superior at oropharyngeal and anal sites
in comparison with cervical, vulvovaginal, and penile
sites; however, we identified only 5 noncervical anogenital cancer studies, highlighting the overall dearth of data
on blood-based biomarkers for these cancers. Therefore,
in light of available evidence and the lower incidence of
anal cancer, HPV-16 E antibodies may serve as a better
biomarker for OPC, the most common HPV-associated
cancer, in comparison with other HPV-associated cancers.
The heterogeneity in biomarker validity across anatomic sites is multifactorial. First, some investigators have
argued that infection or cancer arising in the oropharynx
may be more immunogenic than infection or cancer arising at other anatomic sites, such as the cervix. The oropharynx is largely lined by the palatine and lingual tonsils
(lymphoid organs contributing to humoral and cell-mediated immunity), whereas cervical immunity is mediated
by the mucosa. [71-73] However, this explanation does not
account for the superior validity of the biomarker for
cancers arising at the mucosally-lined anus. Ultimately,
an improved understanding of differences in immune
response in at-risk mucosal anatomic sites may advance
secondary prevention of HPV-associated cancers.
Second, differences in biomarker validity across anatomic sites may be attributed to differences in etiology.
For instance, approximately 75% of head and neck cancers overall are caused by alcohol and tobacco, with the
other 25% attributable to HPV. [3,74,75] In contrast, alcohol
and tobacco are less significant risk factors for anogenital
cancers in comparison with HPV. [76,77]

Third, the prevalence of HPV type–specific,
site-specific cancers also varies according to geographic
location. [17] Although high-risk HPV DNA is present in
82% of invasive cervical cancers and 79% of anal cancers worldwide, [17-19] in the United States, relatively higher
proportions of these cancers are attributable to high-risk
HPV: 91% of cervical cancers and 91% of anal cancers. [75] The worldwide prevalence of HPV-16–associated OPC varies even more greatly and is: 59.3% in the
United States (95% CI, 53.1%-65.5%), 31.1% in Europe
(95% CI, 22.7%-39.5%), and 4.1% in Brazil (95% CI,
1.1%-7.1%). [78] In our meta-analysis, we found that the
majority of OPC studies included populations from the
United States and Europe, and the majority of cervical
cancer studies included populations from Europe or Asia
(particularly India). These data highlight the need to
develop biomarkers accounting for the heterogeneity of
etiologies. Within individual cancer subsites, HPV-16 E6
antibodies generally trended toward stronger correlations


with HPV-associated cancers in comparison with E7
antibodies. Notably, our pooled analyses demonstrated
no significant differences in validity between the 2 antibody types. Nonetheless, these findings generally corroborate reports that HPV-16 E7 antibodies are present
in a higher proportion of cancer-free control patients
(2%-11%) in comparison with HPV-16 E6 antibodies
(0.4%-0.7%). [29,31,59,70,79,80]

Importantly, antibody cotesting (eg, E6 and/or E7)
exhibited relatively improved validity (E6 and/or E7) or
significantly improved validity (a predefined E antibody
panel) in comparison with independently assessed E antibodies (eg, E6) in pooled OPC case-control studies.
In their recent pooled HPV+ OPC case-control study
(n = 306), D’Souza et al [81] reported mixed results from
cotesting with multiple HPV-16 antibodies. HPV-16 E6
and E7 antibody testing improved specificity (100%)
but decreased sensitivity (69%); HPV-16 E6 and/or E7
testing improved sensitivity (94%) and decreased specificity (93%); and HPV-16 E1, E2, E6, and/or E7 testing
maximized sensitivity (96%) but further compromised
specificity (91%). In contrast, antibody testing of multiple HPV strains and antibodies in cervical cancers was
not shown to have as significant validity. In the absence
of expert consensus, additional validation studies comparing HPV-16 E antibody combinations will be needed
to identify the strongest antibody combination for
screening. [16,32,70,81]

In this meta-analysis, circulating HPV DNA exhibited validity in cervical cancer case-control studies comparable to that of HPV-16 E antibodies. In a review of
HPV+ OPC case-only or case-case studies (HPV+ OPCs
vs HPV– OPCs), pretreatment circulating HPV DNA
detected by conventional real-time and/or quantitative
techniques also exhibited high sensitivities ranging from
61% to 95%. [82] Advances in technology such as digital
droplet polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) may further
augment the validity of circulating HPV DNA. Using
ddPCR targeting select high-risk DNA strains, Chera et
al [83] and Wang et al [84] reported 88% and 86% sensitivity
among patients with known p16+ or HPV+ head and
neck cancer, respectively. Circulating HPV-16 DNA detected by ddPCR is also valid at other subsites: among patients with advanced anal carcinoma, the biomarker was
91% sensitive. [85]

Circulating HPV DNA has shown potential efficacy
in posttreatment cancer surveillance. The detection rate
of HPV DNA in cervical cancers is greater in later stage
cancers in comparison with carcinoma in situ, emphasizing that circulating HPV DNA appears to dynamically

reflect the real-time tumor status of newly diagnosed
patients with HPV-associated carcinomas. [69,86] There is
limited evidence that HPV-16 E6 seropositivity is useful
in the surveillance of patients with HPV-associated cancer. In one study, patients with HPV+ OPC recurrence
had higher E6 and E7 antibody levels than those who did
not experience recurrence ( *P* = .02 and *P* = .002, respectively). [87] These elevated antibody levels after treatment
might reflect evidence of response to the active tumor. [87]
Further studies are needed to evaluate the role of circu
lating HPV DNA and potentially HPV E antibodies in
HPV-associated cancer surveillance.

Pooled cervical cancer case-control study data suggest that the continued search for a blood-based microRNA expression signature for HPV-associated cancers
may be warranted. MicroRNAs are a class of noncoding
short-nucleotide RNAs that regulate posttranscriptional
gene expression. Among the 2500 microRNA sequences
identified to date, some microRNA signature associations
with other cancers and cancer-specific, prognostic, microRNA-based risk classifiers have been established. [88-101]

Further, investigators evaluating HPV+ tumor tissue
have reported discovery of unique microRNA expression
signatures. [102-105] Our meta-analysis features 8 unique
blood-based microRNAs which were correlated with cer
vical cancer, although, none were conserved across the 3
studies. However, because of the vast number of microRNAs and lack of specificity for HPV+ cancers, identifying
a valid expression profile for HPV-associated cancers may
prove challenging. Limited stability also constrain their
promise: microRNA testing requires more intensive and
rapid specimen collection, processing, and storage protocols. [41,106,107] More highly powered case-control studies
evaluating not only microRNA expression profiles specific to HPV-associated cancers but also effective and effi
cient sample analysis techniques are needed to strengthen
its utility as a biomarker.
Although several studies evaluated blood-based
folate, IGF-1, IGFBP-3, and IFN-γ levels as candidate
biomarkers for HPV-associated cancers, none were valid
according to our meta-analysis. There are multiple pathophysiologic bases for the consideration and evaluation of
these potential biomarkers. Folate is a cofactor in several
metabolic reactions, including mitosis and DNA synthesis, and prior studies have shown conflicting results with
respect to folate levels and cancer risk. [108-111] IGF-1 influences cell cycle progression and apoptosis, and IGFBP-3
binds to IGF-1 and thus prevents its degradation and increases its activity. Although lower levels of IGF-1 have
been associated with cervical cancer, higher levels of IGF


have been associated with cancers at other sites, such as
lung and colorectal cancers. [44] IFN-γ is a cytokine contributing to host antiviral responses and antitumor activity by augmenting natural killer cell activity and tumor
cell antigenicity, but it has also been shown to paradoxically promote tumor growth and prevent tumor immunogenicity. [112] The overall poor validity of these biomarker
candidates is likely mostly due to the lack of HPVassociated cancer specificity (unlike HPV E antibodies
and circulating HPV DNA). For example, although folate
levels in erythrocytes are more stable, serum folate levels
fluctuate with diet and assay type. [108] Similarly, IGF-1 and
IGFBP-3 levels vary by age, sex, and nutritional status,
and cytokine levels fluctuate with the immune state. [112,113]

Because of the known complex associations with
HPV-associated cancers and the already broad scope of
this review, we determined a priori to exclude bloodbased genetic markers from the analysis. However, because of the number of studies evaluating this topic,
a cursory discussion of the subject is warranted. To
our knowledge, the most commonly evaluated genetic markers for HPV-associated cancers are HLA
B*08, p53 SNPs, and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α)
SNPs. HLA-B*08 is a class II antigen with a high rate
of occurrence in European populations and with a potential role in autoimmunity. [114] Although 1 study reported that Indian patients with the HLA-B*08 antigen
exhibited 10 times greater odds of cervical cancer
( *P* = .002) in comparison with their respective counterparts, 3 other studies did not report a significant association. [115-117] SNPs are the substitution of a single base
pair for another and play a key role in the evaluation of
disease susceptibility, drug efficacy, and adverse drug reactions. [118] Some investigators have analyzed the TNF-α
G>A rs1800629 SNP. [119-121] According to Duarte et
al, [119] the TNF-α GA genotype showed a modest but
statistically significant difference in genotype distribution (16.4% in controls vs 25.6% in patients with cervical cancer; n = 439; *P* = .012). Other investigators
have analyzed the p53 G>C rs1042522 SNP, and a few
have found that the Arg/Arg genotype had a statistically
higher frequency in cases versus controls; this could sbe
due to the fact that the arginine residue at amino acid
72 may be more susceptible to degradation by HPV E6
protein in comparison with the proline residue. [122-124]
Finally, genetic studies have been inherently limited by
heterogeneous assessment (ie, allele vs genotype vs phenotype frequency), inconsistent use of nomenclature
across HLA studies (World Health Organization classification vs International Cell Exchange classification),

and a lack of a standardized reference group when odds
ratios are being calculated (ie, some p53 studies have
used the Arg/Arg genotype as the references, and others have used Pro/Pro). In addition, certain genotypes
are more prevalent in certain racial/ethnic groups,
and this also decreases the generalizability of genetic
biomarkers. [114-117]

Although robust screening strategies are established
for cervical cancer, other anogenital cancers and OPCs lack
effective preventative screening programs. According to the
analytic framework for evaluating a potential clinical preventive screening service described by the US Preventive
Service Task Force, an effective screening modality must
1) reduce morbidity and mortality, 2) accurately detect
the intended condition, and 3) do no harm. In addition,
diseases that are being screened must 1) have a target,
high-risk population; 2) have treatments for early or intermediate outcomes identified through screening; 3) have
treatments that improve overall disease outcomes if the disease is identified early through screening; 4) have strong
associations between intermediate outcomes and reduced

morbidity and mortality; and 5) have treatments that do
not cause significantly more harm than the disease itself. [125]

A blood-based biomarker is an attractive option
for a screening modality because of the widespread use
of blood-based laboratory tests in medicine and overall
low risk of harm. In our meta-analysis, we have highlighted the potential utility of HPV-16 E antibodies and
circulating HPV DNA for the screening and surveillance of HPV-associated cancers. However, the potential
use of these biomarkers in secondary prevention presents significant challenges. Although these biomarkers
have strong associations with HPV-associated cancers, a
highly specific biomarker is needed to limit false-positive results and reduce harm. Although the incidence of
HPV+ OPC is rising, HPV-associated cancers have a relatively low population-level incidence overall. Therefore,
the identification of a target higher risk population and
improved biomarker specificity will be needed before
the implementation of a screening program is possible.
Furthermore, these blood-based biomarkers lack utility
in that they fail to localize disease: subsequent clinical
and/or radiologic examination of all oropharyngeal and
anogenital sites in biomarker-positive individuals will
be necessary. Although we have identified promising
screening biomarker candidates, substantial progress will
be needed to ensure compliance with all US Preventive
Service Task Force screening intervention criteria.
To our knowledge, this is the most robust evaluation of blood-based biomarkers for HPV-associated


cancers in the literature. As highlighted previously, we
appropriately accounted for differences in biomarker
validity across anatomic sites by stratifying our analysis
accordingly. However, we were unable to further substratify data according to geographic location because
of the limited number of eligible studies. In addition,
because of the low incidence of anal, penile, vaginal,
and vulvar cancers, fewer studies evaluated blood-based
biomarkers at those sites, and this limited our analysis
to the evaluation of primarily OPC and cervical cancer biomarker candidates. Because few studies strat
ified biomarker associations with cancer according to
tumor stage, we were unable to evaluate this potential
correlation. We used rigorous inclusion criteria: hence,
60 otherwise eligible studies were excluded because of
inadequate data (ie, the data were not presented numerically, the data were insufficiently described [eg, the
study included controls in the experimental design but
did not present control data in the results], or the study
did not analyze a single blood-based biomarker alone

[eg, both HPV and *Chlamydia trachomatis* seropositivity]). Finally, because of the dynamic nature of the field,
several important and even landmark studies were published after the date of our literature search and were,
therefore, not included in the data analyses. [70,81,126]

In conclusion, among the biomarkers assessed in this
review, HPV E antibodies and circulating HPV DNA are
the most well-established and valid blood-based biomark
ers for HPV-associated cancers. The lower validity of HPV16 E antibodies in HPV-unspecified cervical, vulvovaginal,
and penile cancers calls attention to the need for biomarkers that account for the diversity of oncogenic HPV types.
Multiple HPV E antibody combinations showed strong
associations for OPC; however, comparative validation
studies are needed to identify the precise combination with
the greatest association with cancer. Studies to further characterize a blood-based microRNA expression signature for
HPV-associated cancers are warranted. In the absence of

novel findings, other previously assessed blood-based biomarker candidates exhibit little promise in screening for or
surveilling HPV-associated cancers.
