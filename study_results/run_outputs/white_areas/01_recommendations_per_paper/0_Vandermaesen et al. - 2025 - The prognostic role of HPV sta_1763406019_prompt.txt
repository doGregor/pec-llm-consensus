INTRODUCTION
Penile squamous cell carcinoma (PSCC) affects approximately 1
per 100,000 men in Western countries [1]. PSCC is a rare but
aggressive disease with involvement of the lymph nodes being
the most important prognostic factor, associated with high
morbidity and mortality [2–4]. The current standard of care
primarily involves surgical resection of the primary tumor and, if
necessary, malignant lymph nodes, which can lead to considerable decrease in patients’ quality of life [5, 6]. A major challenge in
the management of PSCC is the lack of reliable prognostic and
predictive markers, which could potentially determine treatment
decisions such as de-escalation of therapy.
The World Health Organization (WHO) currently classifies PSCC
into HPV-associated (HPV + ) and HPV-independent (HPV-) subtypes [7]. It is estimated that approximately 50% of PSCC globally
are associated with HPV [8]. During a persistent HPV infection with


a high-risk HPV genotype, the viral genome may integrate into the
host cell’s DNA, disrupting the viral life cycle. This integration often
leads to the constitutive transcription and translation of the viral
oncoproteins E6 and E7, which bind to and inactivate the tumor
suppressor proteins p53 and retinoblastoma-1 (Rb), driving cellular
dysregulation and potential malignant transformation, respectively [9]. The cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2 A (CDKN2A)
gene encodes inhibitor of cyclin-dependent kinase 4a p16
(p16 [INK4a] ), which inhibits cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 (CDK4/
CDK6), preventing phosphorylation of Rb and thereby halting the
G1-to-S phase transition in the cell cycle [9]. In HPV infection, the
E7 oncoprotein degrades Rb, leading to p16 overexpression as a
compensatory response [9]. p16 immunohistochemistry (IHC) is
therefore considered a surrogate marker for the presence of highrisk HPV infection with a sensitivity of 90-100% and specificity of
70% [10–13]. Other methods used to determine HPV status in

In other HPV-associated cancers such as oropharyngeal SCC and
vulvar SCC, high risk HPV DNA detection or p16 overexpression
have been demonstrated to be associated with more favorable
clinical outcomes [15, 16]. Although PSCC shares several
characteristics with these malignancies, previous studies have
yielded conflicting results regarding the prognostic significance of
HPV and p16 in PSCC [17–19]. In 2018, the first two meta-analyses
were published on the topic [20, 21]. Zhang et al. analyzed five
studies and found that p16 positivity was linked to better cancerspecific survival (CSS) [20]. Sand et al. reviewed 20 studies,
showing that both p16 and HPV positivity were associated with
improved CSS, but not overall survival (OS) [21]. Both literature
searches covered studies published between 1992 and 2017. An
updated systematic review by Parza et al. (2023), with its metaanalysis published by Mustasam et al. (2025), included 34 studies
(3944 patients) from March 1992 to January 2023 [22, 23]. They
confirmed links between both p16/HPV status and improved CSS,
and only found p16 positivity to be correlated with better OS

[22, 23].
Since early 2023, there has been an increase in observational
studies with larger cohorts documenting the prognostic value of
HPV DNA and p16 status in PSCC. However, previous metaanalyses do not include these recent higher-quality data and
given that accurate prognostic markers could inform treatment
decisions, stratification, and follow-up strategies for patients with
PSCC, there is a pressing need for an updated synthesis of the
evidence on the prognostic value of HPV status in PSCC. We
therefore perform an updated systematic review and metaanalysis to evaluate the prognostic impact of HPV DNA and
p16 status on cancer-specific survival (CSS) and overall survival
(OS) in patients with PSCC. To enhance the reliability and clinical
applicability of our findings, we applied strict inclusion criteria,
including only studies with a cohort size of at least 50 patients and
with a mean or medial follow-up of at least 24 months.

METHODS
Protocol and registration
This review was performed according to the Preferred Reporting
Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Supplementary fig. 1) [24], Cochrane review principles [25]
and AMSTAR2 criteria [26]. The protocol for this review has been
published (PROSPERO registration number: CRD42023420492).

Search strategy and study selection
The electronic databases MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL
(via Ovid) were searched for all relevant publications between
January 1980 and February 2025. The literature search was carried
out based on the search strategy provided in Supplementary Fig.
2. Following de-duplication, all abstracts and full text articles were
screened by two independent reviewers according to the criteria
identified in the eligibility form (Supplementary Fig. 3). Disagreement between two reviewers was resolved by discussion or
interference of an independent third party.

Eligibility criteria
We included randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, casecontrol studies, cross-sectional studies, and conference abstracts.
Publications reporting on HPV status and its association with
survival outcome (OS and/or CSS) in men with PSCC were
included. Case reports, narrative reviews, expert opinions, studies
with less than 50 participants, and studies reporting a survival
follow-up of less than 2 years were excluded. No exclusion was
made based on T-stage, positive lymph nodes (N1, N2, N3),
metastasis (M1), or treatment history. HPV detection tools such as

techniques giving HPV status data were included.

Data extraction and quality assessment
Survival data, participant characteristics and study characteristics
were independently extracted from the selected articles by one
reviewer and checked for accuracy by a second reviewer. Any
disagreement was resolved by discussion or by consulting a third
review author. A standardized data extraction form was developed
and piloted before its use. Data extraction was informed by the
REporting recommendations for tumor MARKer prognostic studies
(REMARK) [27]. The following data were extracted from each study:
study design, countries and institutions where the data were
collected, recruitment period, follow-up period, HPV status
definition and measurement, numbers of participants included,
participant demographic and clinical characteristics (e.g. disease
stage, co-morbidities, treatment), number of patients with HPV+
and HPV- disease, loss and exclusions of participants, and all
unadjusted (univariate) and adjusted (multivariable) associations
between HPV status and outcome. The risk of bias (RoB) and
quality of each included study was independently assessed by two
review authors using the QUIPS tool [28], as recommended by
the Cochrane Prognosis Methods Group [25]. Important cofounding factors were defined as (neo-)adjuvant treatment, TNM
stage, tumor grade, and age of the patients. Any disagreements
were resolved by discussion or by consulting a third reviewer.
A summary of the RoB assessment was generated in RevMan
(v5.4) [29].

Statistical analysis and meta-analysis
Descriptive statistics were used to summarize all studies included
in this systematic review.
For the meta-analysis, only studies with sufficient data to
determine hazard ratios (HR) for OS or CSS were included. Both
unadjusted (univariate) and adjusted (multivariable) HR were
extracted. When both adjusted and unadjusted HR were available,
adjusted values were preferentially included in the meta-analysis
to minimize heterogeneity. If standard errors were absent, we
filled in the missing values using reported P-values or 95%
confidence intervals (CI). For consistency, HR were recalculated
whenever necessary so that HR > 1 indicate a better prognosis in
HPV-positive patients relative to HPV-negative patients. If survival
data were presented only as survival rates, HR were derived based
on the reported values whenever possible. For studies that did not
report HR or survival rates directly but only provided Kaplan-Meier
survival curves, with or without the number of patients at risk, we
extracted survival data from published Kaplan-Meier plots and
estimated HR and 95% confidence intervals using the method
described by Tierney et al. [30]. Studies that did not provide
sufficient data to calculate HR using any of the aforementioned
approaches were excluded from the meta-analysis. Moreover,
studies with overlapping study populations were carefully
reviewed and in such cases where overlap was identified, we
prioritized the selection of studies with the longest follow-up
duration, to minimize duplication bias.
Finally, standardized regression coefficients for continuous
outcomes were converted to log HR as appropriate for synthesis

[31, 32]. We conducted the meta-analyses using a random effects
model, HR were pooled using the inverse variance method, and
forest plots were generated in RevMan (v5.4) to visualize the
extracted effects of p16 and HPV status on the survival outcomes

[29]. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the I²

– –
statistic, with <20%, 25 50, 50 75, and >75% representing

funnel plots and Egger’s test using metafor package (v4.6.0) in R

DISCUSSION
In this systematic review, we included 31 studies, of which 23 were
eligible for inclusion in our meta-analysis. We showed that p16
positivity in patients with PSCC is significantly associated with
improved CSS (HR 0.40, 95% CI [0.20, 0.80], p = 0.009) and OS (HR
0.49, 95% CI [0.29, 0.85], p = 0.01). In contrast, HPV DNA positivity
only showed a statistically significant association with CSS (HR
0.54, 95% CI [0.35, 0.83], p = 0.005), but not with OS (HR 0.86, 95%
CI [0.70, 1.07], p = 0.17). These findings align with previous reports

[20–23] and build upon the analysis of Mustasam et al. [23]. While
the systematic review by Parza et al. (2023) and the meta-analysis
by Mustasam et al. (2025) included 34 studies, our review included
31. This difference is due to our more stringent inclusion criteria,
which required a minimum cohort size of 50 patients and a followup duration of at least two years to minimize heterogeneity and

Our systematic review confirms that HPV-associated PSCC
appears to be linked with more favorable survival outcomes
compared to HPV-independent PSCC —an observation consistent
with other HPV-related cancers [51, 52]. This supports the
importance of two distinct etiological pathways in PSCC: one that
is HPV-driven, typically occurring in younger patients and
associated with better prognosis, and another that is HPVindependent, often linked to chronic inflammatory conditions
such as lichen sclerosis, balanitis, or phimosis [53, 54]. Accurately
defining these two pathways is essential for reliable prognostication and for guiding treatment decisions tailored to the underlying
tumor biology. Given that p16 in our meta-analysis was
significantly associated with both improved CSS and OS, while
HPV DNA positivity was only correlated with CSS, our findings
suggest that p16 expression may serve as a more reliable
prognostic marker in PSCC compared to HPV DNA detection
alone. A first possible explanation for this discordance in OS might
be that p16 more accurately reflects transcriptionally active,
oncogenic HPV involvement. The presence of viral DNA without


integration or expression of viral oncoproteins (e.g., E6/E7), and
thus p16, may have low or no impact on tumor progression and
might explain why HPV DNA positivity fails to consistently
correlate with OS outcomes [55, 56]. HPV DNA-based assays may
detect contaminating or non-integrated viral fragments, which
can lead to the false classification of PSCCs as HPV-associated.
Additionally, several studies in our review included both high-risk
and low-risk HPV types in their DNA-based analyses [46, 57], which
further reinforces that HPV detection using these methods does
not necessarily imply a oncogenic role in tumor biology or
progression. In contrast to HPV detection tools, p16 IHC reflects
downstream functional effects of viral oncogenic activity, making
it a more reliable marker of HPV integration [11, 12, 58]. It is also
noteworthy that p16 IHC is more cost-effective, widely available,
and technically straightforward compared to molecular HPV
assays. It is currently recommended by the WHO for the diagnosis
of HPV-associated disease [53]. However, heterogeneity in p16 IHC
interpretation remains a challenge. Current reports recommend
defining p16 positivity as a “block-positive” pattern only when
≥75% of tumor cells show strong nuclear and cytoplasmic staining

[10, 11]. In our literature search, only 3 of the 10 studies assessing
p16 status clearly adhered to this standard. Moreover, p16 IHC has
