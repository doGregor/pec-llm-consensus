dysfunction (ED) and stress urinary incontinence (SUI) remain unclear.

INTRODUCTION

Erectile dysfunction (ED) and stress urinary incontinence (SUI) are
the most frequent complications after radical prostatectomy. [1] The risk
of post prostatectomy ED and SUI may be up to 94% and 31%, respectively, while, in many cases, the 2 complications coexist. [2][,][3] Concomitant
ED and SUI can occur not only after surgery, radiation, hormone deprivation, or chemotherapy for prostate cancer; but also due to bladder and
colorectal malignancies, as well as due to neurological conditions. [4] ED
may be initially managed with phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors,
intracavernosal injections, and other treatment modalities, [5][,][6] whereas

SUI may be managed with pelvic floor training, biofeedback, and other
conservative approaches. [7] Nevertheless, when these treatments are not
effective or desirable, patients may elect for surgical intervention.

The surgical treatment for ED is centered on penile prosthesis
implantation, whereas the surgical treatment for SUI is mainly driven
by the severity of symptoms, the magnitude of bother, and a history of
radiation therapy. Severe SUI is predominantly managed with the placement of an artificial urinary sphincter (AUS), [8] while mild or moderate
SUI may also be managed with a male urethral sling or, in rare cases,
with a noncircumferential compression device (ProACT). [9] More
recently, the Mini-Jupette sling has been described as a technique for
the management of patients ED and concomitant climacturia and/or
mild SUI. [10]

Accumulating evidence suggests that synchronous surgery for concomitant ED and SUI reduces costs and rehabilitation time, without
compromising safety and efficacy compared to asynchronous, 2-stage
surgery. [11] Alternatively, synchronous surgery may increase the shortand long-term complication rates compared to isolated surgery for ED
or SUI. [12] We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis aiming
to evaluate the available synchronous surgical approaches for concomitant ED and SUI and evaluate the reoperation rates compared to asynchronous surgery and surgery solely for ED or SUI.

METHODS

Search strategy
This systematic review and meta-analysis was performed based
on the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and the PRISMA statement. [13][,][14] The methodology was prespecified in a protocol registered at PROSPERO (ID:
CRD42022326941). PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Embase
databases were systematically searched until June 2022. A targeted
hand-search of grey literature (conference abstracts published in
major journals, clinical trial registries, and reference lists of all
included studies) was also performed. The detailed search syntax
and search string are available in Supplementary Material 1.

Selection criteria, data extraction, and quality

assessment

We included prospective or retrospective comparative studies
in patients undergoing any synchronous surgical approach due to
concomitant ED and SUI. Single-arm studies published in any
language evaluating different synchronous surgical approaches in
patients with concomitant ED and SUI or climacturia were also
considered eligible. On the contrary, we excluded studies that
did not encompass patients that underwent synchronous surgery
and assessed surgery only for ED or SUI. Furthermore, case
reports and review articles were not eligible. When multiple
records with overlapping populations were identified, the most
recent study was only considered.

Two authors (NP, DB) performed an independent screening
of the title and abstract of all identified records based on our eli
gibility criteria. The eligible full texts were subsequently evaluated and information on baseline values, perioperative outcomes,


and follow-up data was retrieved for each included study. The
Robins `−` I tool was used to evaluate the risk of bias in the
included studies. [15] Any discrepancies throughout the whole process were resolved by consensus.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis
The primary outcome of this systematic review and metaanalysis was the reoperation rate after synchronous surgery for
ED and SUI versus asynchronous surgery, as well as versus surgery solely for ED or SUI. Secondary outcomes included mean
improvement in erectile function and daily pad use, subjective
improvement of SUI and climacturia, as well as mean operative
time. Based on data availability, we undertook an inverse variance random effects meta-analysis of odds ratios (ORs) comparing reoperation rates after synchronous surgery in patients with
concomitant ED and SUI versus asynchronous surgery, as well as
surgery solely for ED or SUI. Heterogeneity was assessed with
the I [2] and its significance was determined with the Cochran `’` s Q
test. [16] The corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were
estimated for all meta-analytic effects. All analyses were performed with the R statistical software (version 3.6.3) and P-values lower than .05 were considered statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

The present systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrates
that synchronous surgery with penile prosthesis and AUS results
in similar reoperation rates compared to asynchronous surgery.
However, combined surgery with penile prosthesis and AUS is
associated with a higher reoperation rate compared to single
placement of a penile prosthesis or AUS. In terms of functional
outcomes, the data suggest that the available surgical approaches
offer similar satisfaction rates, as well as similar levels of postoperative continence and erectile function when applied after
thoughtful workup and upon correct indications. Of note, the
existing scarce evidence suggests that combination of penile prosthesis with a male sling or the ProACT device may be safe and
effective in the hands of experienced prosthetic surgeons, but further high-quality studies are mandatory. Similarly, the Mini-Jupette is an emerging and promising technique to treat climacturia,
as well as leakage in patients with minimal SUI. Preliminary single-arm studies have demonstrated safety but further studies are
needed for validation as well as long term outcomes.

Our findings demonstrate that it is reasonable to consider
concomitant ED and SUI surgery. However, it should be recognized that our review found concomitant surgery to be associated
with a higher reoperation rate. Synchronous surgery offers several
advantages including reduced cost, quicker recovery of both ED
and incontinence, and a single recovery period. [35] A young prostate cancer survivor who still works full-time may find these benefits substantial and be a good candidate for a combined surgery.
However, there are several disadvantages of synchronous surgery
including potentially more involved recovery, longer operative
times, and a higher infection risk. [26] Additionally, operative teams
may be unfamiliar with a dual AUS and penile prosthesis placement and this risk cannot be understated, especially for the lowvolume implanter. Ultimately, these are complex patients who
will have unique goals of care and it is critical for surgeons to
individualize treatment which may or may not include concomitant surgery for both ED and SUI. [35]

Even though concomitant ED and SUI surgery is not widely
accepted due to the aforementioned reasons, it should be
highlighted that it is associated with important cost savings


compared to asynchronous surgery for ED and SUI. In particular, Sellers et al. performed a cost analysis comparison and suggested that synchronous inflatable penile prosthesis and AUS
placement costs about $25,000 compared to $32,000 after asynchronous inflatable penile prosthesis and AUS placement, [26]

while Gorbatiy et al. stated that synchronous implantation of an
inflatable penile prosthesis and a male sling saved about $9,000
compared to asynchronous surgery. [28] Across different treatment
modalities for refractory ED and SUI, it should be noted that
inflatable penile prosthesis and AUS are considered the most
cost-effective. [36][,][37] Still, the existing evidence is scarce and substantial research is mandatory to assess the cost-effectiveness of
different concomitant ED and SUI treatment modalities across

different patient populations, countries, and reimbursement systems. [38] A full cost analysis was beyond the scope of this article.

The findings of the present study should be interpreted with
respect to some limitations relevant to the methodological quality
of most included studies. Additionally, there is significant heterogeneity in how these surgeries are performed. For instance, penile
prosthesis can be placed through penoscrotal, infrapubic, subcoronal, and rarely a perineal incision; whereas, AUS is placed
typically through a perineal or penoscrotal incision. Dual
implantation can be carried out through a single or multiple incisions. AUS cuff sizing strategies vary among surgeons. Given
these significant variations from operation to operation, comparing outcomes among different studies is challenging. Due to the
paucity of comparative data, no strict recommendations on the
matter can be made. Even though we performed a systematic
review and meta-analysis focusing on functional outcomes, complications, and reoperation rates, the level of provided evidence
remains low. Prospective studies were characterized by a small
sample size, short follow-up, or lack of comparative arm, whereas
retrospective studies, in most cases, did not adequately address
the effect of surgical treatment on functional outcomes (improvement of ED and SUI). Moreover, well-designed, prospective,
comparative studies are lacking. Based on the previous notion,
the paucity of high-quality data did not permit us to undertake
additional predefined analyses and comparisons among the different surgical strategies. Importantly, our findings may not
reflect the real-world data on reoperation rates after surgery for
ED and SUI, considering that most included studies in the
meta-analysis had a retrospective design and were conducted by
high-volume expert surgeons.

CONCLUSION

The present systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrate
that synchronous penile prosthesis and AUS implantation seems
to be equally safe and effective as asynchronous, 2-stage surgery
in patients with severe ED and SUI. Placement of only a penile
prosthesis or an AUS, however, results in lower reoperation rates.
Nevertheless, the available evidence is limited. The literature
regarding male sling, ProACT, and Mini-Jupette slings is
