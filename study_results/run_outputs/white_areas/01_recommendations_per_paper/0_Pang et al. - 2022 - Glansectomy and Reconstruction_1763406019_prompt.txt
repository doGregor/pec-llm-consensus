1. Introduction


Abstract

Penile cancer (PeCa) is a rare disease, with a global incidence of 36 068 new cases
reported in the 2020 GLOBOCAN database. Narrower excision margins are now
acceptable without compromising survival. Glansectomy is commonly performed for
invasive PeCa confined to the glans penis. The majority of data on glansectomy are from
small single-centre series. We provide a contemporary update on the outcomes of glansectomy via a systematic review of glansectomy for PeCa. Overall, 20 studies were
included in the analysis. The local recurrence rate was 2.6–16.7%. The incidence of
salvage penectomy for positive margins and/or recurrence was 1.2–8.3%. The diseasespecific survival rate was 89–96.6%. A split-thickness skin graft was commonly used
to reconstruct a neoglans and the graft loss rate was 1.5–23.5%. The incidence of meatal
stenosis was 2.8–14.3%. Good cosmetic outcomes and normal erections were reported in
95–100% and 50–100% of cases, respectively. Glansectomy provides acceptable
oncological control without significantly compromising functional outcomes.
Patient summary: Penile cancer invading into the head of the penis can be surgically
treated with a procedure called glansectomy that has good cancer control and cosmetic
outcomes. In addition, penile length can be preserved, which allows men to urinate
standing up and to achieve penetrative sexual intercourse.

2. Methods

We used OVID to perform a search on MEDLINE/PubMed
and EMBASE using the terms ‘‘glansectomy OR penilepreserving surgery’’ AND ‘‘penis OR glans’’ AND ‘‘cancer
OR malignancy OR tumour OR neoplasia’’. Original articles
reporting on cancer confined to the glans treated with
glansectomy were included. Reviews, case reports, letters,
editorials, congress abstracts, and articles not in English
were excluded. A total of 211 articles were retrieved, and
175 underwent abstract screening after removal of duplicates. Abstracts, full-text articles, and the reference lists of
the articles included were independently screened for
appropriateness by two reviewers (K.H.P. and H.M.A.).
Data on study design, number of patients, complications,
and recurrence and survival rates were extracted, and the
lower and upper ranges for oncological outcomes were
summarised.

3. Results

Overall, 26 full-text articles and six articles identified from
reference lists were screened. In total, 20 were included in
our analysis [3,6–24]. We identified 16 studies reporting
on glansectomy alone [6–18,20,21,24,25] and ten studies
reporting on PPS as a whole [2,3,5,19,22,23,26–29]. However, it was only possible to extract data for glansectomy
alone from four of the PPS studies [3,19,22,23].

3.1. Glansectomy technique

PeCa invading the glans spongiosum is deemed suitable for
glansectomy. Depending on the size and location of the
tumour, the entire glans or part of the glans can be excised.
Tumour extending into the distal corporal tips may also
require a distal corporectomy. A neoglans can be reconstructed using a split-thickness skin graft (STSG) harvested
from the thigh [21] followed by a tie-over dressing for graft
application (TODGA technique), which allows immediate
patient mobilisation and discharge home with a urethral
catheter [30].

3.2. Glansectomy outcomes

We identified 16 studies on partial or total glansectomy
(Table 1), which may also combine a distal corporectomy
followed by reconstruction with a STSG. The LR and positive
surgical margin (PSM) rates were 2.6–16.7% and 2.9–22.6%,
respectively. The incidence of salvage penectomy for positive margins and/or recurrence was 1.2–8.3%. The overall
survival (OS) rate was 78.6–91.9% and the disease-specific
survival rate was 89–96.6%. The graft loss rate was


1.5–23.5% and the meatal stenosis rate was 2.8–14.3%. The
assessment of functional outcomes was heterogeneous,
with some studies using validated questionnaires and some
using locally designed nonvalidated tools. Good cosmetic
outcomes were reported in 95–100% and normal erectile
function in 50–100% of cases (Table 1).
Roussel et al [17] recently reported results for a large
multicentre series consisting of 897 glansectomies. At median follow-up of 35 months, the LR rate was 10.5% and the
PSM rate was 12.6%. The 1-yr, 3-yr, and 5-yr recurrencefree survival rates were 93.6%, 89.1%, and 86.4%, respectively. High-grade disease and pT3 stage were predictors
of LR on multivariable analysis [17].
Tang et al [24] reported an LR rate of 7.6% at median
follow-up of 42 mo for a large multicentre series of 410
glansectomy patients. The 1-yr, 2-yr, and 5-yr recurrencefree survival rates were 98%, 94%, and 78%, respectively.
Albersen et al [20] investigated factors predictive of LR
following glansectomy using pathological data for 117
men. On univariate analysis, carcinoma in situ, high-grade
disease, perineural invasion, and PSM were predictive of LR.

4. Discussion

PPS is now the standard treatment to preserve penile length
and function without compromising oncological outcomes

[2,3]. Smaller resection margins are now accepted since
studies showed that a 2-cm clear margin is no longer
required for PeCa surgery [2,3]. Therefore, invasive lesions
confined to the glans can be safely treated with glansectomy. Most single-centre studies on glansectomy are small
series with fewer than 50 patients, although one series has
reported on 172 men [21].
The overall LR and PSM rates observed here were up to
16.7% and 22.6%, respectively. Those with LR or PSM can
be offered a salvage penectomy, and the amputation rate
was up to 8.3%. PSM appears to be a predictor of LR and
therefore frozen section analysis of proximal margins
should be considered in some cases to avoid LR and penectomy. Formal staging with magnetic resonance imaging is
important to distinguish between T2 (confined to spongiosum) and T3 (cavernosa involvement) disease in order to
select the correct patients for glansectomy alone or with
distal corporectomy. Following glansectomy, obtaining
deep separate tunical biopsies from the corpora helps with
accurate pathological assessment of surgical margins.
Slight variations in glans reconstruction may explain the
variable graft take rates and cosmetic outcomes observed in
this study. In addition, the varied tumour stage and followup duration within the studies may influence bias. However, in general, glansectomy provides good oncological
outcomes with preservation of function.
There have been a few reported series on PPS as a whole
in the past few years, and some were not included in the
current analysis because separation of data was not possible. However, it is worth discussing the largest series in
the literature by Kamel et al [28], which included 4155
men who underwent PPS or amputation. A total of 1211
men underwent PPS and 2944 underwent PP or total penectomy (TP). The 5-yr OS rates for PPS, PP, and TP were 88%,
