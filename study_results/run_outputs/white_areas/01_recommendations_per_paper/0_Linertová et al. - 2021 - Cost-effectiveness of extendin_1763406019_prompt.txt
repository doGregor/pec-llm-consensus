**ABSTRACT**
**Background** Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection
can have severe consequences both in women and
men. Preadolescent girls are vaccinated against HPV
worldwide but a gender- ­neutral vaccination is being
adopted only little by little. This systematic review offers
an overview of cost- ­effectiveness of the gender- ­neutral
HPV vaccination.
**Methods** Economic evaluations of gender- ­neutral
HPV vaccination with a two- ­dose schedule compared
with girls- ­only strategy were systematically searched in
Medline, Embase and WOS up to June 2020. Incremental
cost- ­effectiveness ratios and key parameters were
analysed.
**Results** Nine studies met the inclusion criteria. Four
studies concluded in favour of the gender- ­neutral
programme, another four found it cost- ­effective only in
alternative scenarios. The most influential parameters
are the discount rate of benefits (same as for costs vs
reduced), vaccine price (listed vs publicly negotiated) and
included health problems (inclusion of oropharyngeal
and penile cancers). Sponsorship was not decisive for the
final result, but there were differences between industry- ­
funded and independent studies in some cost categories.
**Conclusions** The evidence of the cost- ­effectiveness
of extending HPV vaccination to boys is scarce and
ambiguous. Before the adoption of such a strategy,
countries should carry out context- ­specific cost- ­
effectiveness analyses, but the decision should also
take into account other criteria, such as gender- ­related
equality.

**INTRODUCTION**
Although the highest burden of human papillomavirus (HPV) continues to predominate in women, a
substantial burden is now recognised in men. In this
population, HPV is responsible for a proportion of
anal, oropharyngeal and penile cancers, as well as
anogenital warts. [1] Consequently, women and men
can suffer from HPV infection, which makes the
question of whether to rely on the herd effect of
the female immunisation, which does not protect
men who have sex with men (MSM) population,
or to offer the same protection to males a case of
urgency. [2]

Currently, there are three vaccines authorised
by the European Commission for the prevention
of various diseases related to HPV: the bivalent
(2- ­valent), tetravalent (4- ­valent) and nonavalent
(9- ­valent) vaccines. These vaccines offer protection

against specific pathogenic HPV genotypes that
are included in each vaccine. All three vaccines are
licensed for the prevention of cervical cancer, as
well as precancerous lesions of the cervix, vulva,
vagina and anus (HPV- ­related diseases 16/18).
Only the 4- ­valent and 9- ­valent vaccines are indicated to prevent genital warts as well. At present,
WHO recommendations indicate that two doses of
HPV vaccine are enough when given to preadolescents under 15 years of age, while the three- ­dose
schedule, previously used in preadolescents, is now
recommended for those above 15 years of age and
for immunocompromised individuals. [3]

By 2018, all European Union (EU) countries had
introduced HPV vaccination in girls. [3] Moreover,
by 2020, 45% of the EU countries offer a gender- ­
neutral HPV immunisation, that is, vaccination of
both girls and boys (updated in June 2020). [4] Outside
the EU, gender- ­neutral vaccination against HPV is
offered in the USA, Canada, Australia, Israel, New
Zealand, Argentina and Switzerland. However,
the cost- ­effectiveness of the extension of the HPV
immunisation to boys has been evaluated in only a
few countries. [5] When there is no country- ­specific
cost- ­effectiveness analysis for the decision- ­making,
the countries have to base their HPV immunisation
politics on criteria other than the cost- ­effectiveness.
Some countries even decided to extend the vaccination programme to boys in spite of unfavourable
cost- ­effectiveness analysis (eg, Ireland or Canada), [6 7]
given that the economic aspect is not the only criterion to be taken into account. The issue of justice,
gender equality and non- ­discrimination seems to be
especially urgent. [8–10]

We systematically reviewed the evidence on cost- ­
effectiveness of the extension of a two- ­dose HPV
vaccination to boys in countries where only- ­girls
HPV vaccination had been established.

**METHODS**
A review protocol was established at the beginning of the study, and the authors can provide it

on request.

**Selection criteria**
Comprehensive economic evaluations that compared
a gender- ­neutral HPV vaccination programme with
a girls- ­only vaccination programme. The population comprised girls and boys from 9 to 13 years
of age, who received two doses of one of the three
available vaccines (2- ­valent, 4- ­valent or 9- ­valent).

**Search and selection**
The search was performed in three electronic databases, Medline,
Embase and WOS, in April 2019 and was updated in June 2020,
using economic evaluations filters, without any further restric[tions. The search strategy can be found in the online supple-](https://dx.doi.org/10.1136/jech-2020-216305)

database were imported into a file of the Reference Manager
Edition V.10 (Thomson Scientific, USA), for the elimination of
duplicated references. It was subsequently exported to a Microsoft Excel 2013 sheet (Microsoft) where the study selection was
carried out by two reviewers and disagreements were solved by
a third reviewer.

**Data extraction and methodological quality assessment**
Selected articles were exhaustively read in full text by two
reviewers. Relevant data of included studies were extracted by
a reviewer and checked by another one. Methodological quality
was assessed by the Consensus on Health Economic Criteria
(CHEC) list. [11]

**Analysis**
Costs and health results of each alternative and the incremental
cost- ­effectiveness ratios (ICER) were considered. Key parameter
values were analysed according to the sponsorship. For comparison purposes, selected analysed costs were converted to EUR
2017 in Spain using the web- ­based tool Campbell and Cochrane
Economics Methods Group—EPPI- ­Centre Cost Converter
v.1.6, [12] which takes into account gross domestic product (GDP)
deflator index and conversion rates based on purchasing power
parities for GDP.

**Characteristics of the studies**
Four studies were funded by the manufacturer of one of the evaluated vaccines. [13–15 20] Three studies funded by MSD, manufacturer of the 4- ­valent and 9- ­valent vaccines, adapted a dynamic
transmission model for Spain, Italy and Germany. [13–15] Another
study was funded by GSK, manufacturer of the 2- ­valent vaccine,
and developed a static Markov model for Netherlands. [20] The
rest of the studies were not directly sponsored by industry,
although two studies declared a conflict of interest of one or
more authors. [7 18] These non- ­industry- ­funded analyses were
performed for Ireland, Canada, Sweden, UK and Netherlands
(table 1).
All studies, except one, [18] carried out a cost- ­utility analysis
with quality- ­adjusted life years (QALY) as an effect outcome.
Five studies used a dynamic transmission model, [7 13–15 19] while
the others opted for some kind of modified static model. Despite
the different modelling methods, all the models simulated the
transmission of the infection in the population counting on the
herd effect. Most of the studies adapted an existing model that
had been developed for HPV vaccination in another context
(different strategies, or country). Thus, the epidemiological
model developed in the USA by Elbasha *et al* [21] was adapted for
Spain, Italy and Germany, [13–15] while another model from the
USA developed by Chesson *et al* [22] was adapted for Ireland and
Sweden by their health technology assessment agencies. [6 16 17]

Vaccination coverage in girls and in boys varied greatly between
studies, depending on the vaccine situation in each country and
historical uptake in girls. The uptake in boys was assumed by
the authors and some studies assumed the same coverage in boys
and girls. [7 14 15 17 19] However, a lower uptake in boys seems more
realistic, taking into account the experience of countries where
gender- ­neutral vaccination has been introduced. [3]

**Cost-effectiveness results**
The results of the included studies are ambiguous and not always
easily interpretable. The three MSD- ­funded studies claimed that
a gender- ­neutral strategy would be cost- ­effective (or marginally
cost- ­effective) in Spain, Germany and Italy, when comparing
the gender- ­neutral 9- ­valent vaccine with girls- ­only 4- ­valent
vaccine. [13–15] However, looking at the cost- ­effectiveness frontier,
the gender- ­neutral 9- ­valent strategy should have been compared
with girls- ­only 9- ­valent strategy, which was the next most


effective option. In this scenario, the gender- ­neutral strategy
would only be cost- ­effective in Italy (ICER €16 495/QALY
according to our own calculations).
The results showed a great sensitivity of the ICERs to the
values of key parameters such as the price of the vaccine (listed
vs tender), discount rate of benefits (same as for costs vs reduced)
or included health problems (inclusion of oropharyngeal and
penile cancers). When these values were modified, in many cases
the ICER fell below the threshold of cost- ­effectiveness (willingness to pay), that is, the gender- ­neutral strategy became cost- ­
effective (table 3).

**Quality assessment**
All the included studies met the basic criteria of methodological quality. The most important flaws were due to not reporting
physical units for costs, or not including certain key parameters
in sensitivity analysis. Five studies used a dynamic transmission
model, whose general limitation is the inability to perform probabilistic sensitivity analysis and relative opacity compared with
static models. On the other hand, if applied well, they can better
simulate the complex social behaviour of the whole population. [23]

Almost all the models were published in peer- ­reviewed journals, except the Irish ETS report. [6] Journals often place limits on
the length and format and although appendices with additional
information are allowed, the format does not promote transparency in the presentation of data. Given the inherent complexity
of any infectious disease model, the brevity of the information
does not allow to decide whether it meets all the requirements.
In addition, many studies adapt previously published models
that are referenced rather than offering an exhaustive description of their structure. Another source of bias is the conflict of
interest which was present in six studies out of nine. [7 13–15 18 20]
Full evaluation with the CHEC list instrument can be found in

**DISCUSSION**
This systematic review offers an overlook of the cost- ­
effectiveness of the gender- ­neutral HPV vaccination in countries with established girls- ­only HPV vaccination, and it presents


heterogeneous results. In spite of this, all the analysed countries,
except Spain, have reached the decision to fund the gender- ­
neutral HPV programme. Looking at the results of the analysis
in each country, the ICER was only clearly below the threshold
in Italy, Netherlands and Sweden. [14 17 18 20] On the contrary, the
ICERs estimated for Germany, Spain, Ireland, UK and Canada
surpassed the threshold in their base cases. [6 7 13 15 19]

Beyond the efficiency criteria, other dimensions such as
equity and reducing inequalities related to the feminisation of
HPV have been reported as key drivers in this decision, hence
explaining this inconsistency. [24 25] The cost- ­effectiveness component is a relevant criterion in healthcare decision- ­making, but
not the only one. [26] For example, in the palliative care, the
National Institute for Health and Care Excellence accepts from
2009 interventions whose ICER is above the standard threshold
because of the value that society gives to the time at the end
of life. [27] To solve these dilemmas, multicriteria decision analysis
has been proposed since it allows accepting ethical dimension
by incorporating social values such as the reduction of inequalities. [26] Regarding the HPV vaccine- ­related equity, the conception
that by protecting the female population, the male population
would be protected by extension, has proven false and led to
inequity issues, making several male populations more vulnerable. [8] The initial view of herd immunity by extension is based
on a heteronormative view, and girls- ­only HPV vaccination
does achieve sufficient protection for all men and excludes some
high- ­risk groups like MSM. [25] In addition, the systematic review

From the methodological point of view, it has been argued
that the evidence for some of the parameters related to the
consequences in men of HPV infection, such as the relationship
between HPV and anal, penile and oropharyngeal cancers, was
low. [28] It is true that current evidence of the efficacy of the HPV
vaccine in men is limited to the prevention of persistent HPV
infections, genital warts and precursor lesions of anal cancer
(anal intraepithelial neoplasia) with the 4- ­valent vaccine. [5] No
significant estimate of the efficacy of the vaccine to prevent
penile intraepithelial lesions and anal, penile and oropharyngeal cancers has been documented. [28] However, oropharyngeal
cancer is the second most common HPV- ­related cancer in the
USA, and it is estimated that it will soon exceed HPV- ­related
cervical cancers. [29] It seems biologically plausible that reducing
HPV exposure would reduce the incidence of associated head
and neck cancers.
As expected, when a study included effectiveness against penile
and oropharyngeal cancers, the cost- ­effectiveness of gender- ­
neutral vaccination improved. In the Spanish study, the ICER
lowered to below the threshold when the model incorporated
the effect of gender- ­neutral vaccine on head and neck cancers. [13]
Although assuming this effectiveness is a non- ­evidence- ­based
assumption today, all of the reviewed models included anal
cancer in their base case and most of them penile and head


and neck cancers. This does not correspond to the conservative
approach recommended for evaluations of health technologies,
although it is probable that protection against these cancers in
men will ultimately be demonstrated.
It has been shown that with higher coverage in girls, the
gender- ­neutral strategy becomes less cost- ­effective due to herd
immunity. [6 13 19] Thus, for example, in the Health Information
and Quality Authority (HIQA) model, [6] with a coverage of
90% in both sexes, the gender- ­neutral vaccination is not cost- ­
effective. If the coverage among boys drops to 50%, keeping
girls’ coverage at 90%, the ICER hardly changes; however,
if girls’ coverage drops to 50%, the ICER of gender- ­neutral
strategy falls below the cost- ­effectiveness threshold. Furthermore, the model adapted by the manufacturer for Spain assumed
high coverage in girls (77.8%) and somewhat lower coverage
in boys (55%) and showed that an increase of 20% of the two
coverage rates worsens the cost- ­effectiveness ratio of the gender- ­
neutral vaccine. [13] However, the relationship between the ICER
of gender- ­neutral vaccination and the coverage rate in girls is
not straightforward, as a gender- ­neutral programme can be cost- ­
effective even in countries with a high coverage among girls. [14 17]
On the other hand, the drop in some countries in the uptake in
girls prompted the implementation of campaigns aimed at its
recovery. [30] However, the cost of these campaigns has not been
taken into account in the economic evaluations reviewed. Apart
from the coverage rates, the scenario for the HPV immunisation
decision- ­making has changed in recent years with the inclusion
of other HPV- ­related diseases and the decrease in vaccine prices
and number of doses. A gender- ­neutral strategy would help
build confidence in the vaccine’s efficacy and would improve
the perception that cervical cancer and other cancers are severe
diseases. [29]

Other studies [31] have demonstrated that industry- ­funded
studies report more favourable base- ­case cost- ­effectiveness
values, increasing the probability for a positive recommendation on a health technology. Our review found some differences
in cost parameters between industry- ­funded and non- ­industry- ­
funded studies, namely treatment costs of genital warts, and
vaccine prices. Although genital warts do not represent a
severe health problem nor do they have serious consequences
in health- ­related quality of life, their relatively high incidence,
and therefore many prevented cases in vaccinated men, can have
an important influence on the total costs of compared strategies. Industry- ­funded studies used higher costs of genital warts
treatment, which could have resulted in a more favourable ICER
for gender- ­neutral vaccination. However, differences in vaccine
prices according to sponsorship had the opposite effect.
In any economic evaluation of an immunisation programme,
the price per dose usually has a strong influence on its cost- ­
effectiveness. Tender prices negotiated by the health authorities have dropped from €100 to €30 in the last 10 years for
the 2- ­valent and 4- ­valent vaccines. [10] This means that the negotiated prices can be up to four times lower than those officially listed. In studies included in this review, the price per
two doses plus administration, adjusted to EUR 2017, varies
between €69 and €279, and significantly lower prices were
used in the non- ­industry- ­funded studies, which applied tender
prices with large discounts, while industry- ­funded studies
only use listed prices and no sensitivity analysis is normally
conducted for the vaccine price. [13 15] A clear example of the
influence of this parameter is the Swedish study, [17] in which
the ICER was estimated at €40 000/QALY with the listed price
of the vaccine (€174 per dose), but when the tender price was
used with an 85% discount, gender- ­neutral vaccination was a

dominant option over only- ­girls vaccination (more effective
and less expensive).
In public health, the size of the cohort receiving the intervention is normally large, while the benefits of reduced morbidity
and mortality are relatively small. [32] It is also important to
mention that most of the costs are incurred at the beginning of
the evaluated period, therefore the discount does not influence
them, while the benefits of the immunisation occur many years
later and are therefore greatly affected by the discount rate. [23] In
the UK, the Joint Committee on Vaccination and Immunization
(JCVI) initially spoke out (in June 2016) against the extension of
the HPV vaccine to boys, based on a model with a discount rate
for benefits of 3.5%. [19 33] In July 2018, the JCVI reviewed the
decision and accepted a 1.5% discount, in accordance with the
UK Economic Assessment Guidelines for Public Health Interventions with Long- ­Term Health Benefits. [34] This modification
placed more weight on the clinical benefits and consecutively
improved the cost- ­effectiveness of the gender- ­neutral vaccination, which was finally recommended by the JCVI. [35] However,
this modification in the universally accepted rules was criticised
by some authors as unethical and favouring a politically popular
but not efficient decision. [36] Most of the analysed models used
the recommended discount rate in their country (3%–5%) and
in their sensitivity analyses they decreased the value (0%–1.5%),
showing significant improvements in the ICER in all cases.
All analysed models assumed that the duration of the vaccine’s
effect is lifelong. To date, studies of vaccine efficacy have not
suggested a decrease in protection over time. [6 28] However, the
longest follow- ­up of available clinical trials is 10 years, and in
theory efficacy could decrease after that time. An analysis of
the potential impact of decreased efficacy after 10 years by the
HIQA agency [6] suggests that it would have a limited impact on
the cost- ­effectiveness of gender- ­neutral vaccination. In any case,
even with decreasing efficacy, the protection period coincides
with the maximum risk period for contracting HPV infection.

**Review**

Our search was limited to studies with a two- ­dose schedule
of HPV immunisation; therefore, studies that evaluated a three- ­
dose schedule were excluded. The two- ­dose schedule has been
shown to be as effective as the three- ­dose one, [37] and therefore it has been adopted worldwide. Consequently, economic
evaluations using a three- ­dose schedule are not relevant for
today’s decision- ­making. Another limitation is inherent to the
complexity of evaluated models. In this review, we tried to
compare the values of those parameters that are considered
key for the cost- ­effectiveness of a gender- ­neutral programme.
However, the results depend to a great extent on the model
structure, which cannot be evaluated and compared with other
models without a deep knowledge of the modelling technique
and access to the full code.
In conclusion, the evidence of the cost- ­effectiveness of
extending HPV vaccination to boys with the two- ­dose vaccine
schedule is still limited and the conclusions of existing models
are not unambiguous. However, cost- ­effectiveness cannot be the
only criteria for the decision makers and the inclusion of boys
in HPV vaccination implies reducing gender- ­related inequities. [25]
As the European Centre for Disease Prevention notes, if the goal
of the immunisation programme is to prevent all HPV- ­related
diseases, and not just cervical cancer, then gender- ­neutral vaccination should be an option to consider. [3]
