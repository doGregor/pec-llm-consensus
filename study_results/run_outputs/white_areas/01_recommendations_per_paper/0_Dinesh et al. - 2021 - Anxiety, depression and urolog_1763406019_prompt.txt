# Anxiety, depression and urological cancer outcomes: A systematic review
## Ayushi Anna Dinesh, B.S.c. (Hons) [a], Sofia Helena Pagani Soares Pinto, B.S.c. (Hons) [a], Oliver Brunckhorst, M.B.B.S., B.S.c. (Hons), M.R.C.S [a], Prokar Dasgupta, M.B.B.S, M.S.c., M.D., F.R.C.S., F.E.B.U., F.L.S., F.K.C. [a], Kamran Ahmed, M.B.B.S., P.h.D., F.R.C.S. [a][,][b][,] *

a MRC Centre for Transplantation, Guy’s Hospital Campus, King’s College London, King’s Health Partners, London, United Kingdom
b Department of Urology, King’s College Hospital, London, United Kingdom

Received 30 May 2021; received in revised form 25 July 2021; accepted 5 August 2021

Abstract

Purpose: The interplay between physical and mental aspects of a cancer diagnosis are well recognised. However, little consensus exists
on the impact of depression and anxiety on urological cancer outcomes. Therefore, this systematic review aimed to investigate the relationship between these conditions and functional or oncological outcomes in urological malignancy.
Materials and Methods: A systematic search was conducted using PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO and Global Health databases up to June
2020. Studies evaluating the relationship of anxiety and depression disorders or symptoms on functional and mortality outcomes were included.
Outcome measures included validated urinary, sexual, body image questionnaire scores and all-cause or disease-specific mortality.
Results: Of 3,966 studies screened, 25 studies with a total of 175,047 urological cancer patients were included. Significant anxiety and
depressive symptoms and disorders were found to impact functional outcomes in several cancer types. A consistent negative association
existed for sexual function in prostate, testicular and penile cancer patients. Additionally, poorer urinary function scores were seen in prostate cancer, with increased body image issues in testicular and prostate cancer. Importantly, both overall and disease-specific mortality outcomes were poorer in bladder and prostate cancer patients.
Conclusions: Co-existing depression and anxiety appears to be negatively associated with functional and mortality outcomes in urological cancers. This appears especially evident in male cancers, including prostate and testicular cancer. Although not proving causation, these
findings highlight the importance of considering mental wellbeing during follow-up for early recognition and treatment. However, current
evidence remains heterogenous, with further studies required exploring patients at risk. � 2021 Published by Elsevier Inc.

Keywords: Anxiety; Depression; Urological cancer; Outcomes; Functional; Mortality

sequelae have a large impact on a patient’s physical quality
of life (QoL) [1-3]. Important functional issues encountered
across urological malignancies include urinary, sexual or
bowel dysfunction, and body image issues [4-8]. Beyond
the physical impact, mental wellbeing during treatment and
survivorship is also often impacted [9-12]. Whilst the

2. Methods

This systematic review was conducted according to the
Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and
Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [24] and Synthesis without
Meta-analysis (SWiM) reporting guidelines [25]. The protocol was prospectively registered on PROSPERO
(CRD42020159606).

2.1. Data information sources and search

A broad search of electronic databases including MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, Global Health and APA PsycINFO was conducted from inception to 1 [st] June 2020. A
combination of MeSH and key terms for mental health,
oncological and urological conditions were combined with
the outcomes of interest (Supplementary material 1). A reference review of identified articles and reviews was conducted, with grey literature searched through ongoing
clinical trial registries (WHO International Clinical Trials
Registry Platform and ClinicalTrials.gov) and authors contacted for any preliminary data.

2.2. Eligibility criteria

We considered studies assessing the impact of depression and anxiety disorders or symptoms on functional outcomes, namely urinary or sexual dysfunction, body image
issues and overall or disease-specific mortality in urological
malignancies. Observational study types were included,
except for case reports and case series of fewer than 10
cases per variable or 30 cases overall. For inclusion, a clear
definition of significant anxiety or depressive symptoms or
disorders was required, using either validated psychometric
scales or a formal clinical diagnosis. We placed no restrictions on time since cancer diagnosis.
We excluded studies with no English translation, those
with insufficient data points including conference abstracts,
and interventional studies only considering the effect of a
treatment on mental health. Data points were deemed insufficient if the functional outcomes of interest were assessed using
a patient’s narrative account rather than validated assessment


scales. Data from studies assessing the impact of mental health
conditions on mortality were deemed insufficient if they were
not expressed through risk and hazard ratios. Additionally, we
excluded studies where no urinary malignancy specific information was provided or only systematic syndromes, such as
von Hippel-Lindau syndrome, were studied.

2.3. Study selection

Two reviewers AAD and SPSP independently reviewed
potentially relevant records arising from the search. Following de-duplication, title, abstract and subsequently full text
screening was undertaken against the eligibility criteria. A
third reviewer (OB) evaluated any inconsistencies until
total agreement was achieved.

2.4. Data collection and data items

One reviewer AAD extracted data onto a pre-defined sheet.
Specific information extracted were author, year, study type
and patient demographics. Additionally, mental health specific
information was extracted including time and method of diagnosis of disorder or symptoms. Outcome measures extracted
were divided into functional and mortality measures. Functional outcomes included sexual or urinary dysfunction and
body or self-image issues as measured through validated questionnaires (Supplementary material 2). Mortality was assessed
through all-cause and disease-specific mortality as expressed
through risk and hazard ratios.

2.5. Risk of bias (quality) assessment

Quality assessment of individual studies was completed
using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [26]. The conversion criteria used for converting NOS scores to the Agency
for Healthcare Research and Quality (ARHQ) standards are
presented in Supplementary material 4. The rationale for the
conversion criteria was adapted from previous studies [2729]. Furthermore, the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) for assessment of evidence about prognosis was implemented to
determine the certainty of evidence on an individual outcome
measure basis [30] (Supplementary material 5, Tables 1-3).

3.3. Testicular cancer

Seven studies [45-51], with a combined 4,500 patients,
assessed the impact of depression and anxiety symptoms on
sexual dysfunction and body image in patients with testicular cancer. All these studies included patients that had been
treated for their cancer. They collected information on baseline mental health symptoms and diagnoses after cancer
treatment. Six studies [45-49,51] evaluated the impact of
depressive and anxiety symptoms on erectile dysfunction
and sexual function. Higher depressive symptom load postoperatively was significantly correlated with worsening of
erectile function scores (P = 0.045) [45], which persisted at
three months post-operatively (P <0.05) (moderate certainty of evidence) [51]. Anxiety symptoms were also consistently correlated with worsening sexual function. Three
studies demonstrated increased odds of sexual dysfunction
in those with significant anxiety symptoms (OR = 1.1-6.6,
all P <0.001) (low certainty of evidence) (47-49).
Body image and self-image issues were identified to be
increased in those with significant anxiety and depression
symptoms in two studies (low certainty of evidence)

[49,50]. Significant anxiety symptoms resulted in a worsening of self-image (OR=1.75, P = <0.001) and body image


(P = <0.001) [46]. Similarly, depressive symptoms were
also associated with worse body image scores (P = <0.001)

[50]. One study revealed that depressive and anxiety symptoms were independent predictors of worse body image
scores through regression analyses [50]. No studies were
identified which evaluated the impact of depressive or anxiety symptoms or disorders on either urinary functions or
mortality outcomes in testicular cancer.

3.4. Bladder cancer

Two studies [52,53] with a combined 66,940 treated
patients assessed the impact of mental health on mortality
in bladder cancer. One study collected baseline data on
mental health symptoms and diagnoses prior to cancer diagnosis [53] and the other [52] after cancer treatment. Both
identified significant effects, with depressive disorders and
symptoms associated with increased mortality risk
(HR=1.83, P = 0.024) [52], reduced disease-specific survival (HR=1.20, P <0.01) and reduced overall survival
(HR=1.31, P <0.01) (low certainty of evidence) [53]. Similarly, anxiety disorders were associated with reduced overall survival (HR=1.12, P <0.01) with the same effect when
concurrent depression and anxiety disorders were present
(P <0.01) (low certainty of evidence) [53]. Significant
recent depressive symptoms also had a negative association
with all-cause mortality (HR=1.83, P = 0.024) [52]. No
studies identified evaluated the impact of depression and
anxiety on any functional outcome of interest in bladder

cancer.

3.5. Renal cancer

A single study [54] evaluated the impact of both anxiety
and depression diagnoses on mortality in treated renal cancer in 1,990 patients. Baseline data on mental health diagnoses was collected either prior to or at surgery. No
statistically significant association was identified when
assessing disease-specific mortality (P = 0.42), with only a
trend seen with all-cause mortality (P = 0.065) (low certainty of evidence). No studies were identified that evaluated the impact of mental health on functional outcomes of
interest in renal cancer.

3.7. Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias assessment revealed overall adequate individual study quality. Seventeen studies were rated as ‘good’
quality, six ‘fair’ and two ‘poor’ quality according to
AHRQ standards (Tables 2 and 3). Predominant issues
identified were a lack in comparability criteria, with poor
quality studies particularly lacking in cohort selection.
Prostate cancer studies were overall of good quality, with
adequate comparability and follow-up. With regards to certainty of evidence, four outcomes displayed a moderate certainty, eight outcomes a low certainty and one outcome had
a very low certainty of evidence (Supplementary material
5, Tables 1-3). It is important to note that while individual
studies demonstrated statistically significant results, the certainty of evidence assessment around outcomes ranged
from moderate to very low.

4. Discussion

This systematic review provides an overview of current
literature surrounding the impact of depression and anxiety
on urological cancer outcomes. Current evidence demonstrates that co-existing depression and anxiety symptoms or
disorders have an important impact on both functional and
mortality outcomes in most urological malignancies. Male
cancers, such as prostate and testicular cancers, appear to
be particularly impacted. Sexual function was consistently
negatively associated with both anxiety and depressive
symptoms in these, with body image issues similarly
impacted. Additionally, in prostate cancer urinary function
was worse in those affected by these psychiatric symptoms.
Importantly, both disease-specific and all-cause mortality
outcomes were impacted in those with depressive disorders


in bladder and prostate cancer. Considering the regression
values identified across studies, it appears that the effect
size for this is small to medium. Unfortunately, only scarce
evidence is available evaluating depression and anxiety
effects on renal, bladder and penile cancer. Some evidence
demonstrates the effect of depressive disorders on renal and
bladder cancer mortality, however, there is surprisingly no
significant evaluation on functional outcomes in any of
these malignancies. The studies included in this review
highlight an associative relationship between mental health
and the outcomes of interest (functional outcomes and mortality). Whilst not proving absolute causation due to the
observational design of studies; with the significant impact
mental health issues have on patient’s QoL during survivorship, these findings are important for clinicians and can
guide future research in these cohorts to evaluate the potential links identified.
The significant associations identified could be
explained by a variety of factors. Patients with pre-existing
mental health issues have been found to present at a more
advanced stage compared to the general population [53] or
are more commonly misdiagnosed [56]. Depression is also
associated with other patient factors, including reduced
treatment adherence, smoking, poor diet and physiologic
changes to the cardiovascular system which could contribute to the increased mortality rates [57-60]. Both anxiety
and depression are also known to be associated with physiologic and endocrine dysfunction, impacting both urinary
and sexual function [23,61-64]. Lastly, anxiety and depression are known to be associated with negative body image

[65,66]. In testicular cancer specifically, this could be due
to the reproductive role and the symbolic role in masculinity played by the testes [49,67,68].
Our findings should however be interpreted within the
limitations of currently available data. The existing literature
is profoundly heterogenous in terms of methodology, exact

of the observational design of included studies, it is difficult
to determine an absolute causal relationship between mental
health and cancer-related functional and mortality outcomes,
with a variety of possible confounding factors present. Many
papers included in this review measure mental health symptoms and diagnoses post-treatment. Multiple studies have
shown that the treatment modality can affect mental health
scores [4,6,40,55,69-72]. Unfortunately given the heterogenous methodology used by studies included in this review,
we were unable to stratify results into outcomes for treated
patients followed by outcomes for untreated patients. Additionally, sociodemographic factors such as social support

[50,73], relationship status [48,51], home income [38] and
unemployment [74,75] may play an important role. Furthermore, combining these confounders with the complex relationship that exists between physical and mental health it is
difficult to definitively state the directionality of the relationship identified. The reverse relationship has previously been
demonstrated through deteriorating functional outcomes
leading to poorer mental health [75,76]. Additionally, within
benign pathology in prostatic hyperplasia the relationship
between urinary symptoms and depression has even been
suggested to be bidirectional [77]. Therefore, it is important
to consider these factors when definitively considering the
directionality of the relationship discussed within this review.
While studies demonstrate a statistically significant association, the poor study quality and low certainty of evidence
identified in some penile and testicular cancer studies should
be considered. Their results should be interpreted with caution and as an indication for areas of improvement in future
research.

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review considering the impact of anxiety and depression on urological
outcomes. Despite current limitations in available data, the


findings of this review have important clinical implications.
The significant associations between mental health and
these outcomes highlights the need for increased awareness
of these issues during urological cancer care. A proactive
approach by clinicians is required, especially with patients
reticent about mental health issues, classically men

[69,72,78-80]. Early identification via screening patients
during routine follow-up and referral to appropriate resources for accurate management of any mental health issues
can lead to a better QoL [69,81-83]. This could potentially
also address the increased mortality rates identified in urological cancer patients with mental health issues.
Current weaknesses in the available literature should be

addressed considering many outcomes in this review demonstrated low to very low certainty of evidence. Therefore,
further large well-controlled prospective studies are needed
to carefully assess the impact of mental health on functional
outcomes. A comparator group of patients with mental
health disorders, but no urological malignancies could be
contrasted with participants with mental health disorders
and urological malignancies. This would provide further
assessment of the impact of mental health on functional outcomes and their natural history in patients with mental
health disorders, potentially adding further evidence for the
complex relationship between the two. Additionally, confounding effects of treatment modalities on functional outcomes seen in current studies can be mitigated by
investigating patients undergoing a single treatment. Importantly, current evidence also does not allow for accurate
stratification and comparison of patients by treatment subtypes, particularly in evaluating those who undergo no
treatment, for example active surveillance in prostate cancer, compared to active treatment. This would aid in identifying any variances in the impact of mental health on

functional outcomes in these groups. Further subgroup analyses could consider the severity of mental health disorders
in urological cancer patients and whether this has varying
impacts on functional outcomes. Lastly, given the high incidence rates of bladder and renal cancers and the relative

lack of data it is important to expand research in this group
of urological patients. However, whilst penile cancers
require further evidence, due to its low incidence research
in this area may be more limited to specialist centres given
low patient numbers and a potential lack of research funding.
There is a current lack of evidence in identifying patients
at risk of developing psychological issues [84]. Analysis of
sociodemographic and cancer related potential risk factors
for the development of mental health issues (such as time
since diagnosis and oncological characteristics such as disease stage), would be beneficial. This could allow for the
development of risk prediction models, aiding identification
of at-risk patients who can be targeted for screening and
treatment. Furthermore, anxiety and depression varies over
time in patients with prostate cancer [85]. It would be interesting to investigate whether functional outcomes varied
alongside the patient’s mental health, providing clinicians
with increased insight into the impact of depression and
anxiety on functional outcomes. Fig. 2 highlights the main
points of this review.
Beyond the limitations of the currently available data
this systematic review does have its own limitations.
Despite the comprehensive search strategy and broad sources used, there is the potential for further pertinent articles
to have been missed. Additionally, with limited data available in renal and penile cancers, limited interpretations
could be made in these cohorts. Lastly, a formal statistical
data synthesis of the results via a meta-analysis was unfortunately not possible due to the heterogeneity of data types
and outputs encountered within the literature.

5. Conclusion

Depressive and anxiety symptoms and disorders have
important implications for functional and mortality outcomes in urological cancers. Urinary and sexual function,
and body image issues appear to be negatively impacted,
particularly in prostate and testicular cancer. Additionally,
mortality was negatively impacted in most studies included
in bladder and prostate cancer. These findings reveal a clear
associative relationship between depression, anxiety and
functional and mortality outcomes in many urological
malignancies. However, in view of current study designs
these studies cannot prove a causative association or absolute directionality of the relationship and further appropriately designed studies are required to explore this.
However, these results remain significant, highlighting the
importance of considering the biopsychosocial aspect of
health through early identification of these problems,


allowing for prompt referral for care and potential improvement of outcomes for urological patients.
