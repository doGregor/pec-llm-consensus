**BACKGROUND.** Treatment recommendations of penile cancers are determined primarily by the local extent of the primary tumor. Clinical palpation is used for local staging.

**OBJECTIVE.** We reviewed diagnostic performance of MRI in local staging of penile
cancer in three clinical scenarios (questions [Qs] 1 through 3, Q1–Q3) and one imaging
scenario (Q4). Q1 asked whether MRI reliably distinguishes ≤ T1 from ≥ T2 disease. Q2
asked whether clinical staging reliably identifies ≤ T1 versus ≥ T2 disease and how clinical staging compares to MRI. Q3 asked if MRI is accurate for diagnosis of T3 disease. Q4
asked if artificial erection (by intracavernosal injection of prostaglandin E 1 ) improved accuracy of MRI in T categorization.

**EVIDENCE ACQUISITION.** MEDLINE, EMBASE, and Cochrane Library databases
were searched through September 13, 2021, for studies evaluating local staging of penile
cancer using MRI with surgical pathology as the reference standard. Diagnostic accuracy was calculated using a bivariate random-effects model and hierarchic summary ROC
mode Meta-regression was performed to test for covariate effects of MRI and artificial
erection in Q3 and Q4, respectively.

**EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS.** Eight studies and 481 patients were included. The sensitivity and specificity of MRI for Q1 were 86% (95% CI, 73–94%) and 89% (95% CI, 77–95%),
respectively. AUC for MRI (0.94; 95% CI, 0.92–0.96) did not differ from clinical staging
(0.87; 95% CI, 0.84–0.90; *p* = .83). For Q3, MRI had sensitivity and specificity of 80% (95%
CI, 70–87%) and 96% (95% CI, 85–99%), respectively. For Q4, sensitivity and specificity for
MRI with versus without artificial erection were 85% (95% CI, 71–92%) and 93% (95% CI,
77–98%) versus 86% (95% CI, 68–95%) and 84% (95% CI, 70–93%), respectively ( *p* = .50).

**CONCLUSION.** MRI staging of penile cancer may be considered for ≤ T1 versus ≥ T2
disease but did not appear more accurate than clinical staging. High specificity of MRI
for diagnosis of ≥ T3 disease suggests that MRI may be useful when organ-sparing approaches are planned. MRI with and without artificial erection showed similar accuracy
in local staging.

**CLINICAL IMPACT.** MRI, with or without artificial erection, may be valuable in routine preoperative evaluation of local staging of penile cancer, particularly when organ-sparing options are considered.

Penile cancer is the third most common male-specific cancer with an incidence of 0.1–1 per
100,000 men [1]. Treatment recommendations in major clinical practice guidelines are driven
primarily by the extent of the primary tumor (i.e., clinical T category) [2–4]. The clinical T category is mainly determined by physical examination, in line with international guidelines [4, 5].
However, establishing T category purely by clinical palpation is expectedly limited [6–8]. In a
few studies, sonography has been reported to be better than physical examination, but it has
poor diagnostic accuracy because of limited contrast resolution [9, 10]. MRI has superior contrast resolution and is ideally suited for evaluation of local stage in penile cancer.
The 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM classification

[11] contains revised staging for penile cancers. In the 7th edition, invasion of either corpora cavernosa (CC) or corpus spongiosum (CS) was considered T2 disease, and invasion

To our knowledge, studies evaluating MRI for staging of penile

was to meta-analytically evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of MRI

**Evidence Acquisition**

The study protocol was registered in the International Prospec[tive Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO CRD42020164001).](https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/display_record.php?RecordID=164001)
Best practices for diagnostic accuracy of systematic reviews were
used for conduct and reporting of the study [16, 17]. The ­PRISMA
framework for diagnostic test accuracy was followed [17, 18]. A
multidisciplinary multiinstitutional group with expertise both in
diagnostic accuracy meta-analyses and in management of patients with penile cancer (medical-radiation oncology, urology, pathology, and radiology) drafted the clinical scenarios and
­meta-analysis methodology. T categorization of penile cancer
was based on the revised 8th edition AJCC TNM definitions [11].

***Study Questions***

The study used three questions to evaluate clinical scenarios
with respect to T category thresholds. Question 1 (Q1) asked if MRI
can reliably identify ≤ T1 versus ≥ T2 disease, the latter of which
may warrant concurrent prophylactic inguinal lymph node dissection or dynamic sentinel node biopsy even in patients with nonpalpable lymph nodes. Question 2 (Q2) asked if clinical staging can
reliably identify ≤ T1 versus ≥ T2 disease and whether staging on
MRI is better than clinical staging. Question 3 (Q3) asked whether
MRI is accurate for diagnosis of T3 disease (e.g., tunica albuginea
or CC invasion), which precludes organ-sparing approaches and
warrants penectomy. A fourth question, Q4, was included to identify the optimal technique of penile MRI; it asked whether artificial
erection by intracavernosal injection of prostaglandin E 1 significantly improves the accuracy of MRI in T categorization.

***Search Strategy***

A librarian assisted with searches of MEDLINE, EMBASE, and
Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials without any lan




guage restrictions [19]. Two authors (S.K. and S.W.) independently screened titles and abstracts through September 13, 2021, and
retrieved relevant full texts for inclusion. References from includ
ed studies were reviewed. One author (S.K.) queried clinical trial
registries and conference proceedings (July 2018 to June 2021)
for unpublished studies.

***Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria***

To be included in the meta-analysis, a study needed to involve
penile MRI for local staging of penile cancer in humans using surgical pathology as the reference standard. Data had to be either
provided or retrievable to allow calculation of a 2 × 2 contingency table for a minimum of one study question. Studies with patients who had penile surgery, neoadjuvant chemotherapy, or radiation therapy before penile MRI were excluded, as were studies
classified as case reports, editorials, reviews, systematic reviews,
or commentaries.

***Data Extraction***

Two authors (S.K. and S.W.) independently extracted the following data: first author; country of publication; journal of publication; publication year; recruitment dates; study design; number of patients with each T category; mean age and age range of
patients; lesion size; index test interpretation details (number of
readers, experience); exclusions (study overlap, insufficient test,
no reference standard); imaging technique (field strength, resolution of T2-weighted imaging); gadolinium (administered or
not); DWI (performed or not); artificial erection (performed or
not); definition of positivity; number of true-positive, true-negative, false-positive, and false-negative studies; and reference
standard applied. Disagreements were resolved by consensus.
The 8th edition of the AJCC manual definitions for T categories
were followed [11]. For Q1, true-positive was defined as a positive
index test (invasion of the CC or CS by tumor on MRI) and diagnosis of ≥ T2 disease by the reference standard; true-negative was
defined as a negative index test (CS and CC not invaded by tumor
on MRI) and diagnosis of ≤ T1 disease by the reference standard;
false-positive was defined as a positive index test not confirmed
by the reference standard; and false-negative was defined as a
negative index test disproven by the reference standard. Similar definitions were used for Q2 and Q3 as well as for urethral involvement. In light of the assumption that nondiagnostic tests
occur randomly, they were excluded from the analysis [20]. When

***Data Analysis***

For each study, we extracted 2 × 2 contingency table data
(true-positive, false-negative, false-positive, and true-negative)
by using values directly provided from the included study or by
calculating them from the provided sensitivity and specificity
values. Sensitivity and specificity were pooled using a bivariate
random-effects and hierarchic summary ROC (HSROC) modeling
and visualized using HSROC curves [23]. Heterogeneity was evaluated using several methods. A Cochran *Q* test was performed
with *p* < .05 signifying heterogeneity. A Higgins inconsistency index ( *I* [2] ) test was conducted, and the following criteria for inconsistency index were used to interpret the degree of heterogeneity:
0–40%, heterogeneity might not be important; 30–60%, moderate heterogeneity may be present; 50–90%, substantial heterogeneity may be present; and 75–100%, considerable heterogeneity is present [24]. Meta-regression analysis was performed to
test for covariate effect of MRI in identification of ≤ T1 versus ≥ T2
disease compared with clinical staging and covariate effect of artificial erection with intracavernosal injection of prostaglandin Ecompared with MRI without artificial erection. We also planned 1
to perform subgroup analyses for field strength, value of DWI,
and gadolinium enhancement.
Data extraction was performed using Microsoft Excel (version
2022). We planned to assess publication bias using the Deeks
funnel plot and asymmetry test. However, the test was not performed because the meta-analysis included fewer than 10 studies [25]. Data analysis was done using STATA software (version

**Discussion**

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides diagnostic
accuracy data for three pertinent clinical questions for management of penile cancer and addresses the value of artificial erection for MRI in T categorization of penile cancer.
Our results indicate that MRI is accurate for differentiation
of ≤ T1 from ≥ T2 disease (AUC, 0.94) and may be comparable
with clinical staging (AUC, 0.87), but with overlapping 95% CIs
and no statistically significant difference between MRI and clinical staging. Comparison was limited in particular by the low number of included patients and presence of considerable heterogeneity in the clinical staging group, although the MRI staging
group did not have significant heterogeneity.
Differentiating ≤ T1 from ≥ T2 disease is crucial in the management of penile cancer. For example, presence of T2 disease may
warrant prophylactic inguinal lymph node dissection or dynamic sentinel node biopsy, even in patients with nonpalpable lymph
nodes, rather than staged or delayed lymph node dissection [4,
5]. Studies have reported that early inguinal lymph node dissection may be associated with better cancer control [31, 32]. Therefore, MRI staging may warrant consideration in this scenario either
to routinely complement clinical staging or to serve as an alternative when clinical assessment is equivocal or challenging. MRI
staging is especially important in patients opting for penile preservation. For patients opting for a primary radiation-based therapy approach, lack of pathologic staging makes MRI staging even

more relevant and crucial. There are no clinical practice guidelines
in this clinical scenario, and multicenter prospective studies with
large patient populations are required to confirm our findings.
In our study, MRI was highly specific (96%) for identification of
tunica albuginea/CC invasion (e.g., T3 disease). Presence of CC invasion usually precludes organ-sparing approaches and warrants
complete or partial penectomy depending on the location of the
tumor [4, 5]. The high specificity of MRI for T3 disease supports
the recently published European Association of Urology guidelines, which recommend MRI staging in all patients for whom organ-sparing surgery is planned [4].
We evaluated the need for artificial erection by intracavernosal
injection of prostaglandin E 1 and whether this technique improves the accuracy of MRI for T categorization of penile cancer.
The pooled sensitivities of MRI with and without artificial erection were similar (85% and 86%), and although pooled specificity of MRI with artificial erection (93%) was higher than MRI without artificial erection (84%), we again found overlapping 95% CIs
and no statistically significant difference. AUCs of MRI with and
without artificial erection were similar (0.93 vs 0.92). Overall, our
results indicate that MRI with and without artificial erection may
both be considered as reasonable options for local staging of penile cancer. Institutions may opt to omit artificial erection from
MRI protocols because the added time, expense, and intervention of achieving artificial erection adds no additional value to
MRI evaluation of penile cancer.
We did not have sufficient data to compare MRI technique by
system field strength or use of gadolinium. The accuracy of MRI
for evaluation of small structures, such as the penis, relies on
high spatial resolution. A higher field strength (e.g., 3 T) would
therefore be expected to be superior to lower field strengths.
T2-weighted imaging is considered the fundamental sequence
for local staging of penile cancer [33, 34]. The value of additional parametric sequences in local staging of penile cancer is unknown. Gadolinium-enhanced MRI was used in all of the includ
ed studies, so we could not evaluate the accuracy of unenhanced
MRI for staging purposes. Similarly, the added benefit, if any, of
DWI could not be evaluated. MRI has been suggested to offer additional benefits in preoperative staging by allowing assessment
of vertical tumor depth, pattern of invasion, proximal resection
margin, fat-pad thickness over the pubic symphysis, and residual
penile length [33].
The results of our study add to published results in this domain

[35]. This meta-analysis included the most up-to-date literature
on the topic, excluded an overlapping study (Scardino et al. [14]),
evaluated the role of artificial erection for penile MRI staging, and
addressed multiple pertinent management questions regarding clinical staging versus MRI staging. In addition, we uniformly applied the 8th edition AJCC TNM classification to the older
literature, which helps specifically address some previously unanswered questions related to important clinical and imaging-related issues in the context of the most recent edition.

One of the major limitations of this meta-analysis was the small
number of included studies ( *n* = 8) and the consequent small
number of included patients ( *n* = 481) that had uneven distribution of T categories. However, this situation is expected given the
relatively low incidence of penile cancer and the current lower
utilization rate of penile MRI for staging of penile cancer. Large


multicenter trials comparing staging strategies in a prospective
cohort to confirm our findings would be optimal. The 8th edition
of the AJCC TNM classification emphasizes evaluation of penile
cancers separately in the foreskin, glans, and shaft; however, this
additional criterion could not be separately assessed because
most published studies did not provide this anatomic distinction.
All included studies had an unclear risk of bias in at least one do
main, which could have introduced variability.

**Conclusion**

MRI staging of penile cancer may be considered for distinguishing ≤ T1 from ≥ T2 disease, but it did not outperform clinical
staging. High specificity of MRI for diagnosis of ≥ T3 disease indicates MRI may be particularly useful when organ-sparing surgery
is planned. MRI without artificial erection shows similar accuracy
to MRI with artificial erection. Therefore, institutions may choose
to omit artificial erection in MRI protocols in preoperative local
staging of penile cancer.
