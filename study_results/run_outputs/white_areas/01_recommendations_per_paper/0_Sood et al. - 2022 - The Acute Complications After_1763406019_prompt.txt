## The Acute Complications After Surgery for Penile Carcinoma and Strategies for Their Management: A Systematic Review of the Literature

Akshay Sood [a], Jan K. Rudzinski [a], Philippe E. Spiess [b], Curtis A. Pettaway [a][,] 
a Department of Urology, University of Texas M.D. Anderson Cancer Center, Houston, TX
b Department of Genitourinary Oncology, Moffitt Cancer Center, Tampa, FL

Introduction


Objectives: To investigate the contemporary rates of 30-day complications after surgery for penile cancer and
to discuss the currently used preventative and therapeutic practices aimed at mitigation of these postoperative adverse events.

Data Sources: A systematic review in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews
and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines was performed, and studies reporting on the contemporary rates,
nature, or management of acute complications following primary penile surgery or inguinal lymph node dissection for penile cancer were abstracted. Medline (PubMed) and EMBASE libraries were used to retrieve the
articles published between January 1984 and December 2021 (n = 170 articles). Ultimately, 38 articles were
included. The primary outcome of interest was 30-day (acute) postoperative complications, stratified by
those associated with treatment of the primary penile lesion and those with inguinal lymph node dissection.
Risk of bias assessment was undertaken. Special attention was paid to studies reporting management strategies for these complications.
Conclusion: This comprehensive review revealed that the quality of existing studies reporting on complications is poor and the risk of bias is high. Within these studies, the rates of acute complications following primary penile surgery and inguinal lymph node dissection ranged between 0% and 29.4% and 6% and 90%,
respectively. More than 50% of these complications were wound related. Over the past two decades, several
studies have reported on improved surgical techniques and protocolized postsurgical care pathways.
Although the newer techniques have been associated with improved outcomes, the absolute rates of complications have remained high even in the most contemporary series. Therefore, there is an urgent need for
health care providers and stakeholders to reach consensus regarding preoperative workup and medical optimization goals, stage appropriate therapies, and postoperative care pathways, as has been done for other
malignancies associated with high morbidity.
Implications for Nursing Practice: Penile cancer is a disease of the elderly, and surgical management of the primary lesion or the groins is associated with a high rate of complications. Most complications are wound
related. Meticulous surgical technique and careful postoperative monitoring with early intervention are keys
to mitigating surgery-related morbidity. However, equally important is dissemination and adoption of these
principles by all health care workers universally.

In this article, we provide data from a systematic review of the literature on the contemporary rates, types, and management patterns
of acute (30-day) complications after surgery for penile cancer.

Evidence Acquisition

Search Strategy

The review was performed according to the Preferred Reporting
Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, [7] and the search was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of
Interventions. [8] Medline (PubMed) and EMBASE libraries were
searched to retrieve all contemporary articles published between


January 1984 and December 2021, on complications after surgery for
penile cancer. We used the search terms “((penile cancer) AND (complications)) NOT (inguinal)” to identify articles related to treatment
of the primary penile lesion and “((penile cancer) AND (complications)) AND (inguinal)” to identify articles related to complications
after groin surgery. The search was limited to the title and abstract
fields while running the query, but no language restrictions were
imposed. The search was conducted on January 5, 2022. Reference
lists of the selected papers were scrutinized for additional relevant
material but yielded no additional studies. Independent reviewers
(AS and JKR) screened all abstracts and full-text articles independently. Disagreement was resolved by a third party (CAP).

Types of Study Design and Participants Included

All study designs were included. Eligible studies had to include
patients with penile cancer that had received surgical treatment.
Patients treated with both curative and palliative intent were
included. Patients were not excluded for nonsquamous cell histology
or an advanced stage of penile cancer if they had received surgical
therapy. Similarly, no age restrictions were imposed. However, surgical studies not reporting on complications were excluded. Further,
for studies reporting on acute complications following ILND, we only
selected studies that included more than10 patients. Fig. 1 provides a
PRISMA diagram of the selected studies.

Types of Interventions Included

Interventions studied included surgical treatment of the primary
penile lesion or the groin. The treatment to groin incorporated studies evaluating both an open and a minimally invasive approach and
radical and modified surgical templates. Studies reporting on patients
undergoing radiation therapy alone or nonoperative palliative treatments were excluded.

Types of Outcome Measures Included

The primary outcome of interest was 30-day postoperative complications. These were stratified by the procedure type into those
associated with treatment of the primary penile lesion and those
with groin surgery. The acute complications of interest with regard
to surgical treatment of the primary penile lesion included urinary
tract infection (UTI), seroma or hematoma formation, superficial surgical site infection (SSI), wound dehiscence, meatal stenosis, and graft
loss in studies where split thickness skin grafting (STSG) was performed. The acute complications of interest related to ILND included
SSI, cellulitis, abscess formation, wound dehiscence, skin edge necrosis, flap necrosis, seroma or hematoma, lymphocele, lymphedema,
and lower limb edema. In addition, we evaluated complications not

Assessment of Risk of Bias and Confounders

Risk of bias (RoB) in the included noncomparative series was evaluated independently by two reviewers (AS and JKR). The aim of this

whether study participants were selected consecutively or were representative of a wider patient population, along with attrition bias,

available (indicating a prospective study design). [9]

Evidence Synthesis

Quantity and Quality of Evidence

A total of 170 unique articles were identified by the literature
search. Of these, 50 articles were selected for full-text screening and
ultimately 38 were eligible for inclusion (Fig. 1). All studies were case
series: Seven were prospective and the remainder were retrospective. Systematic reporting of complications was poor with only 11
(29%) out of the 38 examined studies reporting on complications
using the Clavien-Dindo classification. Further, the studies did not
consistently report on the specific timeline for development of these
postoperative complications, making it difficult to assess the true
acuity of these adverse events. Therefore, overall, in all included studies, the RoB was high (Fig. 2).

Acute Complications After Treatment of Primary Penile Cancer Lesion

Table 1 provides details on studies reporting acute complications
after surgical treatment of the primary penile lesion (n = 10 studies).
Two studies assessed complications after partial or total penectomy,
and the remaining eight evaluated complications in patients undergoing organ sparing surgery (OSS) for early-stage penile cancer.
The overall 30-day complication rate in patients undergoing partial or total penectomy was »19%. [5][,][10] A vast majority of these complications were wound related, either surgical site infections or
dehiscence. Patients undergoing glansectomy with STSG OSS had the
highest rates of 30-day complications, ranging between »7.5% and
30%, [11-15] while patients undergoing glans resurfacing OSS had the
lowest rates, ranging between »0% and 15%. [16-18] Partial graft loss (in
patients undergoing OSS with STSG application), meatal stenosis (in
patients undergoing glansectomy), and SSIs were the top three most
common complications in patients undergoing OSS. Other complications such as UTIs, hematomas, seromas, and systemic complications
including DVT/PE, MI, pneumonia, renal failure, and so on were rare,

Table 2 provides a comprehensive summary of studies reporting
on acute complications following ILND (n = 28 studies); the table is
stratified into studies reporting on acute complications following radical or modified techniques of ILND. Eight studies reported on complications after open radical ILND, [19-26] two studies reported on
complications after either open modified or radical ILND, [27][,][28] and six

Following management of the primary penile lesion, bilateral
ILND is considered one of the standard-of-care options for patients
that are clinically node negative but have high risk primary tumors


(�pT1b, N0) or among patients with palpable or visible disease subsequent to cross sectional imaging (pTany, N1-3). [4] ILND has been
shown to aid in appropriate staging and improve survival in patients
with penile cancer. The lymphatic drainage of the penis tends follow
a sequential pattern starting with superficial inguinal nodes, to deep
inguinal nodes, pelvic nodes, and retroperitoneal nodes with bilateral
involvement in up to 80% of cases. [6] Postoperative complications following ILND are broadly classified into major and minor. Major complications include those requiring hospital admission, operative care
under general anesthesia, or are potentially life-threatening such as
skin flap necrosis requiring a graft, sepsis, DVT/PE, and so on. On the
other hand, minor complications are those managed on an outpatient
basis, without the need for general anesthesia, or are not life-threatening or debilitating and include seroma formation, lymphocele, skin
edge necrosis, lower limb edema, and local wound issues requiring
debridement under topical anesthesia. Overall, reporting of postoperative complications following ILND was poor with only 8 out of 28
studies using the Clavien-Dindo classification system.
Radical ILND is considered the oncologic gold standard and
involves resection of nodal tissue defined by the following anatomic
borders in the “classical description”: anterior superior iliac spine
superiorly, pubic tubercle superomedially, the adductor longus medially, and the sartorius muscle laterally with ligation of greater saphenous vein and complete excision of lymph nodes over the femoral
vessels. [19-28] Furthermore, the radical template also entails dissection
of both superficial and deep inguinal nodes. [19][,][20] As demonstrated in
Table 2, this surgical approach is associated with high rates of postoperative complications ranging from 30% to 80% in series published
between 1984 and 2013. [19-28] Most of the reported complications are
wound related and include development of surgical site infection/cellulitis, skin edge necrosis, flap necrosis, development of seroma, lymphoceles, and lymphedema.
To minimize the morbidity associated with the radical ILND, modified ILND techniques have been proposed to limit the size of incision,
which excludes nodal dissection lateral to femoral artery (medial dissection technique) and distal to fossa ovalis, preservation of greater
saphenous vein, and elimination of Sartorius muscle flap. [6][,][29-33] The
modified technique tends to be used for treatment of patients with
high-risk primary penile cancer with clinically negative groins. As
noted in Table 2, studies reporting on modified ILND suggest overall
reduction in rates of postoperative wound complications compared
to radical ILND, ranging from 6.8% to 33%. [6][,][29-33] More specifically, the
modified template is associated with lower SSI rates, seroma rates,
and skin edge necrosis. However, the modified technique has been
reported in some series to have higher rates of recurrences. [23][,][29] Due
to this, our group and other authors have suggested expansion of the
modified template to include lymph nodes from midpoint of adductor longus to lateral boundary of sartorius muscle with intraoperative
frozen section to further help guide the extent of ILND. This technique is often described as the superficial ILND. [6][,][28][,][43][,][44] A more recent
expansion of this approach is the fascial-sparing dissection wherein
nodal tissue from within fossa ovalis is also removed. [27][,][31-33][,][41][,][45]

Further attempts to minimize the morbidity associated with open
ILND have been proposed through adoption of minimally invasive
platform with laparoscopic and robotic approaches to groin dissections. [34-45] As shown in Table 2, laparoscopic technique is associated

preservation has been suggested to reduce incidence of acute lymphedema. More contemporary robotic ILND series have demonstrated
shorter hospital stays, lower rates of flap necrosis, and lower rates of

Principles of Surgery and Perioperative Care to Reduce Acute
Postoperative Complications

Measures that may be undertaken to reduce complications in
patients undergoing surgery for penile cancer can be divided into
two broad categories: intraoperative and perioperative.
Meticulous surgical dissection and attention to detail are the key
tenets for avoiding postsurgical complications. Likewise, following
recommendations proposed by the American Urological Association
(AUA) [46] and the Surgical Care Improvement Program (SCIP) [47] for
antimicrobial prophylaxis are important in reducing the incidence of
SSIs, the leading cause of morbidity after penile cancer surgery. Specifically, intraoperatively, one must ensure: (1) atraumatic handling
of anatomic structures to minimize tissue devitalization; (2) careful
control of lymphatics and small vessels (ie, clips, ties), especially during inguinal surgery, to decrease the odds of formation of lymphoceles, hematomas, seromas, and other fluid collections; (3) judicious
use of closed surgical suction drains; (4) use of incisions that follow
the natural skin creases (lines of Langerhans) to access the inguinal
nodes, and avoid S- and T-shaped incisions; (5) creation of skin flap
underneath the Scarpa fascia for ILND to preserve the dermal microvasculature; (6) covering of the inguinal node dissection site with
myocutaneous flaps when primary closure may be associated with
excess wound tension or when prior radiotherapy has been used;
and (7) performance of risk-stratified, patient-tailored surgical dissections such as modified superficial or fascial-sparing ILND or
dynamic sentinel node biopsy (DSNB) or utilization of novel surgical
technologies such as the laparoscopic or robotic platforms.
Measures that may be undertaken perioperatively: (1) for primary
penile surgery include the use of compressive surgical dressings that
stay on for 2-7 days and help reduce postoperative swelling and
hematoma; (2) for ILND include the use of compression stockings to
minimize development of DVT/PEs and lymphoceles and daily measurement of “ins/outs” with stripping of drains to avoid general fluid
overload and local fluid collections; and (3) for both surgeries include
the appropriate use of antibiotics with administration of the first
dose within 1 hour of the surgical incision and then continuation
(either intravenously or orally) either for 24 hours for penile surgery
or until removal of surgical drains for ILND, use of DVT prophylaxis
and early ambulation, performance of simple measures such as
demarcation or tracking of areas of skin erythema or discoloration
using a skin marker for early diagnosis and treatment-response
assessment in patients with or at-risk for SSIs, vascular compromise,
or expanding hematomas, early removal of the Foley catheter (typically by postoperative day 2 for ILND patients, however, may be kept
in longer depending on the patients’ baseline voiding status [ie, history of (h/o) significant lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), retention] and typically about 7 days for patients with primary penile
surgery because these patients frequently undergo neomeatus creation or STSG application where early handling of the penis by the
patient can jeopardize healing, and finally, optimization of nutritional
support in patients that have low albumin or are in poor health.

Medi-honey or acetic-acid, and early mechanical debridement as
indicated. Integrating a wound care specialist early on may further
facilitate optimization of local wound care. Although there are no
existing data on this subject in patients with penile cancer specifically, the role of multidisciplinary wound care teams is well established for surgeries that are associated with high rates of
postoperative wound complications. [48][,][49] Meatal stenosis is usually a
more delayed complication and may be managed with self-catheterization or may require a formal revision of the neomeatus in the operative room. Finally, most graft losses are partial (>95%) and are
managed with observation. Total graft loss is managed with wet-todry dressing changes with delayed regrafting.
Complications are much more common after inguinal surgery and
are of more serious nature. Prevention and early recognition of groin
complications is of paramount importance in the early postoperative
setting. SSI can be prevented by proper sterilization of the surgical
field prior to skin incision along with use of prophylactic antibiotics
with coverage against gram-negative rods, Staphylococcus and
Peptostreptococcus. [6][,][50] Postoperatively, consideration should be given
to keeping the surgical site clean and dry with maintenance of antibiotics such as first-generation cephalosporins and penicillin until
drains are removed. [6] In patients with large body habitus with pannus
extending to the groins, placement of dry gauze (“Interdry”) in the
groin can prevent buildup of moisture and fungal colonization.
Infected inguinal wounds should be managed with meticulous
wound care, daily wound inspection to ensure a healthy pink wound
base, and culture-specific antibiotics.
Prevention of wound dehiscence, skin edge necrosis, and flap
necrosis can be accomplished by avoiding excess wound tension,
debridement of devitalized tissue, development of thick tissue flaps
below Scarpa fascia, and utilization of myocutaneous flaps to cover
exposed vessels or large groin defects. Following inguinal reconstruction with flaps, patient mobilization may be limited for 48-72 hours
to ensure adequate blood supply to the surgical site. Use of wound
vacuum-assisted closure (VAC) devices has been promoted in inguinal surgical procedures; however, Schmid et al [51] recently conducted
a prospective randomized trial comparing conventional wound care
to epidermal vacuum dressing following ILND and found no significant difference in wound dehiscence and SSI rates. However, the trial
was limited by its small sample size of 31 patients. Further, more
than 90% of patients were treated with DSNB rather than ILND. Lastly,
the primary endpoint was daily lymphorrhea output (as a continuous
variable), which left the study underpowered to detect more meaningful endpoints such as complications, hospital stay, reintervention,
or readmission. Hence, for now use of wound VACs remains in practice as large, randomized trials and meta-analysis from other surgical
specialties support its use. [52] Other factors that should be given consideration in the early postoperative setting include assistance in
ambulation to prevent excessive straining and wound tension. Management of flap necrosis and groin wound dehiscence once it happens is typically wet-to-dry dressings or VAC dressing (special
vascular dressings) followed by plastic surgery closure in a delayed
fashion or allowing the wound to heal via secondary intention.
Postoperative seromas, hematomas, and lymphoceles may be prevented by meticulous surgical dissection of blood vessels and lymphatics, as well as insertion of closed suction drain to avoid excessive
fluid collection. The inguinal drain output should be closely monitored for output and consistency and maintained until drainage is
<30-50 mL per 24 hours. [6] In some cases, patients may need to be
educated on proper maintenance because they may be discharged
home with drains in place. As these patients are high risk for developing infections, utilization of chlorhexidine impregnated disc at the
drain exit site may be an avenue to reduce infection rates as has been
shown in randomized controlled trials. [53] In cases with excessive fluid

collection on imaging following drain removal, image-guided drain


placement with later lymphatic embolization [54] or operative exploration may be required in refractory cases.
Lower extremity lymphedema can be minimized in part by saphenous-vein sparing and prevention of SSI, early use of fitted compression devices, and a preemptive referral to lymphedema specialist. [6]

ILND is considered a high-risk procedure for development of DVT/PE.
Strategies such as use of perioperative sequential compression devices, administration of prophylactic low-molecular-weight heparin,
direct oral anticoagulant agents use (associated with better adherence compared to injectable anticoagulants), and early ambulation
can help mitigate the risk in this patient population. [6]

Conclusion

Penile cancer is a disease of the elderly with multiple comorbidities, its surgical management is associated with high rates of acute
postoperative complications. The rates of 30-day complications following primary penile surgery and ILND range from 0% to 29.4% and
6% to 90%, respectively. A vast majority of these complications are
wound related. Among patients undergoing groin surgery, radical
open ILND is associated with highest rates of complications, whereas
modified and minimally invasive approaches appear to be associated
with lower postoperative morbidity. Expert surgery, and early recognition and timely treatment of complications is of paramount importance in management of this patient population. Although steady
advances have been made in surgical techniques and protocolized
postsurgical care pathways over the past 20 years, the rates of complications after penile surgery are high, and have remained so, even
in the most contemporary series. This suggests an urgent need for
health care providers and stakeholders to reach consensus regarding
preoperative workup and medical optimization goals, stage-based
surgical treatment options, and postoperative care pathways for
these patients.
