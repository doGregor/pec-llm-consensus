  Surgery and chemotherapy are common approaches for penile metastases (PMs).

  Radiotherapy in PMs is used mainly in palliative setting.

Novel Insights

  Stereotactic body radiotherapy provides good outcomes in terms of symptomatic relief and local
control.

  A personalized approach should be tailored in a multidisciplinary team to provide the best
treatment option.

presentation mainly related to advanced stages of disease.
Considering the low incidence, an optimal treatment approach has not yet been defined; surgery, chemotherapy,
and radiotherapy (RT) are different options used in the vast
majority with palliative intent. The advances in modern RT
can represent an innovative tool in PM management and a
curative option. This paper aimed to report the case of a PM

Introduction

In the landscape of male genitourinary cancer sites,
one of the most enigmatic and rarely discussed challenges
is the occurrence of penile metastasis (PM). While primary tumors and regional lymph nodes have a higher
incidence, the occurrence of male genitalia metastasis is
infrequent [1, 2].
PM clinical presentation is mainly related to advanced
stages of disease. The main symptoms referable to PM are
priapism associated with pain, obstructive urinary outflow, and, less frequently, superficial skin involvement.
The treatment of such a condition is mainly related to


symptom palliation [3]. The treatment of this particularly
complex and intricate facet of male cancer has been
investigated with surgery and a systemic therapy approach; the potential role of radiotherapy (RT) has also
been investigated [4].
Technological advancements in RT could offer a new
tool in this pathway, providing a tailored solution to
improve symptoms, enhance the quality of life, and extend survival in a curative scenario. Indeed, the advent of
stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), with the evidence
of feasibility and good clinical outcomes in different
subsites, represents an alternative and accurate treatment
option [5, 6]. This review aimed to provide an update on
the available evidence on the role of RT in PM primary
treatment after the description of a case of PM after
primary surgery for a urothelial malignancy undergoing
SBRT in our institution.

Case Report

In November 2021, an 80-year-old Caucasian male with fair
general clinical conditions and a medical history of arterial hypertension came to our attention. Three months earlier, he had
undergone radical cysto-prostatectomy with bilateral ureterocutaneostomy packing and bilateral obturator iliac lymphadenectomy for bladder cancer. At histopathology, a high-grade
nonpapillary urothelial carcinoma infiltrating prostate parenchyma by contiguity, with lymphatic invasion and perineural
invasion, was shown with pT4a pN2(12/13) stage. The patient
underwent radiological examinations with neck, chest, and abdomen contrast-enhanced computed tomography that showed
several adenopathies at the aortoiliac bifurcation with a maximum
size of 2 cm, while the bone scan resulted negative for secondaries.
The referring medical oncologist did not indicate chemotherapy due to heart failure and the patient’s advanced age.
Following a multidisciplinary discussion, the patient was addressed to our department. After clinical examination, his performance status was grade 1 on the Eastern Cooperative Oncology
Group (ECOG) scale. The patient reported hematuria and
stranguria as the main symptoms, and no palpable lesions were
found at physical examination. Considering the pathological
staging, adjuvant RT was prescribed, and the patient was scheduled
for a simulation with 18-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission
tomography (PET). Indeed, the [ [18] F]FDG PET/CT showed
pathological uptakes in the abdominal-pelvic lymph node region,
namely in bilateral common iliac, left para-aortic, and interaortocaval sites, including an area of focal tracer accumulation at
the root of the penile shaft, suspected for metastases. The final
decision was to carry out adjuvant external beam RT on the tumoral bed, pelvic and lumbar-aortic lymph nodes, and FDGpositive lymphadenopathies and to perform a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the abdomen to clarify the suspected
secondary penile lesion.
The patient’s treatment setup included a supine position,
with arms crossed on his chest and legs immobilized with a
device that provides suitable positioning for irradiation of the
pelvic region, Combifix™ (CIVCO, Iowa), and the slices were
acquired at a thickness of 2.5 mm. The patient was treated on
Linac Synergy [®] (Elekta, Stockholm, Sweden). This Linac has an

Meanwhile, the requested MRI confirmed the presence of a
secondary lesion (maximum diameter of 2.1 cm) at the root of the
right corpus cavernosum of the penis. Following a department
discussion, an indication of SBRT was given, and following the end
of phase 1 RT treatment, the patient underwent a new CT simulation. The setup included a supine position, with arms crossed
on his head, and immobilization with a thermoplastic mask for the
pelvic area, Pelvicast™ (Orfit, Belgium), and the slices were acquired at a thickness of 2.5 mm. In order to perform the treatment
plan, a fusion between the images of the CT simulation and the
images of the MRI was performed. The gross tumor volume was
delineated according to the MRI scan, and a 0.3-cm isotropic
margin was applied to obtain PTV.
The prescribed dose was 25 Gy in 5 sessions of 5 Gy, and we
implemented a planned sum with the phase 1 plan. A coverage of
99% on GTV 25Gy and 98% on PTV 25Gy was obtained from 98% of
the prescribed dose. At the beginning of the treatment, we instructed the patient with hygienic-sanitary indications (white and
cotton linen, mild detergents). Both treatments were completed
without interruptions, and the patient did not experience any
discomfort.
At interim [ [18] F]FDG PET/CT 45 days, a complete metabolic
response was shown with no evidence of residual symptoms such
as hematuria and back pain. Furthermore, another [ [18] F]FDG PET/
CT was performed 12 weeks after the end of treatment with no
evidence of pelvic recurrence but a suspected uptake in the
sternum body and right latero-cervical nodal district. A few weeks
later, the patient was admitted to the ward for bowel occlusion
leading to exitus.

Systematic Literature Review

A database search using definite keywords was carried
out following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [7, 8]. A systematic Embase and PubMed/
MEDLINE search was undergone using the following
search strategy: [(“penile metastasis”/exp OR “penile
metastasis” OR (penile AND (“metastasis”/exp OR metastasis))) AND (“radiotherapy”/exp OR radiotherapy)].
Only original articles describing RT in PM management were selected; papers published until October 24,
2023 were included in this analysis. The exclusion criteria
were (1) articles not in English, (2) not original articles
(e.g., editorials, letters, congress communications or
posters, guidelines, book chapters), (3) papers not re

ferred to PM (e.g., primary penile cancer, nodal metastasis), (4) papers not referred to RT (e.g., surgery,
chemotherapy).
All the papers were selected and analyzed by a board of
3 radiation oncologists, followed by an independent
validation of 12 experts in urogenital cancer management
(2 radiation oncologists, three medical oncologists, one
radiologist, five urologists, and one nuclear medicine
physician). Three independent expert radiation oncologists analyzed the search strategies and the whole process.

Results

Overall, 174 studies were obtained using the mentioned search strategy; one record was removed after
screening for duplicates. According to selection criterion
1, a total of 34 papers were excluded; following title and
abstract analysis, ten articles were excluded according to
selection criterion 2.
After full-text analysis, 54 papers were discarded according to selection criterion 3 as not referring to PMs.
Also, 60 articles were excluded as not referring to RT
management of PM. After completing the selection
process, 15 papers were considered eligible for results
analysis. Figure 1 reports the flowchart of the systematic
literature search process.
The RT intent reported in the selected papers was in
the vast majority of symptom palliation, with 14 papers
(94%) and only one report (6%) considering adjuvant RT
with 30 Gy [9]. The treatment was performed 1 month
after surgery to prevent exophytic recurrence.
Considering the location of the primary tumor, 40% of
the experiences (6 articles) referred to metastasis from
rectal cancer. A case of RT treatment for metastatic penile
localization was described by Appu et al. [10] with 25 Gy
in a 10-fraction course delivered to recurrent rectal cancer
and penile metastatic localizations.
A similar dose was administered in the case by Park
et al., with 26 Gy delivered in 13 fractions [11]. Glans
metastasis treatment was described in a case series by
Nunes et al. [12], delivering 30 Gy in a 10-fraction RT
course with concomitant capecitabine. No side effects were
reported, and the patient showed a complete response at 1year follow-up. A 50.4 Gy in 28-fraction RT course with
concurrent oral capecitabine was administered to an anorectum adenocarcinoma with penile localization in the
experience by James et al. [13]. No response to the
treatment was shown at clinical evaluation, and the patient
needed analgesic therapy to control the progression of
symptoms. In the setting of local control, Weng et al. [14]
reported a case of PM from rectal cancer admitted to the
RT course of 60 Gy in 30 fractions early interrupted due to
grade 3 dermatitis (total dose = 44 Gy) reaching, however,
an overall good clinical response.

Discussion

PMs represent a rare disease appearance whose
treatment has mostly been relegated to symptom management. The paucity of prospective evidence or large
cohorts did not allow the identification of an optimal
therapeutic strategy, or different approaches comparison.
In this context, the role of RT was mainly analyzed in a
palliative setting with conventional dosages and fractionations, and most experiences reported treatment
doses between 25 and 42 Gy with several fractions be
– –
tween 10 and 14 [9 12, 16 19, 23].
Higher total treatment doses have been reported in
limited experiences. While increasing the total dose up to
56–60 Gy in conventional fractionation resulted in reasonable symptom control in three experiences [15, 22,
24], in the case report by James et al. [13] the 50.4-Gy
course in 28 fractions did not show symptomatic benefit.
A wide heterogeneity of RT doses administered has been
reported in the selected papers. A symptomatic relief has
been reported also with palliative doses (30 Gy in 10
fractions); the possibility of pursuing also a radical intent
should lead to higher dose administration considering
organs at risk constraints.
In treating metastatic disease, SBRT has proven to be a
valuable approach in different clinical presentations and
with different intents, even in combination with systemic


–
therapies [25 27]. SBRT could represent an alternative
option to surgery also considered the impact of RT
treatment in terms of organ preservation and hospitalization even if no data of comparison in terms of clinical
outcomes are reported in the literature. Considering the
metastatic setting and the necessity to admit patients as
soon as possible to systemic therapies, SBRT could reduce
the time interval to chemotherapy. In the frame of a
personalized approach, the clinical option should be
evaluated in a multidisciplinary team.
Our experience represents the first in the literature to
investigate the application of SBRT in secondary penile
localizations, showing its feasibility in the absence of
toxicity. However, the patient’s death for a noncancerrelated cause did not allow for long-term evaluation of the
curative intent of the treatment and symptomatic management, but the first instrumental evaluation reported a
complete metabolic response and the disappearance of
symptoms.

Conclusion

The literature regarding PM treatment is closely related to a palliation context. In the context of modern
management of oligometastatic disease, SBRT could also
be a curative or at least a prolonged local control
