Introduction

Penile cancer typically metastasises in a sequential pattern
through the lymphatic system, beginning with the superficial


inguinal lymph nodes (LNs), deep inguinal LNs progressing
to the pelvic LNs, and eventually reaching distant sites [1].
Fewer than 5% of patients present with distant metastases,
and such cases usually involve regional LNs. The presence

and extent of LN metastases are crucial prognostic factors for
penile cancer survival, with 5-year cancer-specific survival
rates of ~95%, 80%, 65%, and 35% for patients with N0, N1,
N2, and N3 disease, respectively [2,3].

In patients with clinically LN-negative groins (cN0), where no
suspicious palpable LNs are detected, evidence from a
non-randomised controlled trial (RCT) indicates that early
LN surgery significantly improves outcomes. Specifically, a
3-year survival rate of 84% was observed in those undergoing
early LN surgery, compared to 35% in those who received
delayed surgery. The incidence of extranodal extension was
20% in the early surgery group, compared to 95% in the
delayed group [4]. Consequently, early detection of lymphatic
spread is vital in the management of penile cancer. Given
that the inguinal LNs are typically the first site of
dissemination, initial staging efforts focus on identifying
(micro)metastatic disease in these LNs, with imaging for
distant metastases reserved for patients with clinically
LN-positive disease [1].

Open inguinal lymphadenectomy (OIL) is considered the
‘gold standard’ for managing LN metastasis in penile cancer

[5]. However, this procedure is associated with high
morbidity, including skin necrosis and postoperative surgical
wound infections. Additionally, the extent of IL can result in
higher incidences of lymphocele, lymphorrhoea, lower limb
and genital lymphoedema [6].

In 2006, Tobias-Machado et al. [7] introduced video
endoscopic Inguinal Lymphadenectomy (VEIL) to urological
clinical practice to reduce morbidity while maintaining the
oncological principles of the conventional technique [8]. Since
its introduction, several retrospective studies have reported
promising results. This study is the first meta-analysis using
only prospective studies to elucidate better whether VEIL
offers advantages in perioperative outcomes compared to OIL
in patients with penile cancer.

Methods

Literature Search

The study was conducted on June 2024, in compliance with
the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and
Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [9] statement (Data S1) and
registered in the International Prospective Register of
Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database (Centre for
Reviews and Dissemination [CRD] identifier 42024543081). A
research question was established based on the Patient –
Intervention – Comparator – Outcome – Study design
(PICOS) criteria, which is as follows: which IL for penis
cancer (squamous cell carcinoma [SCC]) has fewer
postoperative complications: a video endoscopic or open
approach?

Study Screening and Selection

Two independent authors (C.V.S and R.D.L) screened all
retrieved records. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion.
The full text of the screened papers was evaluated if found to
be relevant to the present review.

The PICOS Framework

The included studies met the following criteria: patients (P)
with penile SCC underwent IL; the intervention (I) tested was
VEIL; (C) the comparator was the OIL; the primary outcome
(O) evaluated was perioperative complications, and were
selected only (S) prospective studies.

Data Extraction and Endpoints

All variables were extracted into a Microsoft Excel (Microsoft,
Redmond, WA, USA) spreadsheet for analysis and verified by
another author (C.V.S. and R.D.L.). The mean and SD were
documented for the continuous variables obtained from the

included studies. For variables reported as median and

interquartile range (IQR), the original data were converted to
mean and SD [10].

The recorded variables included the follow-up duration of the
studies, demographics data, TNM stage, surgical technical
data, perioperative complications details, and hospital stay.
The postoperative complications were stratified by the
Clavien–Dindo classification [11]. Minor complications were
defined as Clavien–Dindo Grades I and II, while major
complications were defined as Clavien–Dindo Grades III–V.
Specific perioperative complications were recorded as
dichotomous variables, including wound infection,
lymphorrhoea, lymphoedema, lymph collection, haematoma,
skin necrosis, deep venous thrombosis, and pulmonary
embolism. Intraoperative and oncological outcomes were
recorded as continuous variables, including operative time,
number of LNs retrieved, number of positive LNs, period
with drain, recurrence after surgery, and overall survival.

Quality Assessment and Risk of Bias

Prospective observational studies were appraised with the
Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [12]. In this scale, studies are
scored on a 0–9 scale according to the quality of patient
selection, comparability of groups, and adjudication of
outcomes. Quality assessment of RCTs was performed using
the Cochrane Collaboration tool for assessing the risk of bias
in randomised trials (Risk of Bias 2 [ROB2]), in which
studies are scored as ‘high’, ‘low’, or ‘unclear’ risk of bias in
five domains: selection, performance, detection, attrition, and
reporting biases [13]. Publication bias was investigated by
funnel-plot analysis of point estimates according to study
weights and by Egger’s regression test.

Statistical Analysis

This systematic review with meta-analysis followed the
Cochrane Collaboration and the PRISMA statement
guidelines [13]. Odds ratios with 95% CIs were used to
compare treatment effects for categorical endpoints.
Cochran’s Q test and I [2] statistics were used to assess for
heterogeneity; P values inferior to 0.10, and I [2] >25% were
considered significant for heterogeneity. We used a fixedeffect model for outcomes with low heterogeneity (I [2] <25%).
Otherwise, a DerSimonian and Laird random-effects model
was used. The meta-analyses were performed with Review
Manager 5.4 (Cochrane Centre, The Cochrane Collaboration,
Odense, Denmark).

Surgery

The OIL technique was similar across all studies, and all
procedures were performed by surgeons experienced in the
management of penile cancer. The surgical procedure
included the following steps: a transversal inguinal incision of

~
7 cm in length was made 1 cm below the inguinal ligament;
Camper’s fascia was incised; superficial LN stations between
Camper’s fascia and the fascia lata were dissected; and deep
LN packages were isolated and excised at the level of the
fossa ovalis [14–17].

The VEIL technique remained consistent across all studies,
with the camera trocar placed at the apex of Scarpa’s triangle.
For the manipulation trocars, Tobias-Machado et al. [15],
Yadav et al. [16], and Kumar et al. [17] studies used one
5-mm trocar and one 12-mm trocar, while Falcone et al. [14]

Oncological Outcomes

Tobias-Machado et al. [15] reported a mean follow-up of
18.7 months, Yadav et al. [16] of 14 months, and Falcone

postoperatively due to metastasis in the shoulder, and another
died 1 year after surgery due to visceral metastasis.

Discussion

This meta-analysis compared VEIL and OIL in adult patients
with penile cancer (SCC) and included four prospective
studies (three RCTs) with a total of 95 patients and 174
operated limbs. The analysis demonstrates that laparoscopic
surgery is associated with fewer postoperative complications.
To our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis conducted
exclusively with prospective studies, which is reflected in the
low level of heterogeneity in the evaluated complications.

Penile cancer is relatively rare in industrialised nations, with
incidence rates of ~0.94 per 100 000 men in Europe and 0.5
per 100 000 in the USA [18]. However, the prevalence is
significantly higher in certain regions, such as South America,
Southeast Asia, and parts of Africa, where penile cancer may
represent 1–2% of all male malignancies [19,20]. Due to its
rare manifestation, conducting RCTs presents a logistical
challenge for oncology institutions. Consequently, to date,
there are only three RCTs and six retrospective cohort studies
in English that compare OIL with VEIL for penile cancer

[14–19]. The two previous meta-analyses [21,22] combined
both prospective and retrospective studies, resulting in high
heterogeneity, requiring subgroup analyses to address the
high heterogeneity among the studies. Additionally, the
databases searched in these prior meta-analyses were more
restricted compared to the present study.

The surgical technique initially described by Tobias-Machado
et al. [7] has remained consistent among all authors with
minimal variations. However, the initially reported operative
time for VEIL was 126 min compared to 92 min for OIL

[8,17]. In recent studies, the operative times have been
equalised, which can be attributed to the learning curve
associated with laparoscopy [15,16].

An interesting trend noted in this review is the reduction in
insufflation pressure. Initially, Tobias-Machado et al. [15]
used a pressure of 15 mmHg, which was reduced to
12 mmHg in the studies by Kumar et al. [17] and Yadav
et al. [16] and further reduced to 10 mmHg in the recent
study of Falcone et al. [14]. To the best of our knowledge, no
prospective study has compared different insufflation
pressures in the IL. However, there is a trend towards using
lower insufflation pressures in other procedures, such as
radical prostatectomy, to improve tissue perfusion and reduce
thromboembolic events. This rationale could be applied to IL,
potentially improving tissue perfusion and reducing
postoperative ischaemic injuries. Nevertheless, more studies
are needed to understand the influence of insufflation
pressure in this specific type of surgery [23,24].

In previous studies, the drainage time was not significantly
different between the groups [21,22]. However, despite the
distinct protocols for drain removal, the absolute comparison
between the groups and the similar study design in the
present review allowed us to ascertain that the drainage time
appears shorter when using VEIL compared to OIL. The
shorter drainage time, lower intensity and duration of pain,
and fewer complications may be related to this difference in
hospital stay duration.

The two previous systematic reviews described shorter
hospital stays in patients undergoing VEIL, although the high
heterogeneity index makes the results uncertain. The
homogeneity in the trial designs in the present study resulted
in similar findings with a low heterogeneity index [21,22].

Postoperative pain was assessed using various instruments
across studies, but the comparison indicates a reduction in
the intensity and duration of pain in patients undergoing
VEIL compared to those undergoing OIL. This reduction in
pain may be attributed to the smaller incisions and less
tissue traction associated with VEIL. This is the first
meta-analysis to compare postoperative pain evaluations in
IL, highlighting a significant gap in the literature. Future
studies should employ standardised and validated pain
assessment tools to improve the evaluation and make more
robust comparisons.

Wound infection, skin necrosis, and lymphoedema are the
most common postoperative complications associated with IL

[1,25,26]. The incidence of skin necrosis in the literature
ranges from 16.7% to 36% in the OIL group and from 0% to
11.8% in the VEIL group. In our study, we found a 25%
incidence of skin necrosis in the OIL group compared to 4%
in the VEIL group.

Similarly, consistent with other systematic reviews [21,22],
which included retrospective studies and those in languages
other than English, we found no difference between techniques
concerning the presence of lymph collection. Therefore, the

primary advantage of VEIL appears to be in reducing the most
frequent complications associated with OIL.

The global proliferation of robotic platforms has increasingly
led to adopting robotic assistance in various urological
laparoscopic surgeries. This trend includes the conversion of
VEIL to robot-assisted procedures, exemplified by the first
robot-assisted IL (RAIL) described by Josephson et al. in 2009

[27]. This innovation marked a significant advancement in
the surgical management of penile cancer. Over the past
decade, just retrospective studies comparing RAIL to
traditional open techniques have demonstrated comparable
oncological outcomes, with RAIL offering the added
advantages of reduced morbidity and faster recovery times.
Nonetheless, despite these promising early results, long-term
and prospective studies are still required to fully validate the
oncological efficacy and safety of RAIL [28,29].

It is important to emphasise that adopting video laparoscopic
techniques inherently involves overcoming a significant
learning curve. Furthermore, integrating these methods into
healthcare systems will require detailed cost-effectiveness
evaluations in the future to ensure widespread access and
equitable dissemination of the technique.

Despite this study being the first meta-analysis utilizing only
prospective studies, it has several limitations due to the rarity
of the disease and the logistical difficulties in establishing a
study protocol, the limited number of patients. The short
follow-up period of the studies also presents a challenge in
analysing oncological outcomes such as recurrence,
disease-free survival, and overall survival. Finally, there may
be language bias in this meta-analysis as only studies
published in English were included.

However, this study offers the most robust evidence currently
available on the subject, drawing on three RCTs and a
prospective observational study [30]. These findings
significantly enhance our understanding of both laparoscopic
and open approaches to IL for penile cancer, providing
valuable data that can inform future clinical practice and
guide ongoing research [31].

Conclusion

In the present meta-analysis of high-quality RCTs and
prospective cohorts, VEIL had significantly fewer wound
infections, skin necrosis, and lymphoedema. The shorter
drainage time, lower intensity and duration of pain, and
fewer postoperative complications seem to be related to a
shorter hospital stay.

The limited sample of 95 patients across four studies
underscores the need for further randomised trials, but the
present findings support the use of VEIL in managing LNs in
penile cancer. These outcomes are promising and emphasise
