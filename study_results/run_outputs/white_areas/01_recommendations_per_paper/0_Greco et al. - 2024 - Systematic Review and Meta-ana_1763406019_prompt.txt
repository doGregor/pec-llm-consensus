1. Introduction

Penile cancer is a rare disease, with an incidence of
1/100 000 males in Europe. However, in some areas such
as South America, Africa, and Asia, it represents 1–2% of
malignant disease cases among males [1].
In patients with penile cancer, the most important prognostic factor for survival is the presence and extent of nodal
metastases, with 5-yr cancer-specific survival rates of
approximately 93%, 75%, 50%, and 17% for N0, N1, N2, and
N3 disease, respectively [2].
To date, clinical lymph node TNM staging is mainly
based on physical examination of both groins [3]. However,
this has limitations in detecting suspect inguinal nodes
(especially in obese patients) and the risk of micrometastasis can be up to 25% even when nodes are not suspicious on
palpation [4]. Moreover, it has been shown that treating
(micro)metastatic disease at an early stage leads to better
oncological outcome in comparison to surgery when nodes
become palpable [4].
Thus, as noninvasive staging options (nomograms, imaging) are not reliable enough to detect micrometastatic disease, invasive surgical staging remains indispensable [5].
However, as only 20–25% of all clinically node-negative
cases harbour occult metastasis, surgical staging represents
overtreatment in most cases. For selection of patients who
are especially at risk of nodal metastases, risk categories
based on tumour stage, grade of differentiation, and lymphovascular or perineural invasion of the primary tumour
have been established [5]. The most widely used surgical
staging options are dynamic sentinel node biopsy (DSNB)
and (radical or modified) inguinal lymphadenectomy (ILND)

[6-8]. In centres that perform DSNB, this procedure is often
preceded by ultrasound (US) with fine-needle aspiration
cytology (FNAC) for cases with suspicious nodes on US [9].
More recently, video-endoscopic inguinal lymphadenectomy (VEIL), including a robot-assisted approach (RAVEIL), was introduced with the aim of reducing the morbidity of open ILND [10,11]. Previous guidelines generally rec

ommended surgical staging for intermediate- and high-risk
tumours, with the method used for surgical staging left to
the surgeon’s discretion as the diagnostic accuracy and
complication rates of the techniques available are not well
established [5].
The present systematic review was undertaken by the
European Association of Urology (EAU) and American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) Collaborative Penile Cancer
Guidelines Panel to evaluate current evidence on the diagnostic accuracy and safety of minimally invasive nodal staging techniques.

2. Evidence acquisition

2.1. Search strategy and screening of studies

This systematic review was undertaken as part of the EAUASCO penile cancer guidelines update for 2023 [12]. The
review was performed according to Preferred Reporting
Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)
guidelines [13]. The search was conducted in accordance
with principles outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [14]. The systematic review
and the research protocol were registered in the PROSPERO
[database (CRD42023395174; https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/](https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/display_record.php?RecordID=395174)
[PROSPERO/display_record.php?RecordID=395174).](https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/display_record.php?RecordID=395174)
An extensive search to identify published and ongoing
studies reporting on the management of men with localised
or advanced penile cancer with clinically normal inguinal
lymph nodes was performed. The Medline, Embase, and
Cochrane controlled trials and ClinicalTrials.gov were for
all relevant publications from 1990 to date.
Two reviewers (I.G. and L.B.) independently screened the
titles and abstracts of the records identified for eligibility.
The full text of all potentially eligible records was retrieved
and screened independently by the two review authors (I.G.
and L.B.) using a standardised form. In cases of disagreement, a senior reviewer (V.I.S.) acted as an arbiter.

Owing to the rarity of the disease and a lack of welldesigned trials, all relevant studies published in English
(randomised controlled trials, nonrandomised prospective
comparative trials, and comparative and noncomparative
observational studies) with a minimum study sample of
30 inguinal basins (15 patients) were included.

2.3. Patients

The study population included patients diagnosed with
penile cancer with no palpable node disease (cN0) undergoing any type of minimally invasive surgical staging for inguinal lymph nodes (DSNB, VEIL, RA-VEIL, modified open
ILND). Owing to a lack of data, studies with mixed oncological populations that involved a considerable number of
penile cancer cN0 cases were also included. Patients who
underwent ILND for another cancer (melanoma or vulvar
carcinoma) were not included.

2.4. Interventions

According to the protocol, the following interventions were
eligible for inclusion: minimally invasive ILND (open modified template, video-endoscopic or robot-assisted) and
DSNB with or without prior US + FNAC of the inguinal areas.

2.5. Outcome measures

The following outcomes were evaluated in the systematic
review: diagnostic accuracy, negative predictive value
(NPV), positive predictive value (PPV), false-negative (FN)
rate, positive nodes in cN0 cases, sensitivity, and specificity.
Treatment-related complications after lymph node staging
were evaluated according to the Clavien-Dindo classification and divided into wound-related and lymphaticrelated complications [15]. The number of lymph nodes
removed, hospital stay, and surgery time were also
evaluated.

2.6. Data extraction

A standardised data extraction form was developed and
piloted before use. The review team (H.A.G.P., I.G., L.B.,
and L.Z.) extracted and reported information available for
the study design, populations, interventions, outcomes,
and risk of bias (RoB) or confounders. Two reviewers (I.G.
and L.B.) independently extracted data related to prespecified outcomes.

2.7. Assessment of RoB and confounders

The RoB for randomised comparative studies was assessed
using the standard Cochrane Collaboration RoB tool [14].
For nonrandomised comparative studies, a modified RoB
tool was used that includes additional domains on the basis

of confounders previously defined by the panel group [16].
RoB in single-arm case series was assessed using a pragmatic approach based on external validity, such as consecutive inclusion of patients, application of a predefined
protocol, attrition bias management, and proper measurement and reporting of outcomes [17].


Descriptive statistics were used to summarise baseline characteristics. Owing to heterogeneity among the populations
and as most of the studies were nonrandomised or singlearm observational studies, a narrative (qualitative) synthesis
was performed. A quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis) was
considered when methodologically appropriate in cases of
well-designed comparative studies. Statistical heterogeneity
within studies was assessed via visual analysis of forest plots,
the v [2] test for heterogeneity, and the s [2] and I [2] statistics. The
derSimonian-Laird method was used to calculate s [2] and the

Jackson method was applied to determine its confidence
interval. Owing to the absence of standard deviation as a relative value in some studies, we followed the Machin method,
dividing the range between the maximum and minimum values into four. The level of statistical significance was 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed using R v4.1.2 and the meta
package [18-20].

3. Evidence synthesis

3.1. Quantity of evidence identified

Overall, the literature search identified 1364 articles. A total
of 341 studies were eligible for full-text screening and, of
which 94 met the inclusion criteria for the update of the
EAU-ASCO guidelines. Of these, 41 studies on minimally
invasive nodal staging were included in the review. Regarding VEIL/RA-VEIL; only comparative studies on minimally
invasive and open surgery were included in the metaanalysis. Therefore, four studies were considered eligible
for the meta-analysis. A PRISMA flow diagram outlining
the study selection process is shown in Figure 1.

3.2. Study characteristics

Evidence synthesis was performed for the following study
types:

(1) Single-arm studies on DSNB with US + FNAC: 18 studies involving 3370 patients [21-38].
(2) Single-arm studies on DSNB without US + FNAC: 13
studies involving 684 patients [34,39-50].
(3) Single-arm studies on open (m)ILND: six studies
involving 316 patients [51-56].
(4) Two-arm comparative studies on open ILND versus
(RA-)VEIL: four studies involving 382 patients [5760].

Six nonrandomised comparative studies were handled as
cohort series as the type of comparison was outside the
scope of our systematic review [22,27,33,39,49,50].
Another study reported results for two cohorts undergoing DSNB across different time periods. We included these
and analysed the two cohorts separately (in the first cohort,
only DSNB was performed; in the second cohort, preoperative US + FNAC was included) [34].
Most studies were performed at a single centre. Regarding DSNB, all the studies included patients with bilateral
cN0 groins or at least one groin with no palpable lymph
nodes (or, when applied, negative findings on US + FNAC).
Thus, DSNB was performed only on negative groins.

3.4. Intervention results

3.4.1. Outcomes for DSNB with FNAC
Overall, 3370 patients undergoing DSNB with preoperative
US + FNAC were included.
Diagnostic accuracy data were reported in an inconsistent manner. Sensitivity varied from 78% to 100%, while
specificity of 100% was reported. When reported, PPV was
100%, while NPV ranged between 93% and 100%. In most
studies, regional recurrences after excision of a tumour with
a negative sentinel node or after no visualisation were considered as FN cases. Among these studies, the FN rate varied
between 3.5% and 22%; the 3.5% was in one of the studies
with the greatest number of patients [23]. In the study by
Yoganarasimha et al. [36], superficial ILND was performed
in all cases. This was considered the gold standard as the
comparator for DSNB and, among 168 specimens, no FN
results on frozen section analysis were observed. Kathiresan
et al. [38] also performed superficial ILND after DSNB. Comparison of the DNSB and the ILND histopathology results
resulted in an FN rate of 10%.
Reporting on complication rates was inconsistent and
the Clavien-Dindo classification was not universally applied
among the studies. When reported, the minor complication
rate (grade I–II) was 1.1–20% and the major complication
rate (grade III–V) was 0–6.8%.

3.4.4. Outcomes and meta-analysis for open (m)ILND versus
VEIL/RA-VEIL
Four studies compared mILND via an open approach against
a minimally invasive approach (VEIL or RA-VEIL). A metaanalysis was possible, with the following conclusions possible when the level of heterogeneity was suitable.
The number of lymph nodes dissected (p = 0.87) and the
operating time (p = 0.21) did not differ between the groups,
but hospital length of stay was more favourable with the
minimally invasive approach (p < 0.01). The rate of positive
nodes after ILND (pN+), which was reported for three of the
four studies, did not differ on quantitative analysis
(p = 0.97). However, in one study the pN+ rate was 14.3%
in the open ILND group and 27.8% in the VEIL group [58]
(Fig. 2).
Grade I–II Clavien-Dindo complication rates ranged
between 25% and 68% with the open approach, and between
17% and 76% with the minimally invasive approach. Quantitative analysis showed an equal distribution between the
approaches (odds ratio [OR] 0.67; p = 0.28) for grade I–II
complications, which are normally considered minor complications. By contrast, the rate of grade III–V complications
ranged between 6.9% and 40.6% with the open approach,
and between 2% and 10.6% with the minimally invasive
approach. Quantitative analysis of grade III–V complications, which are normally considered major complications,
favoured the minimally invasive approach (OR 0.18;
p < 0.01; Fig. 2).
We also divided the complications into wound-related
(infection and skin necrosis) and lymphatic-related (lymphoedema and lymphocele) classes. Overall, the rate of
wound complications with the open approach varied
between 33% and 86%, with infection rates of 6.5–42% and
necrosis rates of 2.1–27.5%. By contrast, the minimally invasive approach had wound complication rates that varied
between 3.7% and 11.7%, with infection rates of 0–9.8%
and necrosis rates of 0–6.8%. In general, fewer woundrelated complications were observed following VEIL/RAVEIL procedures (OR 0.14; p < 0.01), and fewer wound infections (OR 0.229; p < 0.01) and skin necrosis complications
(OR 0.16; p < 0.01), considering both groups as homogeneous (heterogeneity test p > 0.05; Fig. 3).
The rate of lymphatic complications with the open
approach ranged between 27.5% and 64% (lymphoedema
14–23.2% and lymphocele 13–55%). By contrast, the rate
of lymphatic complications with the minimally invasive
approach varied between 20.6% and 49% (lymphoedema
0–21.2% and lymphocele 10.3–49%). Thus, lymphaticrelated complications were equally probable in the two
groups overall (p = 0.12) and for lymphoedema (p = 0.42)
and lymphocele (p = 0.53) individually. The results are
reported in Table 5.

In the prospective study by Lam et al. [25], US + FNAC
alone had sensitivity of 65% per groin and 64% per patient,
while DSNB alone had sensitivity of 92% per groin and
91% per patient. The combination of the two techniques
showed sensitivity of 95% per groin and 94% per patient.
According to the data available, US + FNAC before DSNB
may reduce the FN rate (3.5–22% vs 0–42.9%). A similar
trend was reported by Leijte et al. [34]. In particular, addition of US + FNAC led to a substantial decrease in the FN

rate. The same institution further improved their DSNB
method. Dell’Oglio et al. [24] evaluated the reliability of
DSNB using a hybrid tracer (indocyanine green- [99m] Tcnanocolloid) in a large cohort of 404 consecutive patients.
At median follow-up of 36 mo, the FN rate per patient
was 10% (8.7% per groin) and the probability that a negative
DNSB would be an FN procedure at 2 yr was 2.5% (1.4% per
groin).
Among the series that used preoperative US + FNAC, the
lowest FN rate was reported by Lee et al. [23] for a study
involving 403 patients treated in a high-volume referral
centre. Two systematic reviews also revealed higher sensitivity and detection rates among larger series [61,62]. These
findings support the existence of a potential learning curve
and the importance of performing this procedure in highly
experienced referral centres.
Preoperative US + FNAC can reduce the number of groins
requiring DSNB by 10–13% and ILND can be performed in
cases with positive nodes, allowing early treatment [9,23].
Among the single-arm studies on open (m)ILND, Yao
et al. [52] proposed a modified surgical template for radical
ILND. The technique is characterised by an S-shaped incision, precise separation of layers, dissection of lymph nodes
lateral to the femoral artery, and preservation of the fascia
lata. In comparison to other series on (m)ILND, the authors
reported similar morbidity and mean number of lymph
nodes removed.
Among studies comparing open ILND with a minimally
invasive approach, no significant differences in lymph node
yield or the rate of pathologically positive nodes were
observed. However, the minimally invasive approach
showed better results in terms of hospital stay and major
complications, especially for wound-related events (3.7–
11.7% vs 33–86%) in terms of both necrosis and infection.
There were no significant differences for lymphatic-related
complications (lymphoedema and lymphocele). TobiasMachado et al. [63] reported results for a large series of
105 patients undergoing VEIL. The skin complication rate
was 4.8%, while lymphatic complications were observed in
8.6% of patients. The authors suggested that accurate identification of the lymphatic vessels and the use of clips rather
than cauterisation might have the potential to reduce lymphatic complications.
In a systematic review by Hu et al. [64], rates of wound
infection and lymphoedema were lower in the VEIL group,
with no significant difference in the incidence of lymphocele. The authors also found lower rates of both minor and
major complications according to the Clavien-Dindo system
in the VEIL group.
Several considerations can help in explaining our results.
First, although there were some variations between the series, in each study the dissection boundaries were broadly

experience in robotic or video-laparoscopic surgery and
the availability of instrumentation and a dedicated team
are required to perform ILND via a minimally invasive technique. In addition, DSNB is generally considered logistically
challenging, requiring radiologists for inguinal US, nuclear


medicine physicians for interpretation of nuclear images,
urologists for the surgical procedure, and pathologists for
specimen analysis. A recent meta-analysis showed that
the FN rate was lower when DSNB was performed in highvolume centres [9].
In decentralised settings, especially in centres with
unspecialised clinicians, low operative volume because of
the low incidence of penile cancer limits opportunities to
gain the experience required for optimal management of
the disease [67].
Our systematic review revealed a discrepancy between
postoperative events and sample size, which may be
because of accuracy in recording data and the use of codified classification systems. However, suboptimal management and a lack of adherence to guideline
recommendations have been observed in low-volume cen
tres. In a multicentre study involving 425 patients, Cindolo
et al. [68] found that rate of adherence to the EAU guidelines on lymph node dissection was 73.7%. Woldu et al. [4]
found that only 25.3% of patients with intermediate- or
high-risk localised penile cancer underwent surgical nodal
staging, and that omission of invasive staging was an independent predictor of overall mortality. The authors found
that nonacademic institutions were less likely to adhere to
recommendations. These factors support the need for centralised management of penile cancer. Notably, countries
where centralised health care is offered have experienced
an impressive reduction in understaging and an improvement in survival rates [3,69].

Although the level of heterogeneity and uncertainty in the
evidence base does not allow robust affirmation of any

– When surgical staging is indicated, DSNB shows good

– When DSNB is planned, inguinal US and FNAC of nodes

– When DSNB is not available or not preferred by the

– In comparison to open ILND, VEIL/RA-VEIL appears to be

3.5.3. Strengths and limitations
This review was conducted in a rigorous manner according
to the PRISMA guidelines by a multidisciplinary panel in
line with recognised standards. There was clinical heterogeneity among the studies included in terms of reporting,


surgical techniques, study populations, anatomic fields,
and the aim of the interventions.

4. Conclusions

Of all the current surgical staging options for cN0 penile
cancer, DSNB with preoperative inguinal US + FNAC has
the lowest complication rates and has high diagnostic accuracy, especially when performed in high-volume centres. If
(experience with) DSNB is not available, favourable results
(hospital stay, wound-related and major complications)
were also found for VEIL/RA-VEIL over the open surgical
approach. Lymphatic-related complications are comparable
across both open and video-endoscopic ILND. Multicentre
prospective studies with larger sample sizes are needed to
validate these findings.
