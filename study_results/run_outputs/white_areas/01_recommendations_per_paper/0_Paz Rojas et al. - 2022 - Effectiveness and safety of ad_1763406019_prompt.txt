Abstract

Objectives: To determine the effectiveness of adjuvant chemotherapy compared with neoadjuvant chemotherapy in patients with nodepositive penile cancer in terms of overall and disease-free survival.
Methods: We conducted a search strategy in MEDLINE, Embase, and Central databases. We complemented the search with unpublished literature through manual search, conferences, thesis databases, Open Grey, Google Scholar, and Clinicaltrials.gov. There were no
restrictions in language. We used the MINORS tool to assess the risk of bias. Furthermore, we performed a random-effects meta-analysis
according to the expected heterogeneity. The outcomes were overall survival, progression-free survival, and adverse effects. The Effect
measure was hazard ratio (HR) with a confidence interval of 95%.
Results: We included 1,197 patients. Seven articles reported overall survival; while 3 reported progression-free survival. The pooled

Conclusions: There were no differences when comparing adjuvant vs. neoadjuvant chemotherapy or adjuvant vs. no intervention chemotherapy. We conclude that progression-free survival had a better response with adjuvant chemotherapy when compared with neoadjuvant

burden of morbidity and mortality [1]. Its incidence increases
with age, with a peak in the seventh decade of life [1].
Its etiology is not entirely clear; however, it has a strong
association with the human papillomavirus and a chronic

2. Methods

We performed this study according to Cochrane recommendations [15] and also following the PRISMA guidelines

[16,17].

2.1. Eligibility criteria

We included experimental, quasi-experimental, and
cohort studies. We also included studies that had at least 10

patients diagnosed with penile cancer and clinical or pathological N1 to 3. We accepted studies in which all patients
underwent a groin surgical procedure but compared adjuvant vs. neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimens. Furthermore,
those studies might include nonintervention arms (no chemotherapy). Overall survival and progression-free survival
were sought as the primary outcomes. All studies had at
least a 12-month follow-up. We excluded studies with nonnode metastatic penile cancer (visceral) and studies in
which concomitant radiation therapy.

2.2. Information sources

We performed a search strategy in MEDLINE (OVID),
EMBASE, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled
Trials (CENTRAL) from the beginning of the collection of


the literature in the databases, until April 2020 (Appendix 1).
Furthermore, we performed a reference scan of relevant
articles identified through search, lectures, thesis databases,
Open Gray, Google Scholar, and Clinicaltrials.gov.

2.3. Selection process

Two investigators reviewed each reference by title and
abstract. Then, we scanned the full texts of relevant studies,
applied pre-specified inclusion and exclusion criteria. We
solved disagreements by consensus, and when disagreements
cannot be resolved, a third reviewer solved the conflict.

2.4. Data collection

Two trained reviewers used a standardized form. We

independently extracted the following information from
each article: study design, geographic location, authors’
names, title, objectives, inclusion and exclusion criteria,
number of patients included, losses to follow-up, time, definitions of results, results, and association measures.

3. Risk of bias assessment

Since we only obtained non-randomized studies, we
used the MINORS tool for assessing the risk of bias. We
also used Review Manager 5.3 (RevMan [�] 5.3) [18].

3.1. Statistical analysis

We carried out the statistical analysis in Review Manager
5.3 (RevMan [�] 5.3) [18]. We performed a random-effects
meta-analysis based on expected heterogeneity. Also, the
measure of effect was the hazard ratio (HR) with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals. We assessed the heterogeneity using the I [2] test. For interpretation, the values of
25%, 50%, and 75% in the I [2] test correspond to low,
medium, and high levels of heterogeneity, respectively.

3.2. Publication bias

It was not carried out given the small sample of included
studies.

3.3. Sensitivity analysis

We performed a sensitivity analysis by extracting
weighted studies and running the estimated effect to find
differences.

3.4. Subgroup analysis

The analysis by subgroups estimated in the protocol
according to the number of nodes, tumor, or nodal stage
was not possible to carry out.

4. Results

4.1. Study selection

We found 211 studies with search strategies. We finally
included 7 studies [19−25] for qualitative and quantitative
analysis (Fig. 1).

4.2. Characteristics of the included studies

We finally included 7 retrospective cohort studies in the
analysis. A total of 1,197 patients were recruited; of which
573 received some chemotherapeutic intervention, either
adjuvant or neoadjuvant. All 7 articles reported overall survival, but only 3 reported progression-free survival
(Table 1).

4.3. Risk of bias assessment

We assessed an unclear risk of bias regarding the issues
“prospective data collection,” and “sample size calculation,” in all the studies since they did not provide


information. Only 1 study (Sharma 2015) had a high risk of
bias in the “unbiased assessment of primary outcomes.” We
classified more than 50% of the studies as unclear risk of
bias for the outcomes: “adequate control group,”
“contemporary groups,” and “equivalent groups at baseline” (Fig. 2A and B).

4.4. Primary outcomes

4.4.1. Overall survival

4.4.1.1. Adjuvant vs. neoadjuvant chemotherapy. We included 3
studies [21,23,24], with 288 participants. We found a
pooled HR of 1.41 (0.99−2.02) I [2] : 0%. There were no statistically significant differences, but there was a trend in
favor of adjuvant chemotherapy (Fig. 3A)

4.4.1.2. Adjuvant chemotherapy vs. no intervention. We included 4
studies [19,21,22,25] for overall survival; with a total of
778 patients. We evidenced an HR of 0.95 (0.48−1.89) I [2] :
75% (Fig. 3B); resulting in no differences between these 2
interventions.

4.4.1.3. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy vs. no intervention. We included
2 studies [20,21], with a total of 211 patients. An HR 0.36
(0.27−0.49) I [2] : 90%. We found evidence in favor of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (Fig. 3C).

5.2. Contrast with literature

Chemotherapy is part of multimodal cancer management
in patients with PC and positive lymph nodes [9]. Adjuvant
and neoadjuvant regimens have been used, without finding
extensive prospective studies, or secondary analytical data
in the scientific literature. There is no consensus on which
of the chemotherapeutic regimens, whether adjuvant or
neoadjuvant, has a greater benefit in overall or progressionfree survival in urological guidelines, at some point the recommendations of 1 technique intersect with the other [10].
Retrospective studies have presented the comparison of AC
vs. NC, without obtaining conclusive data in favor of AC

−

[26 29], possibly due to the lack of patients, since penile
cancer is a rare disease [2]. The previous statement makes
it difficult to conduct prospective studies or clinical trials.
Ideally, we would also like to have information in comparisons, such as neoadjuvant chemotherapy + surgery vs.
surgery + adjuvant chemotherapy regarding the nodal stage.
However, currently, it is not possible to solve this question.


This study is the first systematic review and meta-analysis that attempts to resolve this gap in the scientific medical
literature. In this study, we found a trend where overall survival is higher in patients who underwent AC. Unfortunately, the data is not significant enough to give a clear
recommendation on this section. Two recent studies have

shown how characteristics of the tumor and lymph node status (TNM), as well as the use of 18F-FDG-PET/CT of the
pelvis, can achieve a prediction of what patients will benefit
from an additional adjuvant therapy to surgical management or an NC before surgical management [30,31]. This
could explain why the results of the articles included in the
meta-analysis do not lead to significant results, despite
using therapies that should theoretically work. The previously mentioned articles by Bandini et al. should lead to a
better selection of patients who are suitable for adjuvant or
neoadjuvant therapies. Additional stricter studies on selection criteria will need to be conducted.

Currently, neoadjuvant chemotherapy is mostly reserved
for those with advanced nodal disease (cN3), and adjuvant
therapy is indicated in pN2-3 [9]. In this review, most studies similarly treat pN2 and pN3 patients, but putting

5.3. Strengths and weaknesses

It is the first meta-analysis that compares chemotherapy
in node-positive penile cancer, where it was shown that
neoadjuvant chemotherapy is better than no intervention.
The PRISMA recommendations and the Cochrane collabo
ration were considered. Although penile cancer is a rare
pathology, a pooled population of 1,197 patients was
achieved, thus increasing the statistical power of the metaanalysis by the population collected, which can generate a
high impact on the oncological management of penile cancer, supporting currently used behaviors.
Unfortunately, all the studies were retrospective, most
with a small sample size. Furthermore, as these were collaborative multi-institutional and international studies, some
patients might be from the same institutions; hopefully,
there were not many. Nonetheless, as PC is a rare disease,
researchers need to keep doing collaborative studies to fill
the gaps in this vital area. Despite this, it was possible to
gather the management and data from multiple regions of
the world in a similar period, showing that the management
is adequate and of similar characteristics. However, the
studies’’ heterogeneity and the difference in some results
did not give other types of results. The survival analysis
compared the 2 chemotherapy techniques available for

penile cancer. One drawback is the non-stratification of the
intervention and control groups regarding lymph node
involvement. Patients with N3 lymph nodes could initially
undergo neoadjuvant chemotherapy for loco-regional and
systemic control of the disease, assuming they are facing a
more advanced disease, which could give a biased result in
favor of AC.

All the studies included in the meta-analysis use platinum-based chemotherapy. This shows a certain homogeneity in international management. However, there are
differences in complementary drugs, the main ones are taxanes and 5 FU. Not all studies show how many patients
received each of the regimens or even had information to
perform subgroup analysis. These differences in management, the lack of some data in the studies, and the small
number of studies with each of the chemotherapies make it
impossible to compare the different regimens to reveal
which could be the best chemotherapy regimen.
On the other hand, many patients initially classified at
the beginning of their disease as N1-2, with final lymph
node pathology, could become N3 and receive an adjuvant
scheme, when they could receive initial neoadjuvant management. Due to the heterogeneity of the patients and possible changes in the choice of treatment, whether noticed or
unnoticed, AC shows a benefit in relapse-free survival and
a clear trend in overall survival compared with NC. Within
the studies analyzed, some of these did not have explicit
HR, so we had to calculate it. Unfortunately, the safety
analysis was not possible due to a lack of data.
As the included studies were retrospective, it is also
essential to describe that there was an unclear risk of bias in

most of the studies regarding prospective data collection,
sample size calculation, and adequate control group. The
previous, downstage the quality of the obtained results.

6. Conclusions

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy favors overall survival when
compared with no intervention. Nevertheless, there were no
differences when comparing adjuvant vs. neoadjuvant chemotherapy or adjuvant vs. no intervention chemotherapy.
In contrast, progression-free survival had a better response
with adjuvant chemotherapy when compared with neoadjuvant therapy. We suggest more studies with adequate design
to offer a stronger recommendation.
