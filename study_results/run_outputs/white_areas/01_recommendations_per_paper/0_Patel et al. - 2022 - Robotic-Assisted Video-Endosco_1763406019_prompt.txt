# Robotic-Assisted Video-Endoscopic Inguinal Lymphadenectomy (RAVEIL) and Video-Endoscopic Inguinal Lymphadenectomy (VEIL) versus Open Inguinal Lymph-Node Dissection (OILND) in carcinoma of penis: Comparison of perioperative outcomes, complications and oncological outcomes. A systematic review and meta-analysis

Abstract

Objective: To perform a systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating the peri-operative outcomes, complications, and oncological
outcomes of Robotic-Assisted Video-Endoscopic Inguinal Lymphadenectomy (RAVEIL)/Video-Endoscopic Inguinal Lymphadenectomy
(VEIL) with Open Inguinal Lymph-Node Dissection (OILND) for management of inguinal lymph-nodes in carcinoma of the penis.
Methods: A comprehensive literature search was performed in January 2021 using the PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane databases. Data
from human studies comparing RAVEIL/VEIL vs. OILND in carcinoma of penis published in English was extracted and analyzed by two
independent authors.
Results: Two Randomised Controlled Trials and 6 cohort studies were included in the meta-analysis. RAVEIL/VEIL group exhibited
increased operative time (Mean Difference [MD] = 15.28 [14.19; 16.38], P < 0.001), shorter hospital stay (MD = -1.06 [-1.14; -0.98],
P < 0.001), and decreased duration of drainage (MD = -2.82 [-3.21; -2.43], P < 0.001), wound infection (Odds Ratio [OR] = 0.15 [0.08;
0.27], P < 0.001), skin necrosis (OR = 0.12 [0.05; 0.28], P < 0.001), lymphedema (OR = 0.41 [0.24; 0.72], P = 0.002), and major complications (OR = 0.11 [0.05; 0.24], P < 0.001) as compared to OILND group. Recurrence rate and number of deaths were comparable in both the
groups. RAVEIL/VEIL groups showed slightly larger lymph-node yield (MD = 0.44 [0.18; 0.70], P < 0.001) as compared to OILND group.
Conclusion: RAVEIL/VEIL has lesser skin complications, lymphedema, and better lymph-node yield as compared to OILND. It is comparable in terms of lymphocele and recurrence. It has lesser hospital stay and duration of drainage but owing to heterogeneity, the results
should be interpreted with caution. Further studies are required to determine long-term oncological outcomes like overall survival and
disease-specific survival. � 2021 Elsevier Inc. All rights reserved.

1. Introduction

Carcinoma of the penis is a rare malignancy. The most
common histological type of carcinoma of the penis is
Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC) [1]. The incidence of carcinoma of the penis in developed countries is 1/100,000.
However, in developing countries like India, the incidence
is high in a range of 2 to 4/10,000 [2]. The most common
site of metastasis in the SCC penis is inguinal lymph nodes

[3]. The presence of inguinal lymph node metastasis is considered a poor prognostic factor [4]. The 5-year survival
rate of patients after preventive lymphadenectomy can
reach 80% to 90%, while the 5-year survival rate is only
30% to 40% for patients who undergo surgery after the
occurrence of lymph node metastasis [5]. Therefore, it is
very important to address inguinal lymph nodes in the comprehensive management of the SCC Penis.
Open inguinal lymphadenectomy has traditionally been
considered the gold standard for the management of inguinal lymph nodes in the SCC penis. It is, however; associated with high morbidity in the form of complications like
skin necrosis, wound infection, lymphocele, and lymphedema [6]. Studies using SEER and The National Cancer
database show low levels of inguinal lymph node dissection
amongst penile cancer patients, almost certainly due to the
morbidity and rarity of the cancer, and improved survival in
those who undergo inguinal lymph node dissection [7]. Different ways have been adapted to minimize these morbidities. Firstly, attempts have been made to predict the
occurrence of metastasis in inguinal lymph nodes based on

−
various scoring systems [8 12]. Based on it, the patients
with no or lowest risk of lymph node metastasis can be predicted and inguinal lymphadenectomy is avoided in these
subsets of patients. Secondly, the role of active surveillance
and dynamic sentinel lymph node biopsy has been evaluated to predict inguinal lymph node metastasis [13]. However, there is always a risk of micro-metastasis which can
be missed and lead to compromised oncological outcomes

[14]. Thirdly, various technical modifications have been
proposed to decrease complications like saphenous vein
preservation and the use of a modified lymph-node dissection template [15,16]. Lastly, investigators have evaluated
the use of minimally invasive techniques like Video Endoscopic Inguinal Lymphadenectomy (VEIL) and more
recently, Robotic Assisted Video Endoscopic Inguinal
Lymphadenectomy (RAVEIL).
VEIL was first introduced in the clinical management of
inguinal lymph nodes in carcinoma of the penis by Tobias
et al. [17] in 2005 to minimize morbidity and fasten convalescence. RAVEIL was introduced by Josephson et al. [18]
in 2009 to surpass the limitations of VEIL namely steep
learning curve. There is not enough literature and subsequent clarity in guidelines regarding the benefit of minimally invasive inguinal lymphadenectomy as compared to
open. Hence, we performed a systematic review and metaanalysis of available data from the published literature to


compare the perioperative outcomes, complications, and
oncological outcomes of VEIL and RAVEIL with conventional Open Inguinal Lymph-node Dissection (OILND).

2. Methods

The study protocol was performed according to the
Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and
Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [19] after approval
from the Institutional Review Board.

2.1. Literature search

A systematic search of the literature in PubMed,
Embase, and Cochrane Central search Library was done in
January 2021. Studies comparing Robotic or Video Endoscopic Inguinal Lymphadenectomy in SCC penis were
searched by the following keywords: Carcinoma Penis,
Lymph node, Video Endoscopic Inguinal Lymphadenectomy, Robotic Assisted Video Endoscopic Inguinal Lymphadenectomy, Open Inguinal lymphadenectomy, and
outcomes. The search strategy applied in PubMed database
was (“carcinoma”[MeSH Terms] Odds Ratio (OR)
“carcinoma”[All Fields] OR “carcinomas”[All Fields] OR
“carcinoma s”[All Fields]) AND (“penis”[MeSH Terms]
OR “penis”[All Fields]) AND (“lymph nodes”[MeSH
Terms] OR “lymph”[All Fields]) AND (“nodes”[All Fields]
OR “lymph nodes”[All Fields] OR “lymph”[All Fields])
AND (“node”[All Fields] OR “lymph node”[All Fields]).
Abstracts of all the articles were reviewed and studies that

met the inclusion criterion were further searched for refer
ences manually.

2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligibility of the studies to be included in the meta-analysis was based on PICOS principle: - Population (carcinoma penis), Intervention (RAVEIL/VEIL), Comparison
(OILND), and Outcome (perioperative, complications, and
oncological outcomes), and Study design (Randomised
Controlled Trial [RCT]/cohort studies). The inclusion criteria for the analysis were: (1) RCT/Cohort studies comparing
RAVEIL/VEIL with OILND in carcinoma of the penis, (2)
Studies comparing perioperative and oncological outcomes
between RAVEIL/VEIL and OILND, (3) Studies having
sufficient data which was defined as at least 8 out of 15
parameters of perioperative outcomes, complications, and
oncological outcomes, (4) Studies which were published in
the English language, and (5) Human studies. The exclusion
criteria for the analysis were: (1) Study types other than
RCT and cohort studies namely case reports, case series,
review, and letter to editor or editorials, (2) Data was not
sufficient in the studies, (3) Studies published in nonEnglish literature, and (4) Studies performed on non-human
subjects. The selection of the studies to be included in the

analysis was done by two independent authors (KP and AS)
with mutual agreement.

2.3. Data extraction and outcome measures

Data extraction for the analysis was done by two independent authors (KP and GB). Any disagreement between
them was resolved through mutual discussion. Mean and
Standard Deviation was recorded for the continuous varia
bles from the included studies. In those studies which provided a median and inter-quartile range for continuous
variables, we converted them to mean and standard deviation from the original data [20].
The peri-operative outcomes recorded were operative
time (minutes), blood loss (ml), hospital stay (days), and
duration of drainage (days). Complications were recorded
as dichotomous variables and included wound infection,
skin necrosis, lymphocele, and lymphedema. Complications were graded as minor if Clavien-Dindo grade I and II
complications were present and major if Clavien-Dindo
grade III and IV complications were present [21]. The adequacy of dissection was recorded as the number of lymph
nodes (continuous variable). The oncological outcomes
analyzed were the number of deaths as a continuous variable, recurrence rates as a dichotomous variable, and over
all survival as a continuous variable.

2.4. Quality assessment and grade recommendations

The quality of included RCTs was evaluated using the
Cochrane risk of bias (RoB2) tool which included seven
aspects: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of
outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective
reporting, and other bias [22]. Each item was assessed as
having a low, unclear, or high risk of bias by two authors
(KP and GB).
The quality of non-randomized cohort studies was
assessed using the Risk of Bias in Non-Randomised Studies
− of Intervention (ROBINS-I) tool [23]. It included bias
due to confounding, bias in the selection of participants,
bias in classification of intervention, bias due to deviation
from intended intervention, bias due to missing data, bias
in the measurement of outcomes, and bias in the selection
of the reported results. Each item was assessed as low,
moderate, serious, and critical risk of bias by two authors
(KP and GB).
GRADE recommendations were performed on pooled
outcomes of all the studies and a Summary of Findings
table was created [24]. They were done separately for
RCTs and cohort studies. The initial grade for RCT was
high, whereas it was low for cohort study. The factors that
contributed to downgrade were risk of bias, inconsistency,
indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The factors
that contributed to the upgrade were large effect, plausible
cofounding, and dose-response gradient.


2.5. Statistical analysis

RAVEIL/VEIL was considered as experimental intervention and OILND was considered as a control interven
tion. The results of continuous variables (Operative time,
blood loss, hospital stay, duration of drainage, number of
lymph nodes, and overall survival) were expressed as Mean
Difference (MD) and 95% CI. The results of dichotomous
variables (wound infection, skin necrosis, lymphocele,
lymphedema, minor complications, major complications,
and recurrence) were expressed as OR (Odds Ratio) and
95% CI. Forest plots were drawn for each outcome to measure heterogeneity and determine the P-value for it. Statistical heterogeneity amongst the studies was measured using
the I [2] test [25]. The I [2] value cut-offs which were used to
define the heterogeneity were: I [2] < 25% - no heterogeneity,
I [2] = 25%-50% - moderate heterogeneity, I [2] - 50% - significant heterogeneity. If significant heterogeneity was present,
the random effect was used otherwise fixed effect was used.
Subgroup analysis was further performed if the heterogeneity persisted after applying random effects. Further, sensitivity analysis of the outcomes was performed by using
leave-out analysis in which each study was sequentially
omitted from the analysis to record any change in the estimates and heterogeneity. Funnel plots were drawn to assess
publication bias amongst the included studies. We performed the meta-analysis using the Review Manager (RevMan) software 5.4.1 (The Nordic Cochrane Centre, The
Cochrane Collaboration, and Copenhagen).

9. Discussion

The results from the current meta-analysis demonstrated
that the mean operative time required to perform RAVEIL/
VEIL was 111.39 minutes as compared to 95.96 minutes
for OILND. The mean difference between them was 15.28

minutes. The longer operative time in RAVEIL/VEIL can
be attributed to (1) Extra time is needed to create skin flaps,
(2) Limited working space may lead to crowding of instruments and repeated fogging of the lens due to the use of harmonic scalpel which may cause increased operative time

[29], (3) Most of the studies have presented their data
wherein the surgeons were in their initial phase of the learning curve of VEIL/RAVEIL as compared to OILND. This
was further emphasized by Yuvraja et al. [33], who have
demonstrated in their study that with increased experience

−
of the surgeon (2009 2020) there is a significant reduction
in the operative time required for RAVEIL/VEIL, and (4)
The low incidence of carcinoma of the penis itself might be
responsible for the learning curve. Wang et al. [28] reported
higher operative time for OILND resulting in clinical heterogeneity. The reason may be due to the higher number of
pelvic lymphadenectomy in the open arm as compared to
the laparoscopic arm (33% vs. 10%).
Our study also demonstrated that RAVEIL/VEIL
required less duration of hospitalization as compared to
OILND. The studies involving RAVEIL/VEIL had a mean
duration of hospital stay ranging from 2.5 to 10.43 days
whereas in studies involving OILND it was 4 to 12.5 days.
The reason for this early convalescence can be attributed to
incisions in the thigh and not the groin, which might lead to


decreased pain and subsequent early ambulation. The clinical heterogeneity between the studies showing the duration
of hospital stay might be attributed to differences in discharge protocols between the studies. The decrease in skinrelated complications might also have been responsible for
early discharge. The mean duration of drainage was
6.53 days in RAVEIL/VEIL, which was less than OILND
(9.21 days). This can be attributed to higher magnification
and subsequent better control of lymphatics and small blood
vessels [24]. The clinical heterogeneity between the studies
showing the duration of drainage might be attributed to different protocols for drain removal. Yadav et al. [29]
removed the drain when the output was less than 20 ml per
day where Kumar et al. [27], Wang et al. [28], and Singh
et al. [30] removed the drain when output was less than 50.
Prolonged drainage was defined as lymphorrhagia lasting
for 2 to 3 weeks by Yuvraja et al. [33] while the other 2
studies [26,31] that reported it did not provide the definition
of prolonged drainage. This prevents us from drawing conclusions regarding advantage of RAVEIL/VEIL as compared to OILND in terms of decreasing prolonged drainage.
Our analysis also demonstrated that there was a significant decrease in skin-related complications with RAVEIL/
VEIL as compared to OILND. Skin necrosis was found in
0% to 6.4% amongst studies of RAVEIL/VEIL whereas it
was found in 4.03% to 30% amongst studies of OILND.
Wound infection was found in 0% to 7.9% amongst studies
of RAVEIL/VEIL whereas it was found in 1.61% to 68%

amongst studies of OILND. This decline in skin complications in RAVEIL/VEIL can be attributed to smaller inci
sions in the thigh as against that in the groin [26] and less
traction [28] as compared to that in OILND. Our analysis
demonstrated that there was less lymphedema in RAVEIL/
VEIL as compared to OILND. It was seen in 0% to 15.78%
amongst studies of RAVEIL/VEIL whereas in 5.64% to
37% amongst studies of OILND. The reason might be
decreased length of skin incisions and less traction [25] on
skin flaps in RAVEIL/VEIL which may have preserved
more skin lymphatic and vasculatures. No need for Sartorius transposition [26] in RAVEIL/VEIL because of small
incisions in the thigh away from the site of dissection might
also have led to decreased lymphedema. RAVEIL/VEIL
showed decreased major Clavien-Dindo complications but
not minor complications. Similar findings were observed by
Singh et al. [29] in the multivariate analysis of their study.
OILND and pathological lymph-node status appeared as
only factors associated with major complications when confounded with other factors like Sartorius transposition,
radiotherapy, and saphenous vein ligation.
We found that there was more lymph-node yield in
OILND as compared to RAVEIL/VEIL. The number of
lymph-node dissected has been postulated as a surrogate for
adequacy of lymph-node dissection but the definition of
threshold of lymph-nodes is not uniform [34]. A lymphnode yield of 8 was found adequate by Johnson et al. [35]
and Mao et al. [36] whereas yield of 15 was found adequate

K.N. Patel et al. / Urologic Oncology: Seminars and Original Investigations 40 (2022) 112.e11−112.e22 112.e20


by N. Soodana-Prakash et al. [37] and Li et al. [38]. The
mean lymph-node yield in present meta-analysis was 12.06
for OILND whereas it was 11.62 for RAVEIL/VEIL which

indicates the adequacy of lymph-node removal in both.
Whilst this might be statistically significant, it is not clinically significant as it did not translate into a survival benefit.
This shows that minimally invasive lymphadenectomy does
not seem to be inferior to open technique in number of
lymph-nodes removed. There was no difference in recurrence and number of deaths in both the groups which suggests that oncological outcomes were comparable between
RAVEIL/VEIL and OILND. Only one study [33] showed
survival results and found that there was no difference

between the two modalities. The factors that showed a significant difference in survival in their study were pathological lymph-node status, grade of the tumor, T stage of the
primary tumor, and type of surgery for the primary tumor.
In univariate analysis, the type of surgery for lymph nodes
(VEIL vs. OILND) did not show any statistical survival
advantage along with age, smoking, BMI, or T-stage. Clinical node stage, pathological node stage, grade of the tumor,
and type of surgery for primary showed survival advantage
and were subjected to multivariate analysis. Only grade
(P = 0.04) and pathological lymph-node status (P = 0.024)
were significantly associated with survival on multivariate
analysis.
To date, we found only one meta-analysis comparing
VEIL with OILND [39]. The authors included 10 studies
with 307 patients. They concluded that VEIL was superior
to OILND for perioperative and short-term oncological outcomes. However, the analysis had some pitfalls. Firstly,
they had included articles published in the non-English language. Secondly, some included studies were not specific
for carcinoma of the penis [40]. Thirdly, the method to
assess the risk of bias was the same for RCT and cohort

studies. The advantages of our analysis are that we used
stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria (articles in English
language only) because they were easily accessible. We
have included articles pertaining only to carcinoma of the
penis for the conclusions to become specific and applicable.
We have used separate methods to assess the risk of bias
for RCT and cohort studies as per PRISMA guidelines.
Lastly, by appearing to reduce the morbidity of inguinal
node dissection perhaps minimally invasive surgery will
increase the use of inguinal lymph node dissection in penile
cancer, particularly as a staging operation in cN0 disease.
There is no consensus for an optimal number of cases of
minimally invasive inguinal lymphadenectomy after which
the surgeon may be considered proficient to perform the
procedure (learning curve). Jakub et al. [41] proposed a
minimum 6 cases of minimally invasive lymphadenectomy
as a learning curve in cases of melanoma. However, for
similar procedures (total extraperitoneal hernia repair) at
least 40 to 60 cases have been proposed [42]. Therefore, the
procedure should be performed in high volume centres with
more centralised system.


Nevertheless, the authors acknowledge that there are few
limitations in this analysis. Firstly, there was small number
of patients in the analysis due to the rarity of the disease
and scanty literature, which may have resulted in unbalanced comparisons between some groups leading to glossing of true differences. Secondly, some outcome variables
were not reported by all the included studies. Thirdly, the
method of assessment of certain outcomes e.g. lymphedema
and prolonged drainage were not defined in most of the
studies which could have introduced heterogeneity and bias
in the results. Additionally, the size of lymph-node >3 cm
to be an exclusion criterion for RAVEIL/VEIL was used by
2 cohort studies [31,32] and was not defined by others,
making it a potential source of bias. Also, important missing information in all of our studies was the cost and ergonomics comparison between the two modalities, which can
affect the treatment decision. Lastly, there was a significant
heterogeneity amongst the studies used in the analysis of
perioperative outcomes. This along with the lack of blinding makes it difficult to be certain that one technique is better than the other.

The authors accept that the quality and strength of analysis depend on the quality of input data. Unfortunately, we
found only 2 RCTs and 6 cohort studies that matched the
inclusion criteria and this may have been a potential source
of confounding, selection, and reporting bias. Again, both
the RCTs are underpowered with a total of 39 patients in
each arm. Although, Yadav et al. [29] adopted a fixed
method of randomization by assigning the right groin to
VEIL and left to OILND, the method of randomization was
not specified by Tobias et al. [26]. There was no blinding as
well. Given these drawbacks, we would like to emphasize
the need for a prospective, randomized, double-blinded,
multi-institutional clinical trial with an adequate number of
patients and long-term follow-up data to further validate
our conclusions. In addition, future studies should also
explore the impact of certain factors on the selection of
modality for lymph-node dissection, like template of
lymph-node dissection (superficial vs. deep), adjuvant therapy (neoadjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy), ergonomics, and cost. The impact of these factors on perioperative
outcomes, complications, and long-term oncological outcomes would also serve as an area of future investigation.
Additionally, studies focussing on detailed lymphatic drainage of the lower limb and groin to prevent lymphocele and
prolonged drainage should also help in decision making in
the future.

10. Conclusion

RAVEIL/VEIL can decrease skin-related complications
like skin necrosis and wound infection, major complications, and lymphatic complications like lymphedema as
compared to OILND in carcinoma penis. It can decrease
perioperative outcomes like hospital stay and duration of
drainage but owing to heterogeneity, the results should be

112.e21 K.N. Patel et al. / Urologic Oncology: Seminars and Original Investigations 40 (2022) 112.e11−112.e22


interpreted with caution. Blood loss, minor complications,
and lymphocele are comparable in both groups. RAVEIL/
VEIL is equally adequate in terms of the number of lymph
nodes dissected as compared to OILND. However, recurrence rates, number of deaths, and OS are similar in both
groups. Large, multi-centric, high-quality RCTs with longterm follow-up in the future are required to provide substantial evidence for verification of the results.
