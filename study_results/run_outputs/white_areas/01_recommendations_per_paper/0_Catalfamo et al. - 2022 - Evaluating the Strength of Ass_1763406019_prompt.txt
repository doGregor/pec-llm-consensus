## Evaluating the Strength of Association of Human Papillomavirus Infection With Penile Carcinoma: A Meta-Analysis

Collin J. Catalfamo, MPH, Heidi E. Brown, PhD, and Leslie K. Dennis, PhD


Background: Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a common sexually transmitted infection that is strongly associated with cervical cancer. A link to penile
cancers has been suggested by case series. We sought to assess the strength of
the association between HPV infection and penile cancer by meta-analysis.
Methods: A literature search to identify population-based studies evaluating the risk of HPV infection with penile cancer was conducted via PubMed
and Google Scholar databases through December 2020. Studies were included in the pooled analyses if they presented relative risk (RR) estimates
comparing penile cancer cases with noncases by HPVexposure status. They
were stratified by (1) type of HPV, (2) test used to determine past HPV infection, and (3) the penile cancer type. Pooled analyses were conducted for
stratum with at least 2 independent studies using fixed-effects and
random-effects models.

Results: Fourteen articles representing 9 study populations fit the inclusion
criteria and were included in the pooled analysis. Based on these studies, the

–
pooled RRs are 2.9 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.7 5.0; n = 4 studies) for

–
invasive penile cancer and seropositivity to HPV16 L1, 4.5 (95% CI, 1.3
15.5; n = 2) for seropositivity to HPV18, and 8.7 for anogenital warts (95%

–
CI, 5.1 14.8; n = 5). For the 3 studies reporting invasive and in situ penile
cancer, the risk was 7.6 for anogenital warts.
Conclusions: The pooled RRs indicate up to a 4.5-fold increased risk between seropositivity for HPV infection and invasive penile cancer. This is
much lower than associations seen between HPVand cervical cancer.

Human papillomavirus, a well-known contributor to infectious causes of cancer in the United States, is a common sexually
transmitted infection, affecting 42.5% of US adults. [3] The probability of acquiring an HPV infection for men in the US before readily
available HPV vaccination was estimated to be 89.1%; however,
with many HPV infections being asymptomatic, most infections
among men are undetected. [4] Human papillomavirus infection
manifestations range from low-risk HPV infections causing no
symptoms, which may clear without treatment, to high-risk HPV
infections strongly linked to the development of cervical cancers. [5]

Human papillomavirus was recognized as an important cause of
cervical cancer by the World Health Organization in 1996. [6] Since
then, HPV has been associated with anogenital warts and multiple
anogenital cancers in both men and women. [7] Among these associations, little is known about HPVand penile cancer.
Worldwide, there are an estimated 22,000 cases of penile
cancer per year with a higher incidence in lower-income countries. [8] Incidence can reach as high as 3.3 to 4.2 per 100,000 males
in Brazil and Paraguay, respectively. [8] It is a rare genital malignancy
in the United States, with an incidence rate of 0.4 to 0.9 per 100,000
males between 2011 and 2015. [9,10] Links between HPVand the development of penile carcinoma have been suggested with infection
prevalence, the proportion of people in a given population who have
had the infection during a given period of time, ranging from 47%
to 55% of penile carcinoma cases. [11] However, these estimates have
been based on case-series of HPV and penile cancer without comparison to HPV prevalence in individuals without cancer. Estimates
of HPV prevalence among penile cancer cases have limited use
when assessing the etiology of penile cancer without estimates of
HPV prevalence among comparable, otherwise healthy individuals
without penile cancer. To adequately estimate the association between HPV and penile cancer, analytic studies are needed, such as
case-control and cohort studies. [12]

The aim of this review and meta-analysis is to examine the
strength and consistency of the observed associations of HPV infection and the development of penile cancer. We conducted
pooled analyses of relative risk (RR) estimates from published analytic studies. The analyses are stratified by method of detection
(self-report, medical chart audit, or serologic response), HPV
strain, and type of cancer (invasive, in situ, or both) based on available published or calculated RRs. Pooled estimates are presented
and evaluated against the methods chosen by each study.

DISCUSSION

Only 9 independent studies examining the association between penile cancer and HPV were available for pooling likely because of the rarity of penile cancer. The pooled RR estimates with
HPV and invasive penile cancer ranged from 2.9 to 8.7, much
lower than the RR estimates for cervical cancer of ≥79. [32s] Past infection with HPV18 was only reported in 2 studies of invasive penile cancer that used serologic testing to purified HPV18 L1 proteins for HPV18, whereas past infection with HPV16 was reported
in 4 studies of invasive penile cancer that used serologic testing to
purified HPV16 L1 proteins or HPV16 L1 VLPs. Among the studies of penile CIS and invasive penile cancer, 6 studies used a history
of anogenital warts as an indicator of past HPVexposure. The studies least subject to recall bias are those with serologic measurement
of past HPV infection (16 or 18) and penile cancer showing up to a
4.5-fold risk of penile cancer (Table 2). This is higher than the risk
observed in previous literature for cigarette smoking, but similar to
what has been previously observed in the literature for circumcision,
which may be related to the site of the penile cancer. [17]

These pooled RR estimates are supportive of the role of
HPV in the etiology of penile cancers. The higher association seen
among the pooled data of anogenital warts (RRs of 8.7 for invasive
cancer and 7.6 for CIS or invasive cancer), based on self-report
and medical chart data (which also could be patient report), could
reflect differential recall bias related to socially undesirable sexually transmitted infection, where cases may accurately report and
controls may underreport because of social desirability. [18] Such recall bias inflates the estimate. Although the HPV types related to
warts are less likely to be carcinogenic, they are markers for risk
of developing or obtaining other HPV infections because they
are indicative of high-risk sexual behaviors (such as greater lifetime number of partners and lack of condom use) that would increase the likelihood of acquiring high-risk HPV infections, [19,46s,47s]

Therefore, one could argue that the expected risk with warts would
be equivalent or less than those for the carcinogenic types of HPV16
or HPV18. Anogenital warts are likely used as a marker of exposure
to HPV. However, because other HPV types may never result in

contrast, prevalence rates among penile cancer cases varied from

rates among noncases are not also reported. That is, it is unknown
whether noncases in that same population have the same HPV prevalence rates as cases, which would indicate that the cancers are not
due to the HPV infection, but that HPV infection is an underlying
condition. Case-control studies and cohort studies are thus essential
for showing associations. [12]

A major strength of this meta-analysis is that the studies included for the pooled analyses are case-control and cohort studies
that include HPVexposure measurement among both penile cancer
patients and otherwise healthy noncases. As has been stated previously, this allows for a better examination of the burden HPV infection contributes to penile cancer. Another strength comes from the
fact that the studies included for pooled analyses were conducted
before the availability of HPV vaccinations, and as such, the pooled
analyses are not subject to the potential confounding effects of vaccination. However, this study is not without limitations. There is a
paucity of studies that include measurement of HPV exposure in
both penile cancer cases and noncases. We are unable to examine
the associations of other high-risk HPV serotypes with penile cancer
because of this fact. However, this meta-analysis provides the first
pooled estimates RR of penile cancer after past HPV infection
and still provides an important overview of these associations given
the limited available published literature. The method of measuring
past HPV exposure also varied between studies, which may have
contributed to the heterogeneity we observed among the studies
pooled. Furthermore, the studies that used serology to measure past
HPV infections were unable to discern between different potential
anatomical sites of infection. This may have biased RR estimates
from those studies, as well as our pooled RR estimates, toward the
null. Our pooled analyses of HPV and penile cancer show a 2.9to 4.5-fold increased RR of penile cancer with HPV infection. However, because of the limited number of population-based studies
with inconsistent methods of detection, more research would
strengthen our knowledge of this association. Human papillomavirus
types 6 and 11 (the 2 strains known to cause anogenital warts), [8,47s]

have not been found to have oncogenic capacity. Human papillomavirus types 16 and 18, conversely, are responsible for approximately
50% of high-grade cervical dysplasia and 70% of cervical cancers
and are likely to be important for cancers in other anogenital areas. [27]

individually and together, because prior studies have mostly looked at
