**Discussion**


The significance of cancer development among KT recipients has long been underestimated, with graft survival

and cardiovascular diseases traditionally considered the pri
mary drivers of morbidity and mortality in these patients. [1]

However, as graft survival rates have improved and both
donors and recipients are aging, along with the influences of

immunosuppressive medications and environmental factors,

cancers are currently emerging as a relevant determinant of
transplanted patients’ morbidity. While several studies have

offered relevant information on the incidence and treat
ment outcomes of more frequent GU tumors such as kidney,

urothelial, and prostate cancers, the impact of penile and


6

testis cancers is sparsely reported in this setting. [30,31] In
this regard, the European Association of Urology on Kidney
Transplantation, Penile, and Testis cancer guidelines do not

provide any recommendation or refinement in the treatment
algorithm of these patients. [30,32,33] Thus, the data provided

in this study may help to shed light on the actual incidence,
treatment options, and burden derived from these tumors

in the KT recipient population.
According to our findings, penile and testis cancers are

rare diseases among KT recipients, with incidence rates
comparable to those observed in the general population.
In this regard, while registry studies observed that renal

graft recipients have an increased risk of developing de
novo penile and testis cancer two to fourfold higher com
pared with the general population, [11,13] other observational
studies reported no increased risk. [9] Such variability in incidence estimates may be related to several factors. First,

the transplant population usually undergoes rigorous clinical
monitoring and screening, including routine laboratory tests

and imaging, which may contribute to higher cancer detection rates and prompt treatment of precancerous lesions,

as for PeCa. However, discrepancies in follow-up strategies

across countries can lead to differences in tumors incidence

and time to diagnosis. Second, while observational studies

are generally too small to compare cancer rates after transplantation with those in the general population, a major

limitation of registry studies is the relatively short followup that may inconsistently report events that occur late

after transplantation. In this regard, the primary strength
of this study lies in its integration of data sourced from both
registries and observational studies with relatively large

follow-ups. This approach offers a more comprehensive perspective, potentially providing a more accurate depiction of

the incidence of these tumors in KT recipients. Furthermore,

this study serves as a faithful reflection of global incidence
trends, given that the included studies span different con
tinents.

Our study also highlights important findings regarding risk

factors associated with penile and testis cancer develop
ment in KT recipients. Prolonged immunosuppression has

been considered one of the most relevant risk factors since

transplanted patients were found at a higher risk of cancer
development compared to those in dialysis. In this regard,
inflammatory skin diseases such as lichen planus, phimosis,

balanitis, and genital warts are common risk factors for PeCa

development in the general population, however, immunosuppressive therapy may increase the incidence of such
inflammatory skin diseases in the KT population. Indeed,

Grulich and colleagues reported that although the HPV sub
types detected in the KT recipients were similar to those
described in immunocompetent population, immunodefi
ciency is an important risk factor for de novo appearance

of HPV-related malignancies such as PeCa (OR 15.79, CI
5.79---34.4). [34] Consequently, quadrivalent HPV vaccines have
been tested in this group of patients. Although an adequate

response has been reported, a suboptimal immune response
has been recorded after 12 mo, suggesting that KT patients

should complete vaccination before transplant to achieve a
higher antibody response. [35]

Regarding histological data, all reported cases exhibited
common histologies, with squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)

being the most frequent in PeCa and germ cell carcinoma


(GCC) in testicular cancer. [18,26,36] Notably, Reinholdt et al.
also found that grade 2---3 penile intraepithelial neoplasia
(PeIN) are more frequent (HR 21.9, 95% CI 11.1---43.5) in

comparison to non-transplanted patients. [11] Conversely, the

evidence regarding non-seminomatous germ cell tumors and

testicular lymphoma in the setting of KT is scarce and limited
to a few cases. [37,38]

Finally, in terms of clinical characteristics, most tumors
were localized, with a minority (1/33 PeCa and 3/67 testis
cancers) presenting as locally advanced at the time of

diagnosis. These findings hold significance as they influ
ence potential treatment approaches. While the surgical

treatment of primary tumors remains consistent with that

of the general population for both penile and testis cancers, the management of locally advanced cases may
differ because of the graft. [32,33,39] Specifically, while inguinal

nodal disease doesn’t pose added risks for transplanted

patients with PeCa, the management of testis cancer
with abdominal/retroperitoneal diseases does. Prophylac

lymphnode dissection for treatment of disseminated seminoma can present a problem with injury to pelvic kidney

grafts and the development of radiotherapy-induced nephri

recipients has prompted healthcare professionals and policymakers to address the development of screening and

treatment strategies tailored to this vulnerable population.
According to our study, owing to a lack of direct evidence

and the low incidence of penile and testis cancers in kidney

transplant recipients, general screening protocols are currently not recommended in this setting. There is insufficient

data to guide clinicians to deviate from current treatment
