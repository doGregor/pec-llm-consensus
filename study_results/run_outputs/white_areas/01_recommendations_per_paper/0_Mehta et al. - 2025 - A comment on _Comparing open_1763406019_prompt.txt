## Letter to the Editor
# A comment on: ‘Comparing open and video endoscopic lymphadenectomy for penile cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective studies’


Dear Editor,
We would like to acknowledge and thank the authors of
the systematic review and meta-analysis titled ‘Comparing
open and video endoscopic lymphadenectomy for penile
cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective
studies’ [1] for their contribution to research on the evidencebased management of inguinal lymphadenectomy for penile
cancer. The comparison of video endoscopic inguinal
lymphadenectomy (VEIL) with open inguinal
lymphadenectomy (OIL) has indeed provided useful
information regarding the peri-operative benefits of the
former technique. We would like to thank the authors for
their deep dive into details such as oncological operating
times, peri-operative complication rates, drainage duration,
length of hospital admission, and overall surgical outcomes,
as these have important implications for the clinical
management of penile cancer. Nonetheless, we would like to
address some points that could be strengthened in the
discussion in order to enhance the accuracy of clinical
conclusions when applying the complete evidence-based
approach.

The meta-analysis encompassed three randomised controlled
trials (RCTs) and one prospective cohort study. It may not
have been appropriate to include different study designs, such
as RCTs alongside cohort studies, within a meta-analysis.
This is because RCTs, arguably because of their minimisation
of biases due to randomisation and control, are deemed the
‘gold standard’ in clinical research, whereas cohort studies,
although useful, typically have a greater risk of bias, especially
when there are confounding factors. Hence, blending the
findings of these two approaches as a synthesis might lead to
inconsistencies and compromise the trustworthiness of the
overall evidence. In order to resolve this concern, we
recommend that the authors conduct a sensitivity analysis by
removing the cohort study to examine the effect of its
removal on the robustness of conclusions drawn solely from
the RCTs [2]. Such an analysis would increase the confidence
derived from the outcomes and substantially help to guide
clinicians in their interpretation of the data.

The authors have performed a risk of bias assessment. An
assessment of the certainty of evidence using the Grading of
Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations


(GRADE) approach, in addition, could have been conducted
to determine the certainty of evidence for each outcome [3].
The GRADE system is important in assessing the quality of
evidence because it explains in clear terms how conclusions
are made based on the data presented. With the application
of GRADE, the authors would have been able to assign an
evidence grade for each outcome, such as incidence of wound
infections, skin necrosis and lymphoedema, and how these
outcomes impact clinical practice. This would aid clinicians in
evaluating the degree of trust to place in the findings and
assist in making informed decisions. The meta-analysis is of
clinical relevance; however, applying the GRADE system
would have enhanced the credibility of the evidence, enabling
practitioners to use the findings in a more reliable way.

Although the study compares VEIL and OIL in depth, it is
critical to note that long-term oncological outcomes, such as
recurrence and survival, were insufficiently covered owing to
the short follow-up periods in the studies. We do appreciate
the issues created by the scarcity of penile cancer and the
logistical problems these cancers present, but recommend that
future work focus on the long-term effects of VEIL on
recurrence and survival. This focus would enhance
understanding of the technique’s oncological advantages.

# Response to Mehta et al.

We appreciate the thoughtful comments provided by Mehta
et al. [1]. We would like to clarify that, at all times, our
intention was never to conduct a meta-analysis based solely
on randomised controlled trials (RCTs). As is well known,
the number of available RCTs on this topic is still limited,
often involving few patients and resulting in forest plots with

—
only two studies which compromises the statistical power
and the strength of any conclusions [2].

For this reason, we chose to include both high-quality RCTs
and prospective cohort studies, ensuring a more
comprehensive and representative analysis of the available
evidence. We emphasise that the conclusions of our work are
appropriate within this context, and the methodology was
carefully based on prospective studies.

Thus enabling the performance of a meta-analysis with
sufficient statistical power based solely on prospective studies.
At present, this meta-analysis, which includes prospective
studies (Level 1 evidence), represents the most reliable
evidence available to guide our clinical practice and to offer
our patients the best possible treatment based on the current
clinical data.

We would also like to share an excerpt from our manuscript
that supports this approach:

In the present meta-analysis of high-quality RCTs and
prospective cohorts, VEIL had significantly fewer
wound infections, skin necrosis, and lymphoedema. The
shorter drainage time, lower intensity and duration of
pain, and fewer postoperative complications seem to be
related to a shorter hospital stay.
