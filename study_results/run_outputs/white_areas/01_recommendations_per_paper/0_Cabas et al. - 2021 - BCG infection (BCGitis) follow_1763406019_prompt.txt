Abstract

Objective: Intravesical Bacillus Calmette-Gu�erin (BCG) is an effective treatment in non−−muscle-invasive bladder cancer, however,
extravesical BCG infection may occur in remote organs as a potentially serious complication. Researchers aimed to assess whether a different timing of BCG infection after intravesical administration of BCG could be identified and estimated for each single involved organ.
Methods: We performed a systematic literature review over systemic and genitourinary BCG infection case reports, including 271 published
case reports for a total of 307 patients. Demographic data, clinical features, and timing of BCG infection development were collected and
analyzed for each patient. Results: BCG infection developed with a different timing from last instillation, depending on the involved organ.
Among the genitourinary complications, penile lesions occurred as early as 1 (1;3) weeks, while orchiepididymitis occurred as late as 56
(6.25;156) weeks. At the same time, granulomatous hepatitis and lungs involvement such as miliary pulmonary BCG infection occurred earlier, with a median time of 1 (1;4) and 1 (1;6) weeks respectively, whereas vascular, osteoarticular, and muscular complications developed
with a median timing from last instillation of 52 (20;104), 68 (14;156), and 93 (29;156) weeks, respectively. The analysis detected a cluster
between lungs, liver, and bone marrow complications on one side and muscular and osteoarticular or vascular complications on the other
side was also observed. Conclusions: BCG infection after intravesical BCG for bladder cancer may develop even several months or years
after the last instillation, depending on the involved organs. When BCG infection interests one or more organ, 2 main associative patterns
are common: one involving lungs, liver, and bone marrow, with earlier occurrence but lower rates of microbiological diagnosis achievement, and one involving muscular and osteoarticular or vascular districts, with later occurrence but higher rates of microbiological evidence.

2. Materials and methods

The authors performed a systematic review of literature
in order to identify single or multiple, English-language
case reports which could describe timing of BCG infection
development after last intravesical instillation from single
patients, with no publication year limits. After case reports
identification, the authors collected data regarding oncological, pathological, diagnostic, and therapeutic features, with
particular emphasis on timing of BCG infection onset from
last intravesical instillation.

Before performing the literature research, the leading
research team composed by an Academic Infectious Disease Specialist, an Academic Urologist, an Academic Statistician and a Urology Resident independently identified
and tested different queries in order to optimize pertinence
and sensitivity of the literature search results. Review protocol has been recorded on June 4, 2020 on PROSPERO
with ID 190337, and is currently being assessed. Systematic
review was conducted according to PRISMA guidelines.
Since authors included in the research results both genitourinary and systemic complications, 2 different queries
were identified: “(miliary OR infection OR infective OR
dissemination OR disseminated OR tuberculosis) AND
(BCG OR BCGitis OR Calmette-Gu�erin OR Calmette Guerin) AND (cancer OR bladder OR urothelial OR vesical OR
instillation OR endovesical OR intravesical)” for systemic
BCG infection and “(prostate OR prostatic OR prostatitis)
OR (orchis OR epididymis OR testis OR orchitis OR orchiepididymitis OR testicle OR testicular) OR (penile OR
penis OR glans OR balanitis OR scrotum OR scrotal OR
balanoposthitis) OR (kidney OR nephritis OR pyelonephritis OR pyelitis OR renal) AND (BCG OR bcgitis OR Calmette-Guerin OR Calmette Guerin)” for genitourinary BCG
infection. On June 4, 2020 a PubMed search was performed


with the first OR the second query. A complimentary search
was performed on June 8, 2020 by checking the references
of the previously identified case reports, since the majority
of the authors had reported references of previous case
reports of the same organ BCG infection. A final update
was made the day before submission.
The database included the following items: (1) biographical data; (2) tumor features, including pathological staging
and grading; (3) number of instillations performed and time
from last instillations (TLI) to first symptom development;
(4) involved organ/s; (5) type of imaging performed; (6)
type of microbiological, histological, and/or clinical diagnosis achievement; (7) surgical and/or medical treatment,
including duration of antimycobacterial therapy; (8)
patient’s outcome.
Data are displayed as mean (standard deviation) or
median (quartile 1;quartile 3), given the distribution results
of Shapiro-Wilk’s test. The nature of the current study predisposes to the problem of missing at random data, whose
main consequence is the substantial loss of statistical
power. In particular the overall rate of missing data ranged
from 10% to 84.3% [7]. Therefore, authors decided to display data in a descriptive fashion and not to perform statistical analysis to detect any intergroup differences. However,
under the assumption that TLI and the number of instillations were missing at random, authors used univariate multiple imputation to create several (number of
imputation = 5) complete versions of time from last instillation and the number of instillations by replacing their missing values with plausible data values [8]. In particular,
multiple imputations were performed using fully conditional specification, and the 2 variables of interest were
imputed via linear regression modeling [9]. In addition,
authors measured the strength of association between the
concomitant occurrence of systemic complications using
Cramer’s V, whose coefficient can be interpreted as follows: >0.25, very strong association; >0.15, strong association; >0.10, moderate association; >0.05, weak association;
<0.05, no or very weak association [10]. For all analyses,
two-sided statistical significance was defined as P< 0.05.
Data were analyzed using SPSS (statistical package for
social science) version 25.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics for MAC
OS. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.).

4. Discussion

Results of this systematic review support the following
statements. As shown in Fig. 2, penile lesions, lung BCG
infection and granulomatous hepatitis tend to be early complications of BCG instillation, being more likely to occur
after a short TLI. On the contrary, testicular, vascular, muscular, and osteoarticular complications tend to be late complications. Finally prostatic abscesses, bone marrow
fibrosis, and kidney granulomas showed an intermediate
trend. Our results are in line with the hypothesis firstly
advanced by Gonzales et al. [6], who identified liver and
lungs as early sites of infection (“early presentation disease”) and testicles as a later site of infection (“late presentation disease”). Clinicians should be aware that, while
some BCG infectious complications after intravesical instillation for NMIBC occur earlier and are easier to detect,
others may occur even several months after the last instillation. In addition, a history compatible with intravesical
BCG instillation should always be investigated due to the
possible requirement of an antituberculosis treatment in
parallel to the surgical treatment of such complications.
Also, an early diagnosis could avoid the need of surgery in


some cases [11]. In detail, vascular surgeons should be
aware that BCG mycotic aneurysms or pseudoaneurysms
and endoprosthesis graft infections may occur up to some
years after the last BCG instillation, and similarly should be
orthopedics surgeons with BCG prosthetic joint infections
and urologist with drug resistant epididymo-orchitis, sometimes requiring orchiectomy for turning into an abscess or
because a cancer is suspected (Fig. 3).
The lack of positive microbiological data should not discourage clinicians to consider BCG infection as a differential diagnosis, since microbiological evidence was present
in only 55.6% of patients, depending on the involved
organs. However, in late-occurring infectious complications, when BCG infection is suspected, microbiological
data might be obtained in up to 83.6% of patients with vascular involvement and 91.7% of patients with osteoarticular
involvement (Fig. 4), therefore in these cases it should be
mandatory for mycobacteria research in surgical samples.
At the same time, it is more difficult to achieve a microbiological diagnosis in lungs (35.2%) or liver (31.5%) complications, but being these early complications it is also easier
for the clinician to identify a clinical link with intravesical
BCG administration. However, in patients where a microbiological diagnosis could not be obtained, histology showing
granulomas was crucial for a definitive diagnosis [12,13].
In 28% of included case reports, more than one organ
was involved in the same patient at the same time (Table 1).

Cramer’s analysis showed an association between lungs,
liver, and bone marrow complications on one side and
between vascular, muscular, and osteoarticular districts,
meaning that there are 2 main clusters of systemic BCG
infectious complications: one involving lungs, liver, and
bone marrow, with an earlier occurrence but a lower rate of
microbiological detecting, and one involving vascular, muscular and osteoarticular districts, with a later occurrence but
a higher rate of microbiological detection (Table 2).
Interestingly, only 4 patients (1.3%) were female.
According to European epidemiological data, the age-standardized incidence rate (per 100,000 person/years) is 19.1
for men and 4.0 for women [14]. Therefore, higher incidence of BCG infection in females should have been

expected. This observation sustains the hypothesis that
BCG infection could be related to a traumatic preprocedure
urethral catheterization [1], which anatomically may impact
men more frequently [15].
In terms of treatment options and outcomes, neither trials
assessing optimal therapy for BCG infection, nor an established consensus exist. Mycobacterium bovis is intrinsically
resistant to pyrazinamide, therefore, it seems reasonable to
treat infected patients with 2 months of isoniazid plus
rifampin plus ethambutol, followed by 7 months of isoniazid plus rifampin [11,16]. In terms of BCG-instillation
related death, attributable mortality was estimated to be
10% in systemic BCG infections and 3% in genitourinary
BCG infection [16]. In our systematic review the higher
mortality rate was observed with vascular complications
(15.4%), while no mortality was reported among genitourinary complications.
The main limitation of this study regards the not insignificant issue related to the data source (i.e., case reports).
This led to a considerable number of MAR data (e.g.,
31.1% of missing data regarding the presence or absence of
granulomas on biopsy). However, BCG infection following
BCG intravesical administration is a very uncommon complication, and it is virtually impossible to collect data on a
single or multi-institutional series as proved by the low incidence rates of the Danish nationwide series. In addition, it is
not possible to exclude that the time of presentation of complications may have influenced the effective number of
reported cases. However, the aim of this research is to
make physicians consider possible cases of organ BCG
infection, when their patients underwent BCG intravesical
treatment, and that TLI may help in differential diagnosis.
Also, the authors chose to not perform a statistical analysis
on prevalence and relative incidence of the single organ
involved, since these data could be subject to a publication
bias: for example, we only found 8 case reports of bladder
BCG infection, probably a most common infectious complication of intravesical BCG [4]. A further limitation of
the study is the possibility of a selection bias, meaning that
some eligible case reports could result excluded from the
database due to limitations of the query. Therefore, authors
decided to perform a complimentary search after the first


selection to reduce this bias. Finally, it should be considered
that different timing of symptoms onset could be attributed
to the time needed for the development of abscess and granulomas in the various organs.

5. Conclusions

Infectious BCG complications after intravesical BCG
administration for NMIBC develop with different timing
according to the site of infection: lungs, liver, and penile
complications occur earlier; whereas, testicular, muscular,
vascular, and osteoarticular district complications occur
later; kidney, bone marrow and prostate complications
show an intermediate trend. Also, more than one organ
may be interested in systemic BCG infection with 2 main
clusters: one involving lungs, liver, and bone marrow,
with earlier occurrence but lower rate of microbiological
diagnosis achievement, and one involving muscular and
osteoarticular or vascular district, with later occurrence but
higher rate of microbiological diagnosis achievement.
Clinicians should be aware of these patterns, and clinical
suspicion of BCG infection should be kept in mind even if
several months or years have passed since the last BCG
instillation.
