Abstract

Context: A dynamic sentinel lymph node biopsy (DSNB) is used for inguinal lymph node
staging in patients presenting with primary penile cancer with clinically impalpable
inguinal lymph nodes (cN0). Further completion radical inguinal lymph node dissection
(RLND) is offered to patients diagnosed with metastatic inguinal nodes following a
DSNB.

Objective: To identify the diagnostic accuracy of a DSNB and RLND to detect lymph node
metastasis.

Evidence acquisition: A systematic literature search was performed on PubMed
Medscape, EMBASE, Google Scholar, and Cochrane database for relevant studies between
January 1, 2000 and April 30, 2022. Studies were included if a DSNB was offered to cN0
patients and if RLND was offered to patients with positive histology at the DSNB. Risk of
bias and confounding assessments were performed. Diagnostic odds ratio, pooled sensitivity/specificity, and heterogeneity analyses were displayed.
Evidence synthesis: Overall, 2893 patients in the 28 studies analysed underwent a DSNB.
Twenty-one studies reported patients undergoing RLND for a positive DSNB. A DSNB
showed pooled weighted sensitivity of 0.87 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.82–0.91)
and pooled log diagnostic odds ratio of 6.17 (95% CI: 5.47–6.87). A DSNB showed pooled
weighted sensitivity of 0.50 (95% CI: 0.24–0.59), specificity of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.78–0.87),
and a log diagnostic odds ratio of 1.18 (95% CI: 0.29–2.97) for detecting further positive
lymph nodes at RLND. High heterogeneity, mostly caused by study design, characteristics of the included patients, DSNB technique used, surgical experience/centre case volumes, and follow-up schedule, represents the main limitation of this meta-analysis.
Several sensitivity analyses to address these issues support our findings.
Conclusions: A positive DSNB is poorly able to discriminate which patients will have further metastatic involvement at completion RLND. Better stratification of these patients
would avoid unnecessary overtreatment and possible postoperative comorbidities.
Patient summary: This analysis of the data from the published literature provides evidence that the diagnostic accuracy of a dynamic sentinel lymph node biopsy (DSNB)

1. Introduction

Penile cancer accounts for <1% of cancers with an overall
incidence of <1.00/100 000 males in Europe and the USA,
with India, south-east Asia, South America, and SubSaharan Africa being the areas with the highest incidence

[1–3]. Tumour cells from primary penile cancer follow a
stepwise predictable pattern of spread, firstly into the inguinal lymph nodes followed by the pelvic lymph nodes [4].
Preoperative cross-sectional staging scans such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging
(MRI) are still unreliable regarding the detection of micrometastatic disease and/or lymph node invasion <10 mm in
size [4]. Historical series that have carried out radical lymph
node dissection (RLND) in an unselected population with
penile cancer showed that patients with cN0 disease have
a 15–20% risk of harbouring micrometastatic disease in
the inguinal lymph nodes [5]. To reduce the morbidity of
RLND in cN0 patients, risk-stratified approaches combined
with a dynamic sentinel lymph node biopsy (DSNB) have
been proposed [1,2,6]. Completion RLND is performed
whenever metastatic disease is detected at a DSNB. In this
context, the added value of proceeding with completion
RLND is still unclear, especially in the case of micrometastatic disease regarding the risk of further metastatic disease in the remaining inguinal lymph nodes. The aim of
this systematic review and meta-analysis was to determine
the diagnostic accuracy of a DSNB to detect inguinal lymph
node metastases in patients with cN0 penile cancer. The
secondary aim was to determine the diagnostic accuracy
of a DSNB in predicting further metastasis in the remaining
inguinal lymph nodes when patients proceed to completion
RLND after a positive DSNB.

2. Evidence acquisition

2.1. Search strategy

A systematic review of the literature was performed to
select cohort studies or randomised controlled trials on sen
tinel lymph node biopsy for penile cancer according to the
Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and
Meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement (Supplementary material) [7]. A literature search of full-text English-language
articles on DSNB was performed using PubMed, Medscape,
EMBASE, and Google Scholar on the April 30, 2022, with
the search terms ((((((((penile AND (sentinel AND (lymph
node OR biopsy) OR lymph nodes)) ‘‘English‘‘ [Language]))
AND (‘‘2000’’ [Date - Publication]: ‘‘2022‘‘ [Date - Publication]))) NOT ‘‘review’’ [Publication Type]) NOT ‘‘letter‘‘

[Publication Type]) NOT ‘‘meta-analysis’’ [Publication
Type]) AND cancer (see the Supplementary material) for
all studies published between January 1, 2000 and April


30, 2022. A literature search using the Cochrane database
up to the April 30, 2022 was performed post hoc with the
same search criteria.

2.2. Study selection and data collection

The PICO protocol used the following combinations:

1. Participants: patients with penile cancer and impalpable
bilateral inguinal lymph nodes (cN0).
2. Interventions: DSNB ± completion radical inguinal lymph
node dissection.
3. Comparison: null.
4. Outcomes: cancer lymph node involvement at DSNB
(first meta-analysis) and RLND (second meta-analysis).

The inclusion criteria were full-text English-only articles
with exclusion of conference abstracts, editorials, cover letters, and reviews.
The inclusion criteria for the first meta-analysis on diagnostic accuracy of a DSNB were as follows: (1) DSNB offered
to all cN0 patients, (2) data regarding the numbers of total
procedures, true positives, true negatives, and false negatives (see the Supplementary material), (3) more than five
participants in a single suitable study, and (4) use of radiotracers and/or blue dyes for sentinel lymph node mapping.
True positives for a DSNB were defined as histological
confirmation of cancer spread to groin sentinel lymph
node(s). True negatives were defined as histological confirmation of absence of cancer spread to groin sentinel lymph
node(s) and no evidence (at clinical examination and/or
imaging and/or histological evaluation) of lymph nodes
metastasis within a 12-month period from a DSNB. The
12-month cut-off was chosen as a commonly accepted
threshold for persistent versus recurrent disease [1]. If
follow-up data on inguinal lymph-node metastasis after a
DSNB were not specifically reported at the 12-month
follow-up after the DSNB but at a longer follow-up time
point, no evidence of lymph nodes metastasis within the
12-months period was considered for the analysis. False
negatives were defined as the occurrence of groin lymph
node(s) metastasis (at clinical examination and/or imaging
and/or histological evaluation after a further DSNB or RLND)
after a negative DSNB within a 12-month period from the
DSNB. False positives are not possible for a DSNB and by
definition specificity is 100%. In fact, a histological confirmation of cancer spread to groin lymph node(s) at a DSNB
cannot be falsified. In addition, if the node was positive only
on lymphoscintigraphy and/or intraoperative staining and
not on histopathological testing, this would have been a
false positive for the lymphoscintigraphy and/or intraoperative staining (which however are not cancer specific and

thus suggest only the first draining lymph-node[s] from the
penis), but not for the DSNB.
The second meta-analysis on the diagnostic accuracy of
the DSNB in finding further metastatic lymph nodes at
RLND was conducted on the same studies of the first
meta-analysis that fulfilled the inclusion criteria for this
second meta-analysis. The inclusion criteria for the second
meta-analysis were the following: (1) RLND offered to all
patients with positive cancer spread to the sentinel inguinal
lymph node/s (positive histology at a DSNB); (2) data
regarding the numbers of total procedures, number of true
positives, number of true negatives, and number of false
negatives at the DSNB (see the Supplementary material);
(3) more than five participants undergoing a DSNB in a single suitable study; and (4) precise data regarding the number of total procedures, and number of patients with
negative and/or positive histology after RLND.
True positives for the second meta-analysis were defined
as positive histological confirmation of cancer spread to
groin lymph node/s after a positive DSNB other than the
nodes already known to be positive at a DSNB. True negatives were defined as histological confirmation of absence
of cancer spread to groin sentinel lymph node(s) and no evidence (at clinical examination and/or imaging and/or histological evaluation after a further DSNB or RLND) of lymph
node metastasis within a 12-month period from the DSNB.
False negatives were defined as the occurrence of groin
lymph node(s) metastasis (at clinical examination and/or
imaging and/or histological evaluation after a further DSNB
or RLND) after a negative DSNB within a 12-month period
from the DSNB. False positives were defined as patients
with positive histological confirmation at a DSNB but with
negative histology at RLND.

2.3. Study selection and data collection

Each title and each abstract were screened independently
by three authors (E.P., O.O.C., and G.F.). Full-text articles
were reviewed for inclusion, and data were extracted by
three authors independently (E.P., O.O.C., and G.F.). Data
extracted were the numbers of true positives, true negatives, false positives, false negatives, and patients for each
outcome, and the year of publication. Information about
immunohistochemistry staining and usage of lymphoscintigraphy for preoperative sentinel lymph node visualisation was also assessed. In addition, data on DSNB
procedure, patients’ characteristics, and surgeon’s expertise
in DSNB and RLND (if available) were collected. A complete
list of items collected is given in Supplementary Table 1.
Disagreements were resolved by discussion and consensus
with all the other authors (G.F., Z.T., H.M.A., A.S., and A.M.).

2.4. Assessment of risk of bias

Three authors evaluated the quality of the studies independently (E.P., O.O.C., and G.F.). Literature quality was evaluated according to guideline recommendation using Quality
Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies—2 (QUADAS-2)

[8]. It evaluates two sections: the risk of bias that uses four
domains regarding patient selection, index test (DSNB), reference standard (pathology report), and flow/timing of
patients through the studies, and the risks of applicability
that uses three domains regarding patient selection, index


test (DSNB), and reference standard (pathology report) of
the studies. Any disagreement between the three reviewers
was resolved via consensus with the help of a third reviewer
(either H.M.A. or A.M.).

2.5. Data analysis

A univariate meta-analysis was used to estimate pooled
sensitivity, specificity, and log diagnostic odds ratio (DOR)
for both DSNB and RLND procedures. Given that false positives are by definition zero for a DSNB, specificity is always
100% and therefore it has not been reported in the first
meta-analysis on DSNB diagnostic accuracy. DOR was used
as a global measure for determining the diagnostic accuracy
and discriminative power of a DSNB and RLND. Chi-square
and I [2] tests were used to assess the heterogeneity of sensitivities and specificities, respectively. A sensitivity analysis
to assess the robustness of the results in the case of accuracy (area under the curve) >0.70 was performed by excluding the studies with the worst and the best performance [9].
To address the problem of the high-volume centre and
learning curve, a sensitivity analysis was performed including centres reporting at least 30 procedures, with 30 procedures being an accepted cut-off for a DSNB learning curve

[10]. In addition, given the risk of possible overlapping in
case series, especially in case of multicentric studies, we
performed a sensitivity analysis excluding those studies
that the authors believed might be duplicated. Finally, a
sensitivity analysis including only prospective studies was
performed. Forest plots were used for graphical representation, while funnel plots were used to assess the publication
bias. Statistical analysis was performed using R studio Inc.
(2016; Boston, MA, USA) integrated development environment for R software version 3.5.3. Forest plots for weighted
sensitivity, specificity, and DOR were plotted using the
package ‘‘mada’’.

3. Evidence synthesis

3.1. Results

Overall, 6026 works were found through the literature
search; 28 studies were finally included in the review
(Fig. 1) [10–37]. The study by Dell’Oglio et al [37] was added
post hoc. Details are provided in Table 1. The earliest study
was published in 2000 and the latest in 2022. Overall, 2893
patients underwent a DSNB. Of these patients, 518 had positive histology at the pathological report after a DSNB. Fourteen studies were prospective and 14 retrospective,
including patients from all over the world (mostly from Europe and North/South America). The median age of the
patients included in the studies ranged from 48 to 69 years.
All patients had high-risk penile cancer with impalpable
lymph nodes apart from 15.7% of the patients from the work
of Lee et al [36], who had palpable lymph nodes but with
negative fine needle aspiration histology. In one study by
Rohan and Tankshali [22], only blue dye was used; in all
the other studies, 99 [m] Tc-nanocolloid radiotracer was used
either alone or in combination with blue dye, whereas in
two studies fluorescent indocyanine green was used

[26,37].

3.1.3. Meta-analysis of the diagnostic accuracy of sentinel
lymph node metastatic disease to detect further inguinal lymph
node involvement at completion RLND
Among the 27 studies in the meta-analysis, 21 reported the
histological findings of the completion RLND in patients
with lymph node metastatic disease diagnosed on a DSNB.
These included 1691 patients, and 65 of them had metastatic inguinal lymph nodes at RLND other than the one(s)
found at the DSNB. The pooled weighted sensitivity was
0.50 (95% CI: 0.24–0.59; Fig. 4A). Heterogeneity for sensitivity, measured with v [2] tests ( v [2] = 48.14; p = 0.004) and I [2]

statistic (10.2%), was low. The pooled weighted specificity
was 0.82 (95% CI: 0.78–0.87; Fig. 4B). Heterogeneity for
specificity, measured with v [2] tests ( v [2] = 78.18; p < 0.001)
and I [2] statistic (58.7%), was moderate. The pooled log DOR

3.2. Discussion

In this meta-analysis, we aimed to identify the diagnostic
accuracy of a DSNB in detecting lymph node metastasis in
cN0 penile cancer patients. The weighted pooled sensitivity
of a DSNB to detect a positive lymph node was 0.87 (95% CI:
0.82–0.91), thus bearing a slight risk of having false negative results. False negatives translate into underdetection
of metastatic disease, leading to cancer undertreatment;
in this regard, our results support the importance of careful
follow-up after a negative DSNB. In addition, we aimed to
identify the accuracy of a DSNB in finding further metastatic
disease in inguinal lymph nodes when patients are submitted to completion RLND after a positive DSNB. We identified
that the sensitivity of detecting further metastasis at completion RLND was poor (0.50; 95% CI: 0.24–0.59), while
specificity was higher (0.82; 95% CI: 0.78–0.87). This means
that it is difficult to predict further lymph node metastases
at completion RLND after a positive DSNB; as such, a positive sentinel lymph node is poorly informative regarding a
further lymph node spread.
In this context, several studies have demonstrated that
surgical removal of regional lymph nodes ameliorates
patients’ survival [38–40]. A DSNB has emerged as an extremely valuable tool to identify early inguinal metastatic disease in penile cancer patients with cN0 inguinal nodes. In
fact, over the past two decades, compelling evidence has
accumulated regarding the feasibility and diagnostic accuracy of a DSNB. As nomograms are unreliable to predict


inguinal lymph node invasion, a DSNB plays a fundamental
role in tumour staging [41,42]. In more detail, two systematic reviews and meta-analyses, published in 2011 and in
2016, respectively, evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of a
DSNB among published studies, showing pooled sensitivities of 88% and 92%, respectively, which are in line with
our findings [43,44]. Of note, meta-analyses on diagnostic
accuracy can be challenging to conduct and be interpreted;
this is mainly due to the high heterogeneity of the included
studies. One of the main sources of heterogeneity is the definition of false negatives at a DSNB. This definition can be
difficult to address; here, we defined a false negative result
as an inguinal node recurrence within 12 months after a
negative DSNB, even though a proper consensus on this definition is still not met. Most of the included studies reported
different diagnostic and follow-up schemes, contributing to
a large source of heterogeneity. Other factors contributing
to data heterogeneity were the surgeons’ learning curve
and the use of immunohistochemical staining techniques
to detect micrometastatic disease (<2 mm) at a DSNB [45].
In this context, the surgeon learning curve was analysed
by Leijte et al [46] who demonstrated that a DSNB was a
suitable procedure to stage cN0 penile cancer with a low
complication rate and more importantly with no learning
curve. On the contrary, Zou et al [44] performed a metaregression and subgroup analysis, which revealed that the
use of a preoperative ultrasound scan combined with fineneedle aspiration cytology of morphologically abnormal

came from tertiary referral centres where expertise on the
DSNB procedure is supposed to be solid. Of note, at the sensitivity analysis focused on high-volume centres only,
pooled sensitivity was not different from that of the main
analysis.
In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we also
selected studies that presented data on completion RLND
after positive histology at a DSNB (n = 21). We aimed to
determine DSNB sensitivity and specificity for finding further metastatic lymph nodes at completion RLND other
than the one(s) positive at a DSNB. In routine clinical practice, positive histology at a DSNB is followed by a completion inguinal lymphadenectomy to diagnose and remove
metastatic disease in the remaining inguinal lymph node
packet [46–48]. It should be noted that the postoperative
morbidity after RLND can be up to 50% [39,47,49,50],
including wound infections (1.2–1.4%), skin necrosis (0.6–
4.7%), lymphoedema (5–13.9%), and lymphocele formation
(2.1–4%) [38,39,49,50]. Negative histology at RLND gives a
favourable prognosis, but RLND might drastically decrease
the patients’ quality of life [49]. Several studies have tried
to assess the feasibility and outcomes of RLND by using
laparoscopic or robotic surgical techniques, but no substantial differences have been found compared with the standard surgery [51–53]. Moreover, it has been demonstrated


that patients with a single metastatic inguinal lymph node
(pN1) achieved comparable 5-year survival of patients with
pN0 [54]. In our study, the pooled weighted sensitivity for a
DSNB in finding further positive lymph nodes at completion
RLND was 0.50 (95% CI: 0.24–0.59), with a pooled log DOR
of 1.18 (95% CI: 0.29–2.97) indicating low sensitivity and
poor diagnostic accuracy. In other terms, our findings highlight a possible overtreatment of a certain subpopulations of
cN0 patients undergoing completion RLND after a positive
DSNB. Considering the high morbidity rate after RLND, better methods to stratify patients who deserve completion
RLND following a DSNB are warranted. Indeed, a large proportion of patients with a positive DSNB are likely undergoing unnecessary completion RLND, whereas strict
surveillance might be a strategy in very selected groups of
patients. In this regard, new markers for lymph node invasion at RLND (among cN0 patients) to guide clinicians
towards a more precise indication for completion RLND
are certainly needed. Apart from well-known features associated with cancer aggressiveness (tumour grade, stage,
lymphovascular invasion, perineural invasion, and histological subtypes), some authors suggested to incorporate
biomarkers such as p53, Ki-67, E-cadherin, matrix
metalloproteinase-9, and squamous cell carcinoma antigen
to improve the prediction of lymph node involvement.
However, none of these has been implemented in the current clinical practice [55–57].
As previously discussed, the main limitations of our
meta-analysis are the heterogeneity in study design, characteristics of the included patients, DSNB technique, surgi

cal experience/centre case volume, and follow-up set-up. In
addition, the definition used for a false negative is not standardised; however, the use of a 12-month cut-off represents
a good endpoint to differentiate the false negative finding at
a DSNB from recurrence considering the aggressiveness of
penile cancer. It was impossible to use earlier cut-off, for
example, 6 months, because of the lack of data at this time
point. Finally, the risk of bias of the studies included in this
meta-analysis is not negligible. This is mainly due to the retrospective nature of some of the included studies, absence
of randomisation, absence of blinding to results, differences
in instrumental issues and surgical technique for both DSNB
and RLND, presence of loss to follow-up, and missing data.
To our knowledge, this is the most up-to-date systematic
review and meta-analysis investigating the diagnostic accuracy of a DSNB and the first to assess its diagnostic accuracy
in finding other lymph node metastases at completion
RLND in the context of men with cN0 penile cancer.

4. Conclusions

False negatives after a DSNB are still an issue, thus highlighting the importance of strict follow-up following DSNB.
In addition, a DSNB is poorly informative in detecting further lymph node metastases at completion RLND despite
good specificity, meaning that a high proportion of patients
with no additional lymph node metastasis undergo unnecessary RLND. To avoid overtreatment and severe postoperative comorbidities, better stratification of patients with
positive metastatic inguinal lymph node(s) at a DSNB is
needed. Unfortunately, heterogeneity of the included studies might weaken the certainty of our results.
