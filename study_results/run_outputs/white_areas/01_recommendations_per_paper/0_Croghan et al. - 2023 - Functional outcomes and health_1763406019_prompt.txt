# **Functional outcomes and health-related quality of life** **following penile cancer surgery: a comprehensive review**

**Stefanie M. Croghan, MSc, MRCSI** **[1,2,*]** **, Ivor M. Cullen, MCh, FRCS Urol** **[2,3]** **, Omer Raheem, MD** **[4]**

1 Department of Surgery, Royal College of Surgeons, Dublin D02 YN77, Ireland

4 Department of Surgery, Section of Urology, University of Chicago Medicine, Chicago, IL 60637, United States

**Abstract**

**Introduction:** Organ-sparing surgery (OSS) in penile cancer management aspires to maintain organ form and function and preserve health-related
quality of life (HRQoL), yet there is a lack of integrated evidence exploring these outcomes.
**Objectives:** The aim sought to review HRQoL, functional, aesthetic, and psychological outcomes following OSS or radical penectomy for penile

cancer.
**Methods:** A systematic review of MEDLINE and Cochrane databases included studies reporting on function (sexual, urinary or sensory), genital
appearance or HRQoL/psychological well-being following surgical treatment of primary penile cancer. English-language reports (2000-2022),
incorporating patient-reported or objective clinical outcome measures, were eligible. Studies of nonsurgical treatment strategies and those in
the context of metastatic disease were excluded. Data were compiled and analyzed.
**Results:** Twenty-six studies were included. Sexual function was the most studied outcome (754 pooled respondents; 19 studies), most frequently
with the original 15-item and abridged 5-item International Index of Erectile Function. Preservation of erectile function following OSS is generally
described, with some reduction in overall sexual satisfaction cited. Heterogeneous assessment of voiding function with little preoperative
evaluation render interstudy comparison difficult. Most patients appear able to void from a standing position following OSS, with spraying the
most common symptom. Maintenance of some sensory function is described with both split-thickness skin grafting and urethral glanduloplasty
following radical glansectomy. Limited studies suggest reasonable patient satisfaction with genital cosmesis post-OSS. A negative impact on
HRQoL is described in most studies following penile cancer surgery, variably correlated with aggressiveness of penile surgery and addition of
lymphadenectomy. Anxiety, depression, and reduced self-esteem have been reported in penile cancer survivors. Relationship well-being varies,
with some survivors reporting this to be unchanged.
**Conclusion:** OSS can preserve elements of sexual, urinary, and sensory function, supporting advantages over radical penectomy for eligible
patients. However, a comprehensive understanding remains limited due to small, heterogeneous patient cohorts, challenges in obtaining
premorbid data, and variability in outcome measures. Standardization of patient-reported outcomes following OSS is desirable.

**Keywords:** patient-reported outcome measures.; quality of life; penile function; penile reconstruction; organ sparing; phallus preservation; penile cancer.


**Introduction**

Penile cancer is classified as a rare malignancy, with an incidence of <0.5 to 7.26 per 100 000 person years; the incidence
range is a reflection of geographic heterogeneity. [1] Nevertheless, this translates to a worldwide volume in excess of 36 000
new penile cancer diagnoses each year. [2] Furthermore, countries with a traditionally low incidence of penile neoplasia,
including the United States, the United Kingdom, and Norway,
have reported steadily increasing incidence rates. [3-5]

Radicality was the cornerstone of traditional surgical management of penile cancer, with a 2-cm resection margin originally thought necessary. [6][,][7] Recent decades, however, have
seen this principle challenged. Subsequent research has confirmed that a far more conservative surgical approach, with
excision margins in the range of a few millimeters, is acceptable. [3][,][8] Although a higher local recurrence rate is observed
with more conservative treatments, the overall and metastasisfree survival rates are comparable to those of patients treated
with penile amputation. [9-11] These findings, combined with
the propensity of penile cancer to arise distally, on the prepuce
or glans penis, [12] paved the way for surgical treatments centered on phallus preservation. [13][,][14] Accordingly,organ-sparing


surgery (OSS) for penile cancer has evolved. OSS is now
recommended as an aim of penile cancer treatment, where
feasible, in major urological guidelines. [15][,][16]

Initial research on organ preservation in penile cancer
management naturally focused on oncological outcomes. [17][,][18]

However, there is growing awareness of the myriad of relevant
nononcological endpoints that merit understanding and
consideration following penile cancer surgery. We aimed
to conduct a systematic review of penile cosmetic and
functional outcomes, health-related quality of life (HRQoL),
and psychological well-being in patients surgically treated for
primary penile malignancies.

and “Quality of life.” A systematic review was conducted

**Eligibility criteria**
***Inclusion criteria and rationale***

Case series and cohort studies, with and without comparative
groups, and randomized controlled trials were eligible for
inclusion. The included publication time frame was 2000 to
2022; given the magnitude of evolution in the field of penile
cancer management over recent decades, studies conducted
prior to this were felt to be at risk of incorporating outdated
treatment strategies and thus to be incomparable to the recent
literature. Studies presenting outcomes of radical penectomy
or OSS, with or without inguinal lymph node dissection, in the
management of primary penile cancer were eligible. Included
studies reported on functional, aesthetic, psychological, or
global health–related outcomes. To be eligible for inclusion,
we required either application of an instrument, such as
a questionnaire, or formal interviews with qualitative data
interpretation to have been used by the authors for evaluation
of at least 1 of the previously mentioned domains. We sought
the use of standardized, validated instruments for assessment
of outcomes but accepted the use of novel or customized
tools in addition, so as to avoid the exclusion of understudied areas such as sensory function and cosmesis. However, studies reporting generic statements only, such as “all
patients were satisfied with [outcome of interest],” were not
included.

***Exclusion criteria***

Case reports and conference abstracts were excluded. Reports
of nonprimary penile tumors (ie, metastatic deposits) and
cutaneous malignancy of the penile shaft skin (eg, extramammary Paget’s disease) were excluded, given the difference in
treatment algorithms. Studies reporting on oncological outcomes alone, without measurement of the specific variables
of interest to this review, were not eligible for inclusion.
Investigations of radiotherapy, brachytherapy, laser treatment,
topical applications, or other treatment strategies for invasive
or noninvasive penile malignancy were excluded, as were
studies focusing on management of metastatic or extensive
locally advanced disease.

**Data extraction**

Template-based data extraction was performed based on predefined variables including author and year of publication,
patient demographics, treatment strategy employed, outcome
measurement tools, results of outcome measurement, and
follow-up duration. The majority of studies did not report
outcomes across all domains of interest, as expected from a
provisional scoping review of the literature. All relevant data
were extracted from each study.


**Outcome measures**

Our primary outcomes were evaluated by subjective or objective measures, including patient-reported outcome measures
and/or clinical assessment tools, in the following domains:
global (overall quality of life), functional (urinary, sexual, or
genital sensory function), aesthetic (genital appearance and
cosmetic satisfaction), and psychological and interpersonal
(self-esteem and psychosocial and relational wellness).

**Data reporting and statistical analysis**
Data are presented as reported by the study authors. Metaanalysis was not performed, due to heterogeneity of study
variables.

**Results**

The search strategy resulted in 26 publications that were ultimately deemed relevant for inclusion (Figure 1 and Table 1).
Diverse surgical cohorts were studied, with both inter- and
intrastudy heterogeneity of patient populations.

**Surgical techniques**
Surgical techniques (Tables 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7) [11][,][20][,][21] included
the following:

 - Radical penectomy: This term generally refers to complete
amputation of the penis, and may occasionally be followed
by phalloplasty involving, for example, free radial forearm
flap reconstruction with or without penile prosthesis.

 - Partial penectomy: We view this procedure as encompassing removal of the glans penis with a subcoronal incision,
excising of a portion of the penile shaft, resulting in a
remnant penile stump. Reconstructive methods include
primary closure, split-thickness skin grafting, and urethral
flap reconstruction, in which the distal urethra is preserved and inverted to cover the exposed remnant of the
corporal bodies.

 - Radical glansectomy: This technique involves excision of
the entire glans penis with an incision along the coronal
sulcus. A reconstructive technique generally follows this
organ-sparing procedure, with use of split-thickness skin
grafting or the redundant urethra (urethral glansplasty) to
cover the exposed corporal bodies.

 - Partial glansectomy: This refers to excision of a significant
component of the glans penis but not the entirety. It may
be followed by primary closure, skin graft application, or
preputial flap reconstruction, in which, in an uncircumcised male, the outer layer of the prepuce can be used to
reconstruct the defect.

 - Wide local excision (WLE) and primary closure: This
refers to excision of a lesion—generally a small lesion
on the glans penis—with primary closure. There may be
some overlap of this term with partial glansectomy. For
the purpose of this review, we classified procedures as
described by the authors.

 - Total glans resurfacing: This is a potential treatment
option for noninvasive tumors of the glans, particularly
when diffuse, and involves surgical excision of the
glandular epithelium and subepithelium, followed by
application of a split-thickness skin graft to cover the
entire glans penis.

Sexual Medicine Reviews, 2023, Vol 11, Issue 4 **451**

***Sexual function***
Twenty studies, comprising a pooled total of 805 patient
respondents, involved assessment of patients’ sexual function
following penile cancer surgery (Table 2). The most frequently
applied instrument was the International Index of Erectile
Function (IIEF); the original 15-item IIEF questionnaire (IIEF15) [22] was applied in 10 studies, [23-33] the abridged 5-item
version (IIEF-5) [34] in 8, [35-41] and a modified IIEF-15, in which
“intercourse” was replaced with “sexual activity” in 1. [42]

The Overall Sexual Functioning Questionnaire and the Erection Hardness Score were used in 1 study, [43] the Quality
of Erection Questionnaire in 1, [28] the Erectile Dysfunction
Inventory of Treatment Satisfaction in 2, [28][,][31] and the Sexual
Encounter Profile (SEP) [30] in 1. In addition, patients in one
study participated in semi-structured interviews. [27] Objective
measurements were sought, in addition to the IIEF-15, in one
study, in which patients underwent RigiScan assessment with
audiovisual stimulation. [32] A standardized assessment of sexual function was made both before and after the penile cancer
surgery in 8 studies. [24][,][26][,][28][,][30-33][,][37] One study recruited only
patients with satisfactory erectile function at baseline, [36] and
2 studies attempted to quantify preoperative status by retrospective recall. [23][,][27] The average interval between surgery and
assessment of sexual function ranged from 3 to 54.6 months.
The findings of each individual study regarding sexual
function are summarized in Table 2. When sexual outcomes
are reported for specific surgical populations, the results are
summarized subsequently.

**WLE and/or radical circumcision**
Li et al [26] reported on 29 patients who had undergone WLE
and/or radical circumcision at mean 26.5 (range 2-61) months
follow-up. Twenty-two patients reported no erectile dysfunction or mild erectile dysfunction on the IIEF-15 preoperatively,
and 7 reported moderate or severe erectile dysfunction ED.
The same ratings were reported postoperatively in 28 of
29, and a decrease in IIEF-15 was reported in 1 of 29. [26]

Sedigh et al [30] reported a loss of 0.7 to 1.5 points in the
mean scores of each domain of the IIEF-15 when assessing
sexual function in 12 patients prior to, and at 12 months
after, WLE. The greatest difference was seen in overall satisfaction (mean scores of 6 [range 5-9] preoperatively and 4.5

[range 2-6] postoperatively). The authors also applied the SEP
and found this to remain stable pre- and postoperatively. [30]

Conversely, Wan et al [31] reported significant improvements in
all domains of the IIEF-15 of >2 points in 7 patients who
underwent WLE, comparing scores at 4 weeks preoperatively
and 6 months postoperatively, but reported no statistically
significant difference in Erectile Dysfunction Inventory of
Treatment Satisfaction scores. [31]

**Total glans resurfacing**
O’Kelly et al [37] reported on 14 patients who were sexually
active prior to total glans resurfacing. All resumed sexual
activity within 6 months. The mean IIEF-5 score preoperatively was 21 preoperatively and 22 postoperatively ( *P* = 1.0).
The majority of patients (81%) reported an improvement
in their sex life following glans resurfacing. [37] Perez et al [38]

studied a patient cohort at mean 55.7 months (range 2149 months) post–penile cancer surgery. Fourteen patients
underwent glans resurfacing, with a mean IIEF-5 score of 17.5
at follow-up. [38]

Sexual Medicine Reviews, 2023, Vol 11, Issue 4 **455**

**Partial glansectomy**
Croghan et al [35] reported on 15 patients following partial
glansectomy (11 with preputial flap reconstruction), of whom
9 were sexually active at the time of follow-up, with mean
IIEF-5 scores of 14.9 (range 5-25). A mean reduction in
overall satisfaction with sexual function of 2.8 was reported
compared with premorbid condition (based on retrospective
recall), although statistical significance was not confirmed
( *P =* .07). Yang et al [32] looked at 142 patients following
partial glansectomy with either primary closure (n = 59) or
preputial flap reconstruction (n = 46) at 6 months using IIEF15. The authors reported small mean improvements (approximately 0.5- to 1.5-point improvement in the mean score
of each domain) from preoperative scoring on the IIEF-15.
They found that patients with preputial flap reconstruction
scored better than patients with primary closure in orgasmic
function, intercourse satisfaction, and overall satisfaction ( *P*
< .05). In addition, sexual partners were more likely to report
satisfaction with appearance with preputial flap ( *P =* .011),
but no significant difference in partners’ perception of acceptability for intercourse was noted. RigiScan measurements did
not show a significant difference from preoperative scores. [32]

**Radical glansectomy and reconstruction**
Sedigh et al [30] described the sexual outcomes of 23 patients
following glansectomy with urethral glanduloplasty at
12 month follow-up. Mean score pre- and postoperatively
in each domain were as follows: erectile function (19
preoperatively, 15.7 postoperatively [scale 1-30]), orgasm (6
preoperatively, 4.8 postoperatively [scale 0-10]), libido (7.2

***Health-related quality of life***
Thirteen studies assessed HRQoL in patients following penile
cancer surgery (Table 6). A variety of instruments were used,
most commonly the European Organizations for Research
and the Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) Quality of Life Questionnaire C30. [45] Generally, premorbid measures were absent.
Gulino et al [23][,][24] did attempt to compare predisease and postoperative scores, showing small declines across all BigelowYoung domains, without statistical significance, following
radical glansectomy or partial penectomy and urethral gladuloplasty. Other authors have attempted to compare HRQoL in
penile cancer surgery patients to that of other cohorts. Kieffer
et al [25] reported similar 36-item Short-Form Survey scores
between penile cancer surgery patients (partial penectomy
or other OSS, with or without lymphadenectomy) and the
age-matched general Dutch population. Conversely, however,
Ficarra et al [46] reported a median global health status domain
(EORTC C30) of 50 in patients following partial penectomy,
compared with literature reported scores of 66.7 in a population with a cancer diagnosis and 75 in a general population.
The same authors compared partial penectomy patients to a
cohort post–radical retropubic prostatectomy and found the
penile cancer patients to have significantly impaired overall
health and increased anxiety. [46] Conflicting findings are also
reported when the impact of surgical technique on HRQoL is
examined. Several authors reported no significant difference
in HRQoL scores between patients who underwent radical


penectomy, partial penectomy, or more conservative OSS, with
or without lymphadenectomy, [25][,][31][,][38][,][39] while Sosnowski et
al [47] identified a correlation between the aggressiveness of the
penile cancer surgery and the global health status domain of
the EORTC C30.

**Discussion**

Penile cancer is an aggressive genitourinary malignancy that
often equates to a life-altering diagnosis for a patient. Recent
years have seen increased awareness of the concept of survivorship in cancer patients, and this must become a greater
focus of penile cancer care and research. [49-51]

OSS for penile cancer, when oncologically feasible, has
evolved on the basis that some preservation of organ function
may be possible, and that the psychological morbidity of
radical penectomy may be avoided or reduced. [8][,][15][,][16][,][18] The
former concept is confirmed by this review, while our findings
highlight that psychological aspects of the disease and its
treatment remain poorly understood overall.
A number of challenges are posed to both interpretation of
the current literature and future study design. Penile cancer
is a rare malignancy, [3][,][6] and little research has been published from the geographical regions of highest prevalence.
Accordingly, most studies are based on low patient numbers,
and many incorporate heterogeneous populations, with some
outcome results pooled across different surgical techniques.
Lymph node management is variably reported. While some
authors have stratified outcome according to lymph node
management, [41] the majority did not. This could conceivably have a significant confounding influence on all studied
outcomes, particularly in instances in which radical inguinal
± pelvic lymphadenectomy has been performed. Similarly,
histopathological stage and grade are not routinely reported
in a way that allows correlation with nononcological outcomes, although this would be an interesting point of study in
future research. Furthermore, a lack of standardized reporting, with heterogeneity of outcome measures, significantly
limits interstudy comparison or data pooling. Many current
studies are retrospective in design and fail to capture preoperative baseline data for control purposes. However, even
prospectively designed studies are unavoidably limited, in that
patients generally present to the urologist in the disease state,
with a penile tumor and recent/impending cancer diagnosis

exerting potential influences on aesthetic, functional, and
global well-being. True premorbid baseline measures are desirable but realistically are not possible to capture in the majority
of patients without the hazards of retrospective recall and
associated bias. The impact of surgical technique is difficult to
evaluate as an independent variable given the aforementioned
challenges and that a randomized study is unlikely to be
feasible or ethical.
Currently available tools are often likely inadequate as
stand-alone measures for exploration of each functional
domain in penile cancer patients. Research of postprostatectomy patients has stressed that restoration of erectile function
alone, as measured by the IIEF, does not necessarily equate to
satisfactory overall sexuality for a patient. [52] Questionnaires
assessing urinary tract symptoms tend to be designed for
patients with outflow obstruction or storage symptoms
but may not adequately explore specific urinary symptoms
associated with penile cancer surgery, such as spraying or
difficulty voiding from a standing position due to penile
stump length. [35] Evaluation of quality of life and psychological
well-being are particularly challenging, as they are influenced
by multiple confounding variables, and premorbid baseline
recordings are usually unavailable. The relationship between
these variables and other domains also merits more in-depth
exploration. A cancer diagnosis itself may impart significant
psychological morbidity, including posttraumatic stress
disorder. [53] However, changes in other domains covered in
this review are also likely to influence QoL and psychological
well-being; for example, sexual dysfunction has been directly
associated with psychological distress in male survivors of
pelvic malignancies. [54] Furthermore, we need to be assured
of the sensitivity of measurement instruments to detect small
changes. Current quantitative studies reporting on QoL and
psychological and relational well-being are likely significantly
underpowered to determine differences in these outcomes. For
this reason, we feel that studies reporting, for example, that
no relationship exists between the radicality of surgery and
QoL, should be interpreted with caution.
Our current understanding of penile cancer survivorship
issues would be greatly enhanced by a larger volume of qualitative data exploring patients’ perceptions of the outcomes
discussed in this review, and indeed any others that may
emerge as important to patients. We would hope that such
research would pave the way for a standardized penile cancer–specific patient-reported outcome measure incorporating
functional, aesthetic, and global health–related domains that
could be applied preoperatively and at intervals along a
patient’s treatment and recovery pathway. This would greatly
facilitate assessment of outcomes from potential interventions
in this field, for example biopsychosocial rehabilitation programs such as those reported in prostate cancer survivors. [55]

**Conclusion**

A growing body of literature explores functional, aesthetic,
and global well-being outcomes of penile cancer surgery. This
confirms that OSS can preserve elements of sexual, urinary,
and penile sensory function, offering potential advantages,
compared with radical penectomy, to eligible patients. Further
benefits to OSS may be reflected in improved quality of
life, psychological health, and relational well-being, although
conflicting results have been reported. Overall, current
understanding is challenged by limited data, small,
heterogeneous patient cohorts, a lack of premorbid baseline


measurements,confounding variables and wide variability in
outcome measures used. Future developments should incorporate further qualitative data to deepen our understanding
of patients’ perspectives, priorities, and values. This ultimately
result in the design of standardized patient-reported outcome
measures (PROMs) and their routine implementation both
before and after penile cancer surgery.
