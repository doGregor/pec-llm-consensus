1. Introduction


A B S T R A C T

This meta-analysis aimed to explore penile cancer survival based on p53 and Ki-67 status. A systematic literature
search identified studies assessing overall and cancer-specific survival after penile cancer with p53 or Ki-67
expression. Pooled hazard ratios (HRs) and restricted mean survival time (RMST) differences were calculated
using a random-effects model. The analysis included 930 and 391 men for p53 expression in overall survival (OS)
and cancer-specific survival (CSS), respectively. Those with the p53 mutant pattern exhibited significantly worse
OS and CSS compared to the p53 wild-type pattern (HR OS ¼2.42, 95% CI 1.75–3.34; HR CSS ¼4.18, 95% CI
1.87–9.35). The 5-year RMST difference for OS, according to p53 status, was –10.62 months (95% CI –16.20 to
–5.03). We included 202 men with penile cancer tested for Ki-67 expression and found an HR of CSS of 1.96 (95%
CI 1.15–3.32). In conclusion, men with the p53 mutant pattern penile cancer have worse OS and CSS than men
with p53 wild-type pattern penile cancer. Evidence regarding Ki-67 status was sparse, but the pooled estimate
indicated that penile cancers with high Ki-67 expression may have a slightly worse CSS than those with low Ki-67
expression. These findings may inform clinicians when planning the best management and follow-up strategy for
penile cancer patients.


Penile cancer is a rare malignancy, with approximately 36,000 new
cases diagnosed and 13,000 deaths globally in 2020. [1] The incidence
varies by geographical region, with a higher incidence in less developed
countries. [2] Most penile cancers are squamous cell carcinomas (SCC), and

the mean age at diagnosis is around 60 68 years. [3][–][5] Penile carcinogenesis consists of two major aetiological pathways, one involving oncogenic
human papillomavirus (HPV), and the other associated with non-viral
mechanisms, including inflammatory conditions, smoking and lack of
circumcision. [6][–][8]

In studies of anogenital and oropharyngeal cancer, it has been found
that tumours positive for HPV have superior survival compared to HPV
negative. [9][–][12] In addition, we have previously found that the detection of
p16 expression by immunohistochemical (IHC) staining is a marker of
prognostic value in patients with penile, vulva, vaginal and oropharyngeal cancer. [9][,][11][–][13] In addition to p16, some other IHC markers seem to be
of interest in the prediction of survival after penile cancer, including p53


and Ki-67 expression. Both p53 and Ki-67 are cell cycle-associated proteins. Ki-67 is a marker of cell proliferation and predicts malignant
behaviour in some cancer types. [14][–][17] The tumour suppressor protein p53
is often mutated in cancers, leading to an accumulation of the protein in
malignant cells. In some anogenital SCC, it has been found that HPV E6
protein inactivates p53, causing downregulation of expression. [18] Because
of small study populations, existing studies examining survival after
penile cancer according to p53 and Ki-67 expression status are often
inconclusive. Identifying prognostic biomarkers in penile cancer, as in
other cancers, would allow for better patient stratification based on the
risk of cancer progression, ultimately guiding treatment decisions.
The aim of this study was to examine the association of p53 and Ki-67
expression status with overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival
(CSS) in men diagnosed with penile cancer. In this systematic review and
meta-analysis, we conducted a comprehensive literature search using
multiple electronic databases to identify relevant studies to estimate OS,
CSS, and restricted mean survival time (RMST) difference for men with

2. Material and methods

We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews
and Meta Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, [19] and the study protocol was
registered at PROSPERO (CRD42022373058).

2.1. Search strategy and selection criteria

A systematic literature search of the electronic databases PubMed,
Embase and Cochrane was conducted using a combination of search terms
for penile cancer, p53, Ki-67 and survival. We used text words and Medical
Subject Headings (PubMed, Cochrane Library) or Emtree headings
(EMBASE); detailed search terms are provided in Supplementary Table 1.
Studies published between 1 January 2000 and 17 November 2023 were
included. The search was restricted to English-language publications, and
only peer-reviewed studies were included. The reference lists of the
included studies were reviewed to identify other relevant studies. All titles, abstracts and full texts were reviewed independently by two authors
(FLS and SL), and any inconsistencies in the selection process were discussed with a third reviewer (SKK) until a consensus was reached.
Studies were eligible for inclusion if they examined OS or CSS after
penile cancer, comparing p53 mutant pattern or high Ki-67 expression
with p53 wild-type pattern or low Ki-67 expression, respectively. Studies
using IHC staining to detect p53 and/or Ki-67 expression from histologically verified samples of penile cancer were eligible for inclusion. In
case of overlapping study populations, the study with the most comprehensive data was included.

2.2. Definition of p53 and Ki-67 status

Evaluation of p53 and Ki-67 status was based on IHC staining. As
molecular TP53 status and IHC staining have shown close accordance,
strong nuclear positivity was defined as a p53 mutant pattern, and
heterogenous nuclear staining was defined as a p53 wild-type
pattern. [20][,][21] Most studies defined p53-null tumours as mutant/positive,
though earlier studies often excluded null tumours from mutant cases due
to evolving definitions. Complete p53 negativity in tumour cells is now
widely recognised as mutant status, but in some studies, tumours were
only classified as mutant if a defined percentage of cells showed staining,
thereby excluding null tumours. Ki-67 status was defined as either low or
high expression based on the individual cut-off limits reported in the

papers.

2.3. Data extraction

Two authors (FLS and SL) independently extracted the data, and inconsistencies were discussed with a third author (SKK) until a consensus
was reached. We extracted the following variables from each study: first
author, year of publication, year of sample collection, country, continent,
age (mean, median, range), follow-up time, histology of the tumour,
tumour stage and grade, treatment modality, tissue type, definition of
p53 mutant and wild-type pattern and/or high and low Ki-67 expression,
number of reviewers of IHC staining, sample size, number of p53 cases
with wild-type/mutant pattern and/or low/high Ki-67 expression and
survival endpoints.

2.4. Quality assessment

Based on guidelines from Reporting Recommendations for Tumor
Marker Prognostic Studies (REMARK), we evaluated the quality of the
included studies. [22][,][23] The studies were assessed by two authors (FLS and
SL) independently, and in case of inconsistencies, the studies were discussed with a third author (SKK). The studies were evaluated based on


predefined criteria (Supplementary Table 2) and scored between zero
and seven, with a high score indicating a high study quality.

2.5. Statistical analysis

We used a random effects model to estimate the pooled hazard ratios
(HRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of OS and CSS
comparing penile cancer between cases exhibiting p53 mutant expression and p53 wild-type expression patterns. In the same way, we examined the CSS according to Ki-67 status. However, too few studies were
available to calculate the pooled HR of OS for Ki-67 status.
In studies where Kaplan–Meier curves were available, individual
patient data were reconstructed using the method described by Liu
et al. [24] Based on the individual patient data, we estimated the HR and
RMST difference. The HR was estimated by applying a Cox proportional
hazard model, applying fixed-effects models for pooling. We estimated
the 5-year HR for p53 studies and the 3-year HR for Ki-67 studies. In
studies where the follow-up period was shorter than 5 or 3 years,
respectively, the HR was estimated based on the available follow-up
time. Supplementary Table 3 presents an overview of the estimates
extracted from the individual studies. The proportional hazards
assumption was tested based on weighted Schoenfeld residuals, and the
assumption was met. In studies where Kaplan–Meier curves were unavailable, the study-specific 5-year HRs were estimated using a previously described method by Moodie et al. [25] This method allowed us to
perform meta-analysis, including studies with different lengths of
follow-up time. The results from the pooled analyses are presented
graphically with forest plots, including confidence intervals.
The RMST is an alternative method for comparison across groups.
Individual patient data from studies presenting Kaplan–Meier curves
were used to calculate RMST. [24] For p53 expression status, we estimated
the overall RMST difference after 3 and 5 years of follow-up and the
cancer-specific RMST difference after 5 years of follow-up. For Ki-67
expression status, the cancer-specific RMST difference after 2 years of
follow-up was estimated.
Statistical heterogeneity across the studies was evaluated by the I [2]

statistic, which describes the percentage of variation between studies. [26]

A value of 0% indicates no observed heterogeneity, whereas values of 25,
50, and 75% correspond to low, moderate and high levels of heterogeneity, respectively. In addition, the significance of the heterogeneity
across the studies was determined using the Cochran Q test, with a p
value <0.05 considered statistically significant. [27] Publication bias was
assessed using funnel plots and the Egger regression test.
All analyses were conducted using the statistical software R version
4.2.1, [28] with the packages IPDfromKM, [29] SurvRM2 [30] and meta. [31]

factor for CSS (p¼0.323). [32] The funnel plot showed no strong publication
bias (Supplementary Table 5c).

with high Ki-67 expression compared with low Ki-67 expression. Based

4. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis examining survival
after penile cancer according to p53 and Ki-67 expression status. We
found that men with penile cancer with a p53 mutant pattern had
significantly worse OS and CSS compared with p53 wild-type tumours. In
addition, studies adjusting for other prognostic factors, including stage,
grade and lympho-vascular invasion, concluded that p53 status could be
an independent prognostic factor for survival after penile cancer. We
found that 5 years after the penile cancer diagnosis, men with a p53 wildtype pattern, on average, had lived approximately 11 months longer than
men with a p53 mutant pattern. In contrast, the included studies reported
no association between Ki-67 status and OS but seem to suggest that men
with penile cancers with high Ki-67 expression might have a slightly
worse CSS compared with cancers with low Ki-67 expression. However,
this result was based on relatively few studies.
The p53 expression status as a prognosticator for survival has been
suggested in other anogenital and head and neck cancers. [9][,][13][,][55][,][56] We
have previously reported that women with p53 wild-type vulvar tumours
have superior survival compared with p53 mutant tumours. [13] Studies
have also suggested that p53 expression has prognostic value in vaginal


cancer. [9] The possible prognostic value of Ki-67 expression status in malignancies differs between cancer sites. [57][,][58] Ki-67 expression has been
shown to be a reliable marker for survival in patients with e.g. breast,
tongue, cervical, prostate, renal and neuroendocrine cancer. [14][,][15][,][17][,][59][–][62]

Our results indicated that Ki-67 expression status might be associated with
CSS in men diagnosed with penile cancer. However, the results were
limited by few and smaller studies, and therefore, larger studies are still
warranted.

A recent review examined p53 and Ki-67 status in predicting lymph
node metastasis and CSS in men diagnosed with penile cancer and
concluded that p53 expression has prognostic value for CSS, but more
studies are needed for Ki-67 status. [63] The present study is the first
meta-analysis to calculate a pooled estimate for OS and CSS for penile
cancers with a p53 mutant pattern compared with p53 wild-type pattern
cancers, and CSS for high Ki-67 expression compared with low Ki-67
expression. In addition, we also examined RMST differences according
to p53 and Ki-67 expression status detected by IHC staining. It is a
strength of the study that two authors independently assessed titles,
abstracts and full texts and extracted the data after conducting a thorough literature search of relevant studies in several electronic databases.
The studies examining OS or CSS survival after penile cancer according to
p53 were generally limited by small study population. However, by
combining the studies, we were able to include 930 and 391 men with
penile cancer tested for p53 expression and examined OS and CSS after
cancer diagnosis, respectively. In addition, by extracting individual pa
–
tient data from Kaplan Meier curves, we performed a standard survival
analysis and were able to get estimates that were based on more information and thereby considered more accurate than estimates obtained
using the method described by Moodie et al. [25]

Some limitations should also be considered. Due to the available data,
we were not able to take into account other prognostic factors such as the
stage of the tumours in the analyses. Therefore, it remains uncertain to
what extent p53 status alone influences the survival after penile cancer.
The definition of p53 mutant/wild-type expression pattern and high/low
Ki-67 expression varied between studies, which we were unable to
consider in the analyses. The definition of p53 mutant is currently more
often characterised as strong nuclear staining or total absence of nuclear
expression, while the definition of wild-type is heterogeneous nuclear
staining. [21] We did a sensitivity analysis using only four of the most recent
studies that used a similar criterion for p53 status and found a similar
result. Finally, the survival analysis according to Ki-67 status was based
on only a few studies.
Survival according to p53 and Ki-67 expression status is of clinical
interest, as testing for p53 expression in penile tumour tissue could help
in planning the management and follow-up strategy. Our results indicate
that men with penile cancers with p53 mutant patterns might need to be
followed more closely compared with men with a p53 wild-type pattern.
It has been suggested that combined testing for multiple markers,
including HPV, p16 and p53, increases the accuracy in predicting patient
survival compared with individual markers alone. In head and neck
cancer, the tumour is tested for a group of markers that influence the
treatment regime and follow-up program offered to the patients. [56]

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, in this systematic review and meta-analysis, we found
that p53 status in penile cancer had a significant prognostic value for
both OS and CSS. This was also apparent in studies taking into account
known prognostic markers. Few studies with smaller study populations
limited the interpretation of the results for Ki-67. However, it indicated
that men with high Ki-67 expression might have a worse CSS compared
to those with low Ki-67 expression. These findings suggest that testing for
p53 and possibly Ki-67 expression may be useful when planning the
management and follow-up strategy of men with penile cancer.
